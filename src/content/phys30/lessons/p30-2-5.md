An old television set, an electron microscope and a hospital X-ray tube all begin the same way: electrons are let go near a negative plate and pulled towards a positive one, gaining speed all the way. Between parallel plates the field is uniform, so the force on a charge is constant, and everything you know about motion with constant acceleration applies. The only surprise is the size of the numbers.

## Charges moving in a uniform field

A particle of charge $q$ in a field $E$ feels a force $F = qE$. By Newton's second law its acceleration is

$$a = \dfrac{F}{m} = \dfrac{qE}{m}$$

A positive particle accelerates along the field, and a negative one against it. Electrons and protons have so little mass that their accelerations are enormous, and gravity can be ignored beside the electric force.

:::example The acceleration of a proton
A proton has a charge of $1.60 \times 10^{-19}$ C and a mass of $1.67 \times 10^{-27}$ kg. Find its acceleration in a uniform field of 2000 N/C.
@step $F = qE = 1.60 \times 10^{-19} \times 2000 = 3.2 \times 10^{-16}$ N.
@step $a = \dfrac{F}{m} = \dfrac{3.2 \times 10^{-16}}{1.67 \times 10^{-27}}$.
@answer $a = 1.92 \times 10^{11}$ m/s², in the direction of the field.
:::

### Speed from a potential difference

To find the speed a particle gains, energy is quicker than kinematics. A particle that starts from rest and moves through a potential difference $\Delta V$ loses electric potential energy $q\Delta V$, and all of it becomes kinetic energy:

$$q\Delta V = \dfrac{1}{2}mv^2 \qquad \text{so} \qquad v = \sqrt{\dfrac{2q\Delta V}{m}}$$

The distance between the plates does not appear. Only the potential difference matters.

:::example The speed of an electron
An electron has a charge of size $1.60 \times 10^{-19}$ C and a mass of $9.11 \times 10^{-31}$ kg. It is accelerated from rest through 1000 V. How fast is it moving at the end?
@step $q\Delta V = \dfrac{1}{2}mv^2$, so $v = \sqrt{\dfrac{2q\Delta V}{m}}$.
@step $v = \sqrt{\dfrac{2 \times 1.60 \times 10^{-19} \times 1000}{9.11 \times 10^{-31}}} = \sqrt{3.51 \times 10^{14}}$.
@answer $v = 1.87 \times 10^{7}$ m/s, about 6% of the speed of light.
:::

### A charge that enters across the field

Fire an electron horizontally between two horizontal plates and it behaves like a ball thrown horizontally. Along the plates there is no force, so its horizontal speed does not change. Across the plates it has a constant acceleration, so it follows a parabola.

:::example The deflection of an electron
An electron moving horizontally at $2.0 \times 10^{7}$ m/s enters the space between two horizontal plates that are 6.0 cm long. The field between the plates is 4000 N/C, straight down. How far is the electron deflected by the time it leaves?
@step Horizontally, the speed is constant: $t = \dfrac{0.060}{2.0 \times 10^{7}} = 3.0 \times 10^{-9}$ s.
@step Vertically: $a = \dfrac{qE}{m} = \dfrac{1.60 \times 10^{-19} \times 4000}{9.11 \times 10^{-31}} = 7.03 \times 10^{14}$ m/s². The electron is negative, so it accelerates upward, against the field.
@step It starts with no vertical velocity: $y = \dfrac{1}{2}at^2 = \dfrac{1}{2} \times 7.03 \times 10^{14} \times (3.0 \times 10^{-9})^2$.
@answer $y = 3.2 \times 10^{-3}$ m: the electron is deflected 3.2 mm upward.
:::

:::mistake Using the distance between the plates to find a speed
The speed gained from rest depends only on the potential difference: $q\Delta V = \dfrac{1}{2}mv^2$. Plates that are closer together give a stronger field and a larger acceleration, but for a shorter distance, and the final speed is the same.
:::

## Millikan's oil drops

In 1909 Robert Millikan measured the charge of the electron with drops of oil. He sprayed a fine mist of oil between two horizontal plates. Some drops picked up a few extra electrons as they left the sprayer. Watching one drop through a microscope, he adjusted the potential difference between the plates until the drop hung at rest.

A drop at rest has no net force. The electric force, upward, balances its weight:

$$qE = mg$$

Between plates a distance $d$ apart, $E = \dfrac{\Delta V}{d}$, so

$$q = \dfrac{mgd}{\Delta V}$$

:::example The charge of an oil drop
An oil drop of mass $6.53 \times 10^{-15}$ kg hangs at rest between two horizontal plates that are 2.0 cm apart, with a potential difference of 1600 V between them. Find the charge of the drop, and the number of excess electrons on it. Use $g = 9.81$ m/s².
@step $q = \dfrac{mgd}{\Delta V} = \dfrac{6.53 \times 10^{-15} \times 9.81 \times 0.020}{1600}$.
@step $q = 8.01 \times 10^{-19}$ C.
@step Divide by the elementary charge: $\dfrac{8.01 \times 10^{-19}}{1.60 \times 10^{-19}} = 5.0$.
@answer The charge has a size of $8.01 \times 10^{-19}$ C, so the drop carries 5 excess electrons.
:::

Millikan measured thousands of drops. Their charges were not spread over all possible values. Every one was a whole number of the same smallest charge: one, two, three or more times $1.60 \times 10^{-19}$ C, and never a value in between. This showed that charge is **quantized**, and it gave the size of the elementary charge.

:::example Holding a drop at rest
An oil drop of mass $3.3 \times 10^{-15}$ kg carries 4 excess electrons. The plates are 1.5 cm apart. What potential difference holds the drop at rest? Use $g = 9.81$ m/s².
@step $q = 4 \times 1.60 \times 10^{-19} = 6.4 \times 10^{-19}$ C.
@step For balance, $q\dfrac{\Delta V}{d} = mg$, so $\Delta V = \dfrac{mgd}{q}$.
@step $\Delta V = \dfrac{3.3 \times 10^{-15} \times 9.81 \times 0.015}{6.4 \times 10^{-19}}$.
@answer $\Delta V = 759$ V, with the upper plate positive so that the negative drop is pulled upward.
:::

The same reasoning works backwards on a set of measurements. If four drops have charges of 4.8, 8.0, 11.2 and 16.0, all in units of $10^{-19}$ C, the largest charge that divides into every one of them a whole number of times is $1.6 \times 10^{-19}$ C. That is the best estimate of the smallest charge from these four drops.

:::mistake Expecting any charge to be possible
A drop cannot have a charge of $2.4 \times 10^{-19}$ C, because that is one and a half elementary charges. Divide a charge by $1.60 \times 10^{-19}$ C: the answer must be a whole number.
:::

:::summary
- In a uniform field a charged particle has a constant acceleration, $a = \dfrac{qE}{m}$.
- A particle accelerated from rest through a potential difference gains kinetic energy: $q\Delta V = \dfrac{1}{2}mv^2$.
- A charge that enters across a uniform field keeps its speed along the plates and accelerates across them, so it follows a parabola.
- An oil drop hangs at rest when the electric force balances its weight: $qE = mg$, so $q = \dfrac{mgd}{\Delta V}$.
- Millikan found that every charge is a whole number of the elementary charge, $1.60 \times 10^{-19}$ C.
:::
