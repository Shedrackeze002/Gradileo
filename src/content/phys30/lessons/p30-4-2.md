Thomson's atom was a soft ball of positive charge with electrons dotted through it. In 1909 Ernest Rutherford set two of his team, Hans Geiger and Ernest Marsden, to test it by firing fast particles at a thin sheet of gold. Nearly all of the particles sailed through, as expected. A very few bounced back. Rutherford said later that it was as if a shell fired at a sheet of tissue paper had come back and hit the gunner. This lesson shows what that result meant, and how the energy ideas of Unit 2 give the size of what the particles had struck.

## The gold foil experiment

The particles were **alpha particles**, which some radioactive elements give out. An alpha particle is positive, with a charge of $+2e$, twice the size of the charge of an electron. It is about 7000 times heavier than an electron, and it leaves its source at about one twentieth of the speed of light.

A narrow beam of alpha particles was aimed at a gold foil only a few hundred atoms thick. The foil was made that thin so that an alpha particle would be scattered by one nucleus at most on its way through. Around the foil was a screen coated with zinc sulfide, which gives a tiny flash of light each time an alpha particle hits it. Geiger and Marsden sat in the dark and counted the flashes at each angle.

### What Thomson's model predicted

In Thomson's atom the positive charge is spread through the whole atom, so nowhere is the electric field strong enough to turn a fast, heavy alpha particle. The electrons are far too light to matter, since each has only about one seven-thousandth of the mass of an alpha particle. Every alpha particle should pass through with hardly any deflection.

### What was seen

- **Most** of the alpha particles passed straight through, with little or no deflection.
- **Some** were turned through small angles.
- **A few**, about 1 in 8000, were turned through more than 90°, and some of these came almost straight back.

### What Rutherford concluded

Only a very strong force can turn back a fast alpha particle, and only a very concentrated charge can give such a force. Rutherford proposed a new model.

:::definition Rutherford's model of the atom
All of the positive charge of an atom, and nearly all of its mass, is packed into a tiny, dense **nucleus** at the centre. The electrons move around the nucleus at a distance, held by its electric attraction. Most of the atom is empty space.
:::

Each observation has its place in the model.

| Observation | What it shows |
|---|---|
| Most alpha particles pass straight through | Most of an atom is empty space |
| A few are turned through large angles | The positive charge is concentrated in a small, massive nucleus, which repels them strongly |
| Only about 1 in 8000 is turned back | The nucleus is very small compared with the atom |

The force that turns an alpha particle aside is the **electric repulsion** between the positive alpha particle and the positive nucleus. An alpha particle that passes far from a nucleus is turned through a small angle, and one that passes closer is turned through a larger angle. A foil of a metal with fewer protons in each nucleus repels less strongly, so fewer alpha particles are turned through large angles.

A nucleus is between one ten-thousandth and one hundred-thousandth of the diameter of its atom. If an atom were the size of a football stadium, its nucleus would be about the size of a pea at the centre.

### What the model could not explain

Rutherford's atom had a serious fault. An electron that moves in a circle is accelerating, and an accelerating charge radiates electromagnetic waves. The electron should lose energy and spiral into the nucleus in a tiny fraction of a second, so atoms should not be stable at all. The model also could not explain why each element, when it is hot, gives out light of only certain wavelengths. The next lesson shows how Niels Bohr dealt with both problems.

## How close an alpha particle can get

Picture an alpha particle that heads straight for a nucleus. The repulsion slows it down, and its kinetic energy changes into electric potential energy. At one point it stops for an instant, and then it is pushed back the way it came. The distance from the centre of the nucleus at that point is the **distance of closest approach**. The nucleus must be smaller than this distance, so the experiment puts an upper limit on its size.

### The charges

A nucleus with $Z$ protons has a charge of $Ze$. A gold nucleus has 79 protons.

:::example The charge of a gold nucleus
Find the charge of a gold nucleus, which contains 79 protons. The elementary charge is $1.60 \times 10^{-19}$ C.
@step $Q = Ze = 79 \times 1.60 \times 10^{-19}$.
@answer $Q = 1.26 \times 10^{-17}$ C.
:::

### The energy

The energy of an alpha particle is usually given in **megaelectronvolts**: $1\ \text{MeV} = 1.60 \times 10^{-13}$ J.

:::example Megaelectronvolts to joules
An alpha particle has a kinetic energy of 5.0 MeV. Find its energy in joules.
@step $E_k = 5.0 \times 1.60 \times 10^{-13}$.
@answer $E_k = 8.0 \times 10^{-13}$ J.
:::

### The closest approach

Unit 2 gave the electric potential energy of two point charges a distance $r$ apart as $E_p = \dfrac{kq_1q_2}{r}$. Far from the nucleus the alpha particle has only kinetic energy. At the closest point it has only potential energy. Energy is conserved, so the two are equal.

:::definition The distance of closest approach
$$E_k = \dfrac{k\,(2e)(Ze)}{r} \quad \text{so} \quad r = \dfrac{k\,(2e)(Ze)}{E_k}$$

- $E_k$ is the kinetic energy of the alpha particle when it is far from the nucleus, in joules;
- $2e$ is the charge of the alpha particle and $Ze$ is the charge of the nucleus;
- $r$ is the distance of closest approach, from the centre of the nucleus, in metres;
- $k = 8.99 \times 10^{9}$ N·m²/C².
:::

:::example The closest approach to a gold nucleus
An alpha particle with a kinetic energy of 5.0 MeV heads straight for a gold nucleus, which has 79 protons. How close does it get to the centre of the nucleus?
@step The charges: $2e = 3.20 \times 10^{-19}$ C and $79e = 1.264 \times 10^{-17}$ C. The energy: $E_k = 8.0 \times 10^{-13}$ J.
@step $r = \dfrac{k\,(2e)(Ze)}{E_k} = \dfrac{8.99 \times 10^{9} \times 3.20 \times 10^{-19} \times 1.264 \times 10^{-17}}{8.0 \times 10^{-13}}$.
@answer $r = 4.55 \times 10^{-14}$ m. An atom of gold is about $3 \times 10^{-10}$ m across, so the nucleus is thousands of times smaller than the atom.
:::

A faster alpha particle has more kinetic energy to change into potential energy, so it gets closer. Twice the kinetic energy gives half the distance.

:::example Starting from the speed
An alpha particle has a mass of $6.64 \times 10^{-27}$ kg and a speed of $1.60 \times 10^{7}$ m/s. It heads straight for a gold nucleus. Find its kinetic energy and its distance of closest approach.
@step $E_k = \dfrac{1}{2}mv^2 = \dfrac{1}{2} \times 6.64 \times 10^{-27} \times (1.60 \times 10^{7})^2 = 8.50 \times 10^{-13}$ J.
@step $r = \dfrac{8.99 \times 10^{9} \times 3.20 \times 10^{-19} \times 1.264 \times 10^{-17}}{8.50 \times 10^{-13}}$.
@answer $E_k = 8.50 \times 10^{-13}$ J and $r = 4.28 \times 10^{-14}$ m.
:::

:::example The energy needed to reach a given distance
How much kinetic energy must an alpha particle have, far from a silver nucleus, to come within $3.0 \times 10^{-14}$ m of its centre in a head-on approach? A silver nucleus contains 47 protons.
@step The charge of the nucleus: $47 \times 1.60 \times 10^{-19} = 7.52 \times 10^{-18}$ C.
@step $E_k = \dfrac{k\,(2e)(Ze)}{r} = \dfrac{8.99 \times 10^{9} \times 3.20 \times 10^{-19} \times 7.52 \times 10^{-18}}{3.0 \times 10^{-14}} = 7.21 \times 10^{-13}$ J.
@step In megaelectronvolts: $\dfrac{7.21 \times 10^{-13}}{1.60 \times 10^{-13}}$.
@answer $E_k = 7.21 \times 10^{-13}$ J, which is 4.5 MeV.
:::

### The force near a nucleus

Coulomb's law gives the force at any distance: $F = \dfrac{k\,(2e)(Ze)}{r^2}$.

:::example The force on an alpha particle
Find the electric force on an alpha particle when it is $5.0 \times 10^{-14}$ m from the centre of a gold nucleus.
@step $F = \dfrac{8.99 \times 10^{9} \times 3.20 \times 10^{-19} \times 1.264 \times 10^{-17}}{(5.0 \times 10^{-14})^2}$.
@answer $F = 14.5$ N. This is a force you could feel in your hand, and it acts on a single particle with a mass of less than $10^{-26}$ kg, which is why the alpha particle can be thrown straight back.
:::

### A scale model

:::example An atom built to scale
A nucleus is about $1 \times 10^{-14}$ m across and its atom is about $2 \times 10^{-10}$ m across. In a model, the nucleus is a marble 1.5 cm across. How wide is the atom in the model?
@step The atom is $\dfrac{2 \times 10^{-10}}{1 \times 10^{-14}} = 20\,000$ times as wide as the nucleus.
@step In the model: $20\,000 \times 1.5 = 30\,000$ cm.
@answer The atom would be 300 m across, with a marble at its centre and almost nothing else inside.
:::

:::summary
- Geiger and Marsden fired alpha particles at a thin gold foil and counted the flashes on a zinc sulfide screen.
- Most alpha particles passed straight through, so most of an atom is empty space.
- A few were turned through large angles, so the positive charge and nearly all of the mass are in a tiny, dense nucleus.
- The nucleus is between $\dfrac{1}{10\,000}$ and $\dfrac{1}{100\,000}$ of the diameter of the atom.
- In a head-on approach the kinetic energy changes into electric potential energy: $E_k = \dfrac{k\,(2e)(Ze)}{r}$.
- The force on the alpha particle at a distance $r$ is $F = \dfrac{k\,(2e)(Ze)}{r^2}$.
- Rutherford's model could not explain why the orbiting electrons do not spiral into the nucleus, or why elements give out line spectra.
:::
