A potential difference across a wire sets its free electrons drifting, and that drift of charge is an electric current. In Grade 10 you met current, resistance and Ohm's law, and you worked with resistors in series and in parallel. This lesson puts the two kinds of connection into one circuit, and then looks inside the battery itself, which turns out to have a resistance of its own.

## Series and parallel circuits

Three ideas from Grade 10 are all that a circuit problem needs.

- **Current** is the charge that passes a point each second, in amperes: one ampere is one coulomb per second.
- **Ohm's law** connects the potential difference across a resistor, the current in it and its resistance: $V = IR$.
- **Power** is the energy transferred each second, in watts: $P = VI$, which is also $P = I^2R$.

The two ways of connecting resistors behave differently.

| | In series | In parallel |
|---|---|---|
| Current | The same in every resistor | Shared between the branches |
| Potential difference | Shared between the resistors | The same across every branch |
| Total resistance | $R = R_1 + R_2 + \dots$ | $\dfrac{1}{R} = \dfrac{1}{R_1} + \dfrac{1}{R_2} + \dots$ |

Adding a resistor in series makes the total resistance larger. Adding one in parallel opens another path for the charge, so the total resistance becomes **smaller** than the smallest branch.

:::example Two resistors in parallel
A 6 Ω resistor and a 3 Ω resistor are connected in parallel to a 6 V battery. Find the total resistance of the pair, and the current from the battery.
@step $\dfrac{1}{R} = \dfrac{1}{6} + \dfrac{1}{3} = \dfrac{1}{6} + \dfrac{2}{6} = \dfrac{3}{6}$.
@step Turn the fraction over: $R = \dfrac{6}{3} = 2\ \Omega$.
@step $I = \dfrac{V}{R} = \dfrac{6}{2}$.
@answer The pair has a resistance of 2 Ω, and the battery supplies 3 A.
:::

:::example The total resistance and the battery current
A 12 V battery is connected to a 4 Ω resistor in series with a pair of resistors, of 6 Ω and 3 Ω, that are in parallel with each other. Find the total resistance of the circuit, and the current from the battery.
@step The parallel pair: $\dfrac{1}{R} = \dfrac{1}{6} + \dfrac{1}{3}$, so $R = 2\ \Omega$.
@step The pair is in series with the 4 Ω resistor: $4 + 2 = 6\ \Omega$.
@step $I = \dfrac{V}{R} = \dfrac{12}{6}$.
@answer The total resistance is 6 Ω, and the battery supplies 2 A.
:::

All of the battery current passes through the 4 Ω resistor. At the junction it divides between the two branches, and it joins again on the other side.

:::example Inside the parallel pair
In the same circuit, find the potential difference across the parallel pair, and the current in each of its resistors.
@step The pair behaves as 2 Ω, and carries the battery current: $V = IR = 2 \times 2 = 4$ V.
@step Each branch has that 4 V across it. In the 6 Ω resistor: $I = \dfrac{4}{6} = 0.67$ A.
@step In the 3 Ω resistor: $I = \dfrac{4}{3} = 1.33$ A.
@step Check: $0.67 + 1.33 = 2.00$ A, the battery current. The 4 Ω resistor has $2 \times 4 = 8$ V across it, and $8 + 4 = 12$ V.
@answer 4 V across the pair, with 0.67 A in the 6 Ω resistor and 1.33 A in the 3 Ω resistor. The smaller resistance takes the larger current.
:::

:::example Power in the circuit
In the same circuit, find the power of the 4 Ω resistor, and the total power that the battery supplies.
@step The 4 Ω resistor carries the whole battery current: $P = I^2R = 2^2 \times 4 = 16$ W.
@step The battery: $P = VI = 12 \times 2 = 24$ W.
@answer The 4 Ω resistor has a power of 16 W, out of a total of 24 W. The other 8 W is transferred in the parallel pair.
:::

:::mistake Adding resistors in parallel as if they were in series
A 6 Ω resistor and a 3 Ω resistor in parallel do not make 9 Ω. They make 2 Ω, which is less than either one. Also remember the last step: $\dfrac{1}{6} + \dfrac{1}{3} = \dfrac{1}{2}$ is $\dfrac{1}{R}$, so turn it over to get $R$.
:::

## Emf and internal resistance

Connect a voltmeter across a new 1.5 V cell and it reads 1.5 V. Now make the cell light a lamp, and the reading drops a little. Nothing is wrong with the cell. The charge has to pass through the cell as well as through the lamp, and the materials inside the cell resist the current too.

:::definition Emf and internal resistance
The **emf** of a battery, $\varepsilon$, is the energy it gives to each coulomb of charge that passes through it. It is measured in volts.

The **internal resistance**, $r$, is the resistance of the battery itself. It acts like a small resistor in series with the rest of the circuit.
:::

A battery of emf $\varepsilon$ connected to a resistor $R$ therefore drives its current through a total resistance of $R + r$:

$$\varepsilon = I(R + r)$$

Some of the emf is used up inside the battery. These **lost volts** are $Ir$. What is left is the potential difference across the terminals, which is what the outside circuit gets:

$$V = \varepsilon - Ir$$

When the battery supplies no current, there are no lost volts, and the terminal potential difference equals the emf. This is why a voltmeter alone across a battery reads its emf.

:::example The current and the terminal potential difference
A battery with an emf of 12 V and an internal resistance of 0.5 Ω is connected to a 5.5 Ω resistor. Find the current in the circuit, and the potential difference across the terminals of the battery.
@step $I = \dfrac{\varepsilon}{R + r} = \dfrac{12}{5.5 + 0.5} = \dfrac{12}{6}= 2$ A.
@step Lost volts: $Ir = 2 \times 0.5 = 1$ V.
@step $V = \varepsilon - Ir = 12 - 1 = 11$ V.
@answer The current is 2 A, and the terminal potential difference is 11 V. This is also the potential difference across the resistor: $IR = 2 \times 5.5 = 11$ V.
:::

The internal resistance of a battery can be found from two voltmeter readings: one with no current, and one with a known resistor connected.

:::example Finding the internal resistance
A voltmeter across the terminals of a battery reads 9.0 V when the battery supplies no current. When the battery is connected to a 7 Ω resistor, the voltmeter reads 8.4 V. Find the internal resistance of the battery.
@step With no current the voltmeter reads the emf: $\varepsilon = 9.0$ V.
@step The current in the resistor: $I = \dfrac{V}{R} = \dfrac{8.4}{7} = 1.2$ A.
@step Lost volts: $9.0 - 8.4 = 0.6$ V, and these are $Ir$.
@step $r = \dfrac{0.6}{1.2}$.
@answer $r = 0.5\ \Omega$.
:::

The energy transferred in the internal resistance heats the battery, and it is wasted. Its power is $I^2r$.

:::example The power wasted inside a battery
For the 12 V battery above, with its 0.5 Ω of internal resistance and the 5.5 Ω resistor, find the power delivered to the resistor and the power wasted inside the battery.
@step The current is 2 A.
@step The resistor: $P = I^2R = 2^2 \times 5.5 = 22$ W.
@step Inside the battery: $P = I^2r = 2^2 \times 0.5 = 2$ W.
@answer 22 W reaches the resistor, and 2 W is wasted. Together they make 24 W, which is $\varepsilon I = 12 \times 2$.
:::

A larger current means more lost volts. This is why the headlights of a car dim for a moment when the starter motor draws a very large current from the same battery.

:::mistake Thinking that the emf falls when the current rises
The emf of a battery stays the same. What falls is the **terminal** potential difference, $\varepsilon - Ir$, because a larger current loses more volts across the internal resistance.
:::

:::summary
- In series the current is the same and resistances add. In parallel the potential difference is the same and $\dfrac{1}{R} = \dfrac{1}{R_1} + \dfrac{1}{R_2}$.
- For a mixed circuit, replace each parallel group by one resistor, then use Ohm's law for the whole circuit.
- The potential difference across a parallel group is the battery current times the resistance of the group.
- Power: $P = VI = I^2R$.
- A battery has an emf $\varepsilon$ and an internal resistance $r$: $\varepsilon = I(R + r)$.
- The terminal potential difference is $V = \varepsilon - Ir$. It equals the emf only when there is no current.
:::
