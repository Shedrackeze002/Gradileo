The photoelectric effect showed that light delivers its energy in packets. A real particle has more than energy, though. A moving ball has momentum, and it can knock another ball aside. In 1923 Arthur Compton fired X-rays at electrons and found that a photon does exactly that. It bounces off an electron like one billiard ball off another, and the laws of Unit 1 describe the collision.

## The momentum of a photon

A photon has no mass, so $p = mv$ cannot give its momentum. Maxwell's theory of electromagnetic waves had already shown that light carries momentum as well as energy, and that the two are linked: the momentum of light of energy $E$ is $\dfrac{E}{c}$. For one photon, $E = \dfrac{hc}{\lambda}$, so the momentum is $\dfrac{h}{\lambda}$.

:::definition The momentum of a photon
$$p = \dfrac{h}{\lambda} = \dfrac{E}{c}$$

- $p$ is the momentum of the photon, in kg·m/s;
- $h$ is Planck's constant, $6.63 \times 10^{-34}$ J·s;
- $\lambda$ is the wavelength, and $E$ is the energy of the photon.
:::

The shorter the wavelength, the greater the momentum. A photon of X-rays has thousands of times the momentum of a photon of visible light. If the wavelength doubles, the momentum halves.

:::example The momentum of a photon of light
Find the momentum of a photon of green light with a wavelength of 500 nm.
@step $p = \dfrac{h}{\lambda} = \dfrac{6.63 \times 10^{-34}}{500 \times 10^{-9}}$.
@answer $p = 1.33 \times 10^{-27}$ kg·m/s.
:::

:::example The momentum of an X-ray photon
Find the momentum of a photon of X-rays with a wavelength of 71.0 pm. One picometre is $10^{-12}$ m.
@step $p = \dfrac{h}{\lambda} = \dfrac{6.63 \times 10^{-34}}{71.0 \times 10^{-12}}$.
@answer $p = 9.34 \times 10^{-24}$ kg·m/s, about 7000 times the momentum of the photon of green light.
:::

:::example Momentum from energy
A photon has an energy of 2.49 eV. Find its momentum.
@step In joules: $E = 2.49 \times 1.60 \times 10^{-19} = 3.98 \times 10^{-19}$ J.
@step $p = \dfrac{E}{c} = \dfrac{3.98 \times 10^{-19}}{3.00 \times 10^{8}}$.
@answer $p = 1.33 \times 10^{-27}$ kg·m/s. This is the 500 nm photon of the first example.
:::

### The push of light

Since photons carry momentum, light pushes on whatever it strikes. In Unit 1, force was the rate of change of momentum. A beam of power $P$ delivers an energy $P$ each second, and so a momentum $\dfrac{P}{c}$ each second.

- A black surface **absorbs** the light and takes all of its momentum, so the force is $F = \dfrac{P}{c}$.
- A mirror **reflects** the light straight back. The momentum of each photon is reversed, so its change is twice as large, and the force is $F = \dfrac{2P}{c}$.

This is the same reason that a ball that bounces gives a wall a larger impulse than a lump of clay that sticks.

:::example The force of a beam of light
A lamp sends a beam with a power of 100 W onto a black card, which absorbs all of it. Find the force of the light on the card. What would the force be on a perfect mirror?
@step Absorbed: $F = \dfrac{P}{c} = \dfrac{100}{3.00 \times 10^{8}} = 3.33 \times 10^{-7}$ N.
@step Reflected: $F = \dfrac{2P}{c}$, twice as much.
@answer The force is $3.33 \times 10^{-7}$ N on the card and $6.67 \times 10^{-7}$ N on the mirror. Both are far too small to feel.
:::

The force is tiny because $c$ is so large, but in space it adds up. A **solar sail** is a huge, light mirror that sunlight pushes for months on end, and the push of sunlight makes the dust tail of a comet point away from the Sun.

### Recoil

An atom that gives out a photon recoils, like a rifle that fires a bullet. The total momentum was zero before, so the atom and the photon leave with equal and opposite momentum.

:::example An atom that recoils
A hydrogen atom, with a mass of $1.67 \times 10^{-27}$ kg, is at rest when it gives out a photon of ultraviolet light with a wavelength of 122 nm. Find the speed at which the atom recoils.
@step The photon: $p = \dfrac{h}{\lambda} = \dfrac{6.63 \times 10^{-34}}{122 \times 10^{-9}} = 5.43 \times 10^{-27}$ kg·m/s.
@step The atom has momentum of the same size, in the opposite direction: $mv = 5.43 \times 10^{-27}$.
@step $v = \dfrac{5.43 \times 10^{-27}}{1.67 \times 10^{-27}}$.
@answer $v = 3.25$ m/s.
:::

:::mistake Giving a photon a mass
A photon has no mass, so do not use $p = mv$ for it. Use $p = \dfrac{h}{\lambda}$ or $p = \dfrac{E}{c}$.
:::

## The Compton effect

Compton sent a beam of X-rays of a single wavelength through a block of graphite, and measured the wavelength of the X-rays that were scattered to the side. Some of the scattered X-rays had a **longer** wavelength than the beam that went in. The larger the angle of scattering, the larger the change.

A wave cannot explain this. A wave that shakes an electron makes it send out waves of the same frequency, so the wavelength should not change at all.

Compton treated the X-rays as photons. Each photon collides with one electron, which is almost free and almost at rest. The electron is knocked away, and takes some of the energy of the photon with it. The photon is left with less energy, and since $E = \dfrac{hc}{\lambda}$, less energy means a longer wavelength.

Compton applied the two laws of an elastic collision: **energy is conserved** and **momentum is conserved**, with $\dfrac{h}{\lambda}$ as the momentum of the photon. The result matched his measurements at every angle.

:::definition The Compton equation
$$\Delta\lambda = \lambda' - \lambda = \dfrac{h}{mc}\left(1 - \cos\theta\right)$$

- $\lambda$ is the wavelength of the photon before the collision, and $\lambda'$ is its wavelength afterwards;
- $\theta$ is the angle through which the photon is scattered;
- $m$ is the mass of the electron, so that $\dfrac{h}{mc} = 2.43 \times 10^{-12}$ m.
:::

The change depends only on the angle, and not on the wavelength of the X-rays that are used.

| Scattering angle | $1 - \cos\theta$ | Change in wavelength |
|---|---|---|
| 0° (straight on) | 0 | 0 |
| 90° | 1 | 2.43 pm |
| 180° (straight back) | 2 | 4.86 pm, the greatest |

The change is never more than about 5 pm. That is a large fraction of the wavelength of an X-ray, but far too small to notice in visible light, whose wavelength is a hundred thousand times longer. This is why the effect was found with X-rays.

:::example The change in wavelength
An X-ray photon is scattered through 60° by an electron that was at rest. Find the change in its wavelength.
@step $\Delta\lambda = \dfrac{h}{mc}(1 - \cos\theta) = 2.43 \times 10^{-12} \times (1 - \cos 60°)$.
@step $1 - \cos 60° = 0.500$.
@answer $\Delta\lambda = 1.22 \times 10^{-12}$ m, which is 1.22 pm.
:::

:::example The wavelength after scattering
X-rays with a wavelength of 71.00 pm are scattered through 90° by electrons that were at rest. Find the wavelength of the scattered X-rays.
@step $\Delta\lambda = 2.43 \times (1 - \cos 90°) = 2.43$ pm.
@step $\lambda' = \lambda + \Delta\lambda = 71.00 + 2.43$.
@answer $\lambda' = 73.43$ pm.
:::

:::example Finding the angle
The wavelength of an X-ray photon increases by 3.645 pm when an electron scatters it. Through what angle was it scattered?
@step $1 - \cos\theta = \dfrac{\Delta\lambda}{h/mc} = \dfrac{3.645}{2.43} = 1.500$.
@step $\cos\theta = 1 - 1.500 = -0.500$.
@answer $\theta = 120°$. A negative cosine means that the photon was scattered backwards, through more than 90°.
:::

:::example The energy given to the electron
An X-ray photon with an energy of 50.0 keV collides with an electron at rest. The scattered photon has an energy of 45.5 keV. Find the kinetic energy that the electron gains, in joules.
@step Energy is conserved, so the electron gains what the photon loses: $50.0 - 45.5 = 4.5$ keV.
@step In joules: $4.5 \times 10^{3} \times 1.60 \times 10^{-19}$.
@answer The electron gains $7.2 \times 10^{-16}$ J.
:::

The Compton effect was the second piece of evidence, after the photoelectric effect, that light behaves as particles. It went further, since it showed that a photon has momentum as well as energy, and that both are conserved when a photon collides with an electron.

:::mistake Subtracting the change in wavelength
The scattered photon has less energy, so its wavelength is longer. Add $\Delta\lambda$ to the wavelength before the collision.
:::

:::summary
- A photon has no mass, but it has momentum: $p = \dfrac{h}{\lambda} = \dfrac{E}{c}$.
- Light pushes on a surface. A beam of power $P$ gives a force of $\dfrac{P}{c}$ when it is absorbed and $\dfrac{2P}{c}$ when it is reflected straight back.
- In the Compton effect, an X-ray photon collides with an electron and leaves with less energy and a longer wavelength.
- $\Delta\lambda = \dfrac{h}{mc}(1 - \cos\theta)$, with $\dfrac{h}{mc} = 2.43 \times 10^{-12}$ m for an electron.
- The change is zero at 0° and greatest at 180°. It does not depend on the wavelength of the X-rays.
- Energy and momentum are both conserved in the collision, which shows that light behaves as particles.
:::
