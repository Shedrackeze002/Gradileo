By 1900 the question seemed closed: Young's fringes and Maxwell's equations had shown that light is a wave. Then two puzzles turned up that no wave could explain. One was the colour of the glow from hot objects. The other was a small effect that Hertz noticed while he was making the first radio waves: light that shines on a metal can knock electrons out of it. The answer to both was that light carries its energy in separate packets.

## Photons

A hot object glows red, then yellow, then white as its temperature rises. The wave theory could not predict the mix of wavelengths in this light. In 1900 Max Planck found a formula that fitted the measurements, but only if he assumed that the atoms of the hot object give out energy in separate lumps, and not in a smooth stream. He called each lump a **quantum**. The energy of a quantum depended only on the frequency of the radiation.

In 1905 Albert Einstein went further. He proposed that light itself travels as a stream of these packets. A packet of light is now called a **photon**.

:::definition The energy of a photon
$$E = hf = \dfrac{hc}{\lambda}$$

- $E$ is the energy of one photon, in joules;
- $h$ is Planck's constant, $6.63 \times 10^{-34}$ J·s;
- $f$ is the frequency of the radiation, and $\lambda$ is its wavelength;
- $c$ is the speed of light, $3.00 \times 10^{8}$ m/s.
:::

The second form comes from $c = f\lambda$. The energy of a photon is proportional to its frequency, so a photon of violet light has more energy than a photon of red light, and a photon of X-rays has far more than either. Halving the wavelength doubles the energy of each photon.

A brighter beam of one colour does not have photons of greater energy. It has **more photons** arriving each second.

:::example The energy of a photon from its frequency
Find the energy of one photon of light with a frequency of $6.00 \times 10^{14}$ Hz.
@step $E = hf = 6.63 \times 10^{-34} \times 6.00 \times 10^{14}$.
@answer $E = 3.98 \times 10^{-19}$ J.
:::

### The electronvolt

The energy of one photon is a tiny fraction of a joule, so a smaller unit is useful. In Unit 2 you met the **electronvolt**: the energy that an electron gains when it moves through a potential difference of one volt.

$$1\ \text{eV} = 1.60 \times 10^{-19}\ \text{J}$$

To change joules to electronvolts, divide by $1.60 \times 10^{-19}$. To change electronvolts to joules, multiply by it.

:::example The energy of a photon from its wavelength
Find the energy of one photon of light with a wavelength of 500 nm, in joules and in electronvolts.
@step $E = \dfrac{hc}{\lambda} = \dfrac{6.63 \times 10^{-34} \times 3.00 \times 10^{8}}{500 \times 10^{-9}} = 3.98 \times 10^{-19}$ J.
@step In electronvolts: $\dfrac{3.98 \times 10^{-19}}{1.60 \times 10^{-19}}$.
@answer $E = 3.98 \times 10^{-19}$ J, which is 2.49 eV. Photons of visible light have energies between about 1.8 eV and 3.1 eV.
:::

### Counting photons

The power of a beam is the energy it delivers each second. Divide that energy by the energy of one photon to count the photons.

:::example The photons from a laser pointer
A laser gives out red light with a wavelength of 633 nm at a power of 2.0 mW. How many photons does it give out each second?
@step One photon: $E = \dfrac{hc}{\lambda} = \dfrac{6.63 \times 10^{-34} \times 3.00 \times 10^{8}}{633 \times 10^{-9}} = 3.14 \times 10^{-19}$ J.
@step In one second the laser gives out $2.0 \times 10^{-3}$ J.
@step Number of photons $= \dfrac{2.0 \times 10^{-3}}{3.14 \times 10^{-19}}$.
@answer About $6.4 \times 10^{15}$ photons each second. There are so many that the beam seems perfectly smooth.
:::

:::mistake Thinking that brighter light has more energetic photons
The energy of a photon depends only on the frequency. Making a beam brighter, without changing its colour, increases the number of photons, not the energy of each one.
:::

## The photoelectric effect

When light of a high enough frequency shines on a clean metal surface, electrons leave the metal. This is the **photoelectric effect**, and the electrons that are freed are called photoelectrons.

Careful experiments gave four results.

1. For each metal there is a **threshold frequency**, $f_0$. Light below this frequency frees no electrons at all, however bright it is.
2. Above the threshold, electrons leave at once, even when the light is very dim.
3. Brighter light, at the same frequency, frees **more** electrons each second, but their greatest kinetic energy stays the same.
4. Light of a higher frequency frees electrons with a **greater** kinetic energy.

### Why the wave theory fails

A brighter wave carries more energy, so the wave theory predicts that bright light of any colour should free electrons, and that brighter light should give them more kinetic energy. It also predicts that in dim light an electron would need time to soak up enough energy before it could leave. Every one of these predictions is wrong.

### Einstein's explanation

Einstein explained all four results with photons. Each electron is freed by a **single photon**, which gives all of its energy, $hf$, to that electron. An electron needs a certain least energy to break away from the metal. This is the **work function**, $W$, and each metal has its own value.

:::definition Einstein's photoelectric equation
$$E_{k\,\text{max}} = hf - W$$

- $E_{k\,\text{max}}$ is the greatest kinetic energy of the freed electrons;
- $hf$ is the energy of one photon;
- $W$ is the work function of the metal.

At the threshold frequency the photon has just enough energy to free an electron, so $W = hf_0$.
:::

If $hf$ is less than $W$, no single photon can free an electron, and more photons do not help. This explains the threshold. One photon frees one electron at once, so there is no delay. Brighter light means more photons and so more electrons, each with the same energy as before. A higher frequency means that each photon brings more energy, and the extra becomes kinetic energy.

Some electrons lose energy on the way out of the metal, so the equation gives the **greatest** kinetic energy.

| Metal | Work function |
|---|---|
| Caesium | 2.14 eV |
| Sodium | 2.36 eV |
| Zinc | 4.33 eV |
| Copper | 4.70 eV |

:::example The threshold of sodium
The work function of sodium is 2.36 eV. Find its threshold frequency, and the longest wavelength of light that can free electrons from it.
@step In joules: $W = 2.36 \times 1.60 \times 10^{-19} = 3.776 \times 10^{-19}$ J.
@step $W = hf_0$, so $f_0 = \dfrac{W}{h} = \dfrac{3.776 \times 10^{-19}}{6.63 \times 10^{-34}} = 5.70 \times 10^{14}$ Hz.
@step The longest wavelength goes with the lowest frequency: $\lambda_0 = \dfrac{hc}{W} = \dfrac{6.63 \times 10^{-34} \times 3.00 \times 10^{8}}{3.776 \times 10^{-19}} = 5.27 \times 10^{-7}$ m.
@answer The threshold frequency is $5.70 \times 10^{14}$ Hz, and the longest wavelength is 527 nm. Green, blue and violet light free electrons from sodium, but red light does not.
:::

:::example The kinetic energy of the electrons
Violet light with a wavelength of 400 nm shines on sodium, which has a work function of 2.36 eV. Find the greatest kinetic energy of the freed electrons, in electronvolts.
@step One photon: $E = \dfrac{hc}{\lambda} = \dfrac{6.63 \times 10^{-34} \times 3.00 \times 10^{8}}{400 \times 10^{-9}} = 4.97 \times 10^{-19}$ J, which is 3.11 eV.
@step $E_{k\,\text{max}} = hf - W = 3.11 - 2.36$.
@answer $E_{k\,\text{max}} = 0.75$ eV.
:::

### The stopping voltage

To measure the kinetic energy of the electrons, the metal is put in a vacuum tube, facing a collecting plate. The plate is made negative, so that it repels the electrons. The voltage is raised until even the fastest electrons are turned back and the current falls to zero. This is the **stopping voltage**, $V_{\text{stop}}$.

The fastest electrons use up all of their kinetic energy in climbing through this potential difference, so

$$E_{k\,\text{max}} = eV_{\text{stop}}$$

where $e = 1.60 \times 10^{-19}$ C is the size of the charge of an electron. In electronvolts the link is simple: electrons with a greatest kinetic energy of 0.75 eV are stopped by 0.75 V.

:::example Finding a work function
Ultraviolet light with a wavelength of 250 nm shines on a metal. A stopping voltage of 1.30 V just stops the fastest electrons. Find the work function of the metal.
@step One photon: $E = \dfrac{6.63 \times 10^{-34} \times 3.00 \times 10^{8}}{250 \times 10^{-9}} = 7.96 \times 10^{-19}$ J, which is 4.97 eV.
@step The stopping voltage is 1.30 V, so $E_{k\,\text{max}} = 1.30$ eV.
@step $W = hf - E_{k\,\text{max}} = 4.97 - 1.30$.
@answer $W = 3.67$ eV.
:::

:::example The speed of the fastest electrons
Electrons leave a sodium surface with a greatest kinetic energy of 0.75 eV. Find their greatest speed. The mass of an electron is $9.11 \times 10^{-31}$ kg.
@step In joules: $E_k = 0.75 \times 1.60 \times 10^{-19} = 1.20 \times 10^{-19}$ J.
@step $E_k = \dfrac{1}{2}mv^2$, so $v = \sqrt{\dfrac{2E_k}{m}} = \sqrt{\dfrac{2 \times 1.20 \times 10^{-19}}{9.11 \times 10^{-31}}}$.
@answer $v = 5.13 \times 10^{5}$ m/s.
:::

### The graph of the photoelectric effect

A graph of the greatest kinetic energy against the frequency of the light is a straight line, since $E_{k\,\text{max}} = hf - W$ has the form $y = mx + b$.

- The **slope** is Planck's constant, $h$. It is the same for every metal.
- The line meets the frequency axis at the **threshold frequency**, $f_0$.
- Extended back to zero frequency, the line meets the energy axis at $-W$.

Robert Millikan, who had measured the charge of the electron, spent ten years testing Einstein's equation in this way. He expected to prove it wrong. His measurements matched it exactly, and gave an accurate value of $h$.

Solar cells, the sensors of digital cameras and the light detectors that open automatic doors all rely on photons freeing electrons.

:::mistake Mixing joules and electronvolts
In $E_{k\,\text{max}} = hf - W$, all three energies must be in the same unit. A photon energy from $hf$ comes out in joules, and a work function is usually given in electronvolts. Change one of them before you subtract.
:::

:::mistake Thinking that brighter light frees faster electrons
Brightness sets the number of electrons freed each second. Their greatest kinetic energy depends only on the frequency of the light and on the metal.

:::summary
- Light carries energy in packets called photons. The energy of one photon is $E = hf = \dfrac{hc}{\lambda}$.
- $1\ \text{eV} = 1.60 \times 10^{-19}$ J.
- A brighter beam of one colour has more photons, each with the same energy.
- In the photoelectric effect, light frees electrons from a metal, but only above the threshold frequency.
- One photon frees one electron: $E_{k\,\text{max}} = hf - W$, and $W = hf_0$.
- The stopping voltage measures the greatest kinetic energy: $E_{k\,\text{max}} = eV_{\text{stop}}$.
- A graph of $E_{k\,\text{max}}$ against $f$ is a straight line with slope $h$.
:::
