In 1896 Henri Becquerel left some crystals of a uranium salt in a drawer on top of a wrapped photographic plate. When he developed the plate, it had been fogged as if by light, although no light could have reached it. The uranium was giving out an invisible radiation all by itself. Marie and Pierre Curie soon found other elements that did the same, and the effect was named **radioactivity**. It comes from unstable nuclei, which change into more stable ones by throwing out particles or energy. This lesson describes the three main kinds of radiation and the equations for the changes.

## Decay equations

When an unstable nucleus gives out radiation, we say that it **decays**. The nucleus before the decay is the **parent**, and the nucleus that is left is the **daughter**. A decay is written as an equation, with the parent on the left and the products on the right.

:::definition The two rules of every nuclear equation
- The **total number of nucleons** is the same before and after: the top numbers balance.
- The **total charge** is the same before and after: the bottom numbers balance.
:::

### Alpha decay

An **alpha particle** is a helium nucleus, ${}^{4}_{2}\text{He}$: two protons and two neutrons. Large nuclei, with too many nucleons to be stable, often decay this way. The mass number falls by 4 and the atomic number falls by 2, so the daughter is a different element.

:::example An alpha decay
A nucleus of radium-226, ${}^{226}_{88}\text{Ra}$, decays by alpha decay. Find the nucleus that is left.
@step Mass number: $226 - 4 = 222$.
@step Atomic number: $88 - 2 = 86$, which is radon.
@answer ${}^{226}_{88}\text{Ra} \rightarrow {}^{222}_{86}\text{Rn} + {}^{4}_{2}\text{He}$. The daughter is radon-222.
:::

### Beta-minus decay

A nucleus with too many neutrons can change one of them into a proton. The nucleus gives out a fast electron, called a **beta-minus particle** and written ${}^{0}_{-1}\text{e}$. The mass number does not change, and the atomic number rises by 1.

The electron was not in the nucleus beforehand. It is made at the moment of the decay, together with a particle with no charge and almost no mass called an **antineutrino**.

:::example A beta-minus decay
A nucleus of carbon-14, ${}^{14}_{6}\text{C}$, decays by beta-minus decay. Find the nucleus that is left.
@step Mass number: 14, unchanged.
@step Atomic number: $6 + 1 = 7$, which is nitrogen.
@answer ${}^{14}_{6}\text{C} \rightarrow {}^{14}_{7}\text{N} + {}^{0}_{-1}\text{e}$. Check the bottom numbers: $6 = 7 + (-1)$.
:::

### Beta-plus decay

A nucleus with too few neutrons can change a proton into a neutron. It gives out a **positron**, ${}^{0}_{+1}\text{e}$, which has the mass of an electron and a positive charge, together with a **neutrino**. The mass number does not change, and the atomic number falls by 1.

:::example A beta-plus decay
A nucleus of sodium-22, ${}^{22}_{11}\text{Na}$, decays by beta-plus decay. Find the nucleus that is left.
@step Mass number: 22, unchanged.
@step Atomic number: $11 - 1 = 10$, which is neon.
@answer ${}^{22}_{11}\text{Na} \rightarrow {}^{22}_{10}\text{Ne} + {}^{0}_{+1}\text{e}$.
:::

A positron is the **antiparticle** of the electron. When a positron meets an electron, the two destroy each other, and their mass becomes the energy of gamma rays.

### Gamma decay

After an alpha or a beta decay, the daughter nucleus is often left with extra energy, just as an atom can be left in an excited state. It drops to a lower energy level and gives out a photon of very high energy, a **gamma ray**. Neither the mass number nor the atomic number changes.

| Decay | Given out | Mass number | Atomic number |
|---|---|---|---|
| Alpha | ${}^{4}_{2}\text{He}$ | falls by 4 | falls by 2 |
| Beta-minus | ${}^{0}_{-1}\text{e}$ | no change | rises by 1 |
| Beta-plus | ${}^{0}_{+1}\text{e}$ | no change | falls by 1 |
| Gamma | a photon | no change | no change |

:::example Which decay was it?
A nucleus of polonium-218, ${}^{218}_{84}\text{Po}$, decays and becomes lead-214, ${}^{214}_{82}\text{Pb}$. Which kind of decay is this?
@step The mass number falls by 4 and the atomic number falls by 2.
@answer Alpha decay: ${}^{218}_{84}\text{Po} \rightarrow {}^{214}_{82}\text{Pb} + {}^{4}_{2}\text{He}$.
:::

:::example Finding the parent
A nucleus gives out a beta-minus particle and becomes xenon-131, ${}^{131}_{54}\text{Xe}$. Find the nucleus before the decay.
@step The mass number did not change, so it was 131.
@step The atomic number rose by 1 to reach 54, so it was 53, which is iodine.
@answer The parent was iodine-131: ${}^{131}_{53}\text{I} \rightarrow {}^{131}_{54}\text{Xe} + {}^{0}_{-1}\text{e}$.
:::

### Decay series

The daughter of a decay is often radioactive too, and decays in turn. A heavy nucleus may go through a long **decay series** before it reaches a stable nucleus. Only alpha decays change the mass number, so the change in mass number counts the alpha decays, and the atomic number then counts the beta decays.

:::example Several decays in a row
A nucleus of thorium-232, ${}^{232}_{90}\text{Th}$, goes through 3 alpha decays and 2 beta-minus decays. Find the nucleus at the end.
@step Mass number: $232 - 3 \times 4 = 220$.
@step Atomic number: $90 - 3 \times 2 + 2 \times 1 = 86$, which is radon.
@answer Radon-220, ${}^{220}_{86}\text{Rn}$.
:::

:::example Counting the decays in a series
Through a series of alpha and beta-minus decays, uranium-238, ${}^{238}_{92}\text{U}$, becomes lead-206, ${}^{206}_{82}\text{Pb}$. How many decays of each kind are there?
@step The mass number falls by $238 - 206 = 32$, so there are $\dfrac{32}{4} = 8$ alpha decays.
@step These alone would lower the atomic number to $92 - 2 \times 8 = 76$.
@step It ends at 82, so there are $82 - 76 = 6$ beta-minus decays.
@answer 8 alpha decays and 6 beta-minus decays.
:::

:::mistake Lowering the atomic number in beta-minus decay
The electron carries a charge of $-1$, so the atomic number of the nucleus goes **up** by 1 to keep the total charge the same. A neutron has become a proton.
:::

## The three radiations

Alpha, beta and gamma radiation all **ionize** the matter they pass through: they knock electrons out of atoms and leave a trail of ions. This is how they are detected, and it is also how they harm living cells. They differ greatly in how strongly they ionize and how far they travel.

| | Alpha | Beta-minus | Gamma |
|---|---|---|---|
| What it is | A helium nucleus | A fast electron | Electromagnetic radiation of very high frequency |
| Charge | $+2e$ | $-e$ | 0 |
| Ionizing power | Strongest | Medium | Weakest |
| Stopped by | A sheet of paper, or a few centimetres of air | A few millimetres of aluminium | Reduced by thick lead or concrete |

The two properties go together. An alpha particle ionizes so strongly that it soon loses all of its energy, so it does not get far. A gamma ray rarely meets an atom, so it ionizes weakly and passes through a great deal of matter.

### In electric and magnetic fields

Between two charged plates, alpha particles bend towards the negative plate and beta particles bend towards the positive plate. Gamma rays have no charge, so no field deflects them. In a magnetic field, alpha and beta particles curve in opposite directions. Beta particles are deflected much more than alpha particles by the same field, since a beta particle has a far smaller mass.

### The missing energy of beta decay

Alpha particles from one kind of nucleus all leave with the same energy. Beta particles leave with a whole range of energies, as if some of the energy were missing each time. In 1930 Wolfgang Pauli proposed that a second particle, with no charge and almost no mass, carries off the rest. It was named the **neutrino**, and it was detected in 1956.

### Detecting radiation

A **Geiger counter** has a tube of gas at low pressure. Radiation that enters the tube makes ions in the gas, and each burst of ions gives a pulse of current, which is counted as a click.

A detector never reads zero, even with no source nearby. There is always some **background radiation**, from rocks, the air, food and space. To find the count rate from a source alone, measure the background first and subtract it. A detector 20 cm from an alpha source reads only the background, since the alpha particles cannot travel that far in air.

### Using radiation, and staying safe

The properties of each radiation decide what it is used for.

- A **smoke detector** uses an alpha source. Alpha radiation ionizes the air strongly, so a small current flows, and smoke interrupts the current. The alpha particles cannot travel far enough to reach the people in the room.
- A factory checks the **thickness of aluminium foil** with beta radiation, since the amount that passes through depends on the thickness of the foil. Alpha radiation would not get through at all, and gamma rays would hardly be reduced.
- A **medical tracer** that is followed from outside the body must give out gamma rays, since only they can pass out of the body.

An alpha source is far more dangerous inside the body than outside it. Outside, the dead outer layer of the skin stops the alpha particles. Inside, they give all of their energy to living cells in a very short distance.

Three steps reduce the dose that a person receives from a source: less **time** near it, more **distance** from it, and **shielding** in between.

:::mistake Calling gamma radiation the most ionizing
Gamma rays are the most penetrating, and for that very reason they are the least ionizing. Alpha particles are the most ionizing and the least penetrating.
:::

:::summary
- An unstable nucleus decays by giving out radiation. In every nuclear equation the total number of nucleons and the total charge are the same before and after.
- Alpha decay gives out ${}^{4}_{2}\text{He}$: the mass number falls by 4 and the atomic number falls by 2.
- Beta-minus decay gives out an electron: a neutron becomes a proton, and the atomic number rises by 1.
- Beta-plus decay gives out a positron: a proton becomes a neutron, and the atomic number falls by 1.
- Gamma decay gives out a photon of high energy, and neither number changes.
- Alpha radiation is the most ionizing and is stopped by paper. Beta is stopped by a few millimetres of aluminium. Gamma is the most penetrating and needs thick lead or concrete.
- Fields deflect alpha and beta particles in opposite directions, and do not deflect gamma rays.
- Subtract the background count to find the count rate of a source. Time, distance and shielding reduce the dose.
:::
