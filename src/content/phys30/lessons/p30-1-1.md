A loaded truck and a small car roll towards you at the same slow speed. The car is much easier to stop than the truck. Now a tennis ball and a bullet of the same mass come towards you. The ball is easy to stop, and the bullet is not. How hard it is to stop an object depends on its mass and on its velocity together. Physics puts the two into one quantity: momentum.

## Momentum

:::definition Momentum
The **momentum** of an object is its mass multiplied by its velocity:

$$p = mv$$

Momentum is measured in kilogram metres per second (kg·m/s). It is a **vector**: it points in the direction of the velocity.
:::

:::example The momentum of a car
A 1200 kg car moves east at 25 m/s. What is its momentum?
@step $p = mv = 1200 \times 25$.
@answer $p = 30\,000$ kg·m/s, directed east.
:::

The mass must be in kilograms. Change grams to kilograms before you multiply.

:::example A mass in grams
A 58 g tennis ball is served at 50 m/s. What is the size of its momentum?
@step $m = 58\ \text{g} = 0.058$ kg.
@step $p = mv = 0.058 \times 50$.
@answer $p = 2.9$ kg·m/s.
:::

Compare the two answers. The car has about ten thousand times the momentum of the ball, although the ball is twice as fast. An object at rest has no momentum at all, whatever its mass.

Because momentum is a vector, direction matters when you add the momenta of several objects. For motion along one line, choose one direction as positive. A velocity in the other direction is negative, and so is the momentum that goes with it. A group of objects that you choose to study together is called a **system**, and the total momentum of a system is the sum of the momenta of its parts, with their signs.

:::example The total momentum of two vehicles
A 1500 kg car moves east at 20 m/s, and a 6000 kg truck moves west at 4 m/s. Take east as the positive direction. What is the total momentum of the two?
@step The car: $p = 1500 \times 20 = +30\,000$ kg·m/s.
@step The truck moves west, so its velocity is $-4$ m/s: $p = 6000 \times (-4) = -24\,000$ kg·m/s.
@step Total: $30\,000 + (-24\,000)$.
@answer $+6000$ kg·m/s. The total momentum is directed east.
:::

Momentum is not the same as kinetic energy, although both depend on mass and speed.

| | Momentum | Kinetic energy |
|---|---|---|
| Formula | $p = mv$ | $E_k = \dfrac{1}{2}mv^2$ |
| Kind of quantity | vector, with a direction | scalar, with no direction |
| When the speed doubles | it doubles | it becomes four times as large |
| Unit | kg·m/s | joule (J) |

:::mistake Adding momenta without their signs
Two objects that move in opposite directions have momenta of opposite signs. Add 30 000 and 24 000 for the car and the truck above and you get 54 000 kg·m/s, which is wrong. Give every velocity its sign first, and then add.
:::

## Change in momentum

When the velocity of an object changes, its momentum changes too. We will write $u$ for the velocity at the start and $v$ for the velocity at the end.

:::definition Change in momentum
The **change in momentum** of an object is its final momentum minus its initial momentum:

$$\Delta p = mv - mu = m(v - u)$$

The change in momentum is also a vector. A negative answer means that the change is in the negative direction.
:::

:::example A car that speeds up
A 1200 kg car moving east speeds up from 10 m/s to 25 m/s. Take east as positive. What is the change in its momentum?
@step $\Delta p = m(v - u) = 1200 \times (25 - 10)$.
@answer $\Delta p = +18\,000$ kg·m/s, directed east.
:::

The sign matters most when an object turns back. A ball that bounces off a wall has reversed its velocity, so $v$ and $u$ have opposite signs.

:::example A ball that rebounds
A 0.06 kg tennis ball moving to the right at 20 m/s hits a wall and rebounds to the left at 15 m/s. Take right as positive. What is the change in its momentum?
@step $u = +20$ m/s and $v = -15$ m/s.
@step $\Delta p = m(v - u) = 0.06 \times (-15 - 20) = 0.06 \times (-35)$.
@answer $\Delta p = -2.1$ kg·m/s. The change is directed to the left, the way the wall pushes the ball.
:::

Compare that with a lump of clay of the same mass that hits the wall at the same 20 m/s and sticks. Its velocity goes from $+20$ m/s to zero, so $\Delta p = 0.06 \times (0 - 20) = -1.2$ kg·m/s. The ball that bounces has the **greater** change in momentum, because the wall has to stop it and then throw it back.

A change in direction is a change in momentum even when the speed stays the same. A car that drives around a bend at a steady speed does not have constant momentum, because the direction of its velocity keeps changing.

:::mistake Subtracting the speeds in a rebound
In the rebound above, it is tempting to work out $0.06 \times (20 - 15) = 0.3$ kg·m/s. That treats the two velocities as if they pointed the same way. The ball has reversed, so the velocities have opposite signs, and their difference is 35 m/s, not 5 m/s.
:::

:::summary
- Momentum is mass × velocity: $p = mv$, in kg·m/s, with the mass in kilograms.
- Momentum is a vector. Along one line, give each velocity a sign, and add momenta with their signs.
- Change in momentum: $\Delta p = m(v - u)$.
- An object that rebounds has a greater change in momentum than one that stops, because its velocity changes sign.
- Momentum doubles when the speed doubles. Kinetic energy becomes four times as large.
:::
