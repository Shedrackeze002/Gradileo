Pull a wool sweater over your head on a dry day and your hair stands up, each strand pushing away from the others. Walk across a carpet and touch a door handle, and a spark jumps. Both come from the same thing: a few electrons have moved from one object to another. This lesson is about what charge is, how it is counted, and the three ways an object can be charged.

## Electric charge

All matter is made of atoms, and atoms contain two kinds of charged particle. **Protons**, in the nucleus, carry a positive charge. **Electrons**, round the outside, carry a negative charge of exactly the same size. An atom with equal numbers of each is **neutral**.

- Like charges repel: two positive objects push apart, and so do two negative objects.
- Unlike charges attract: a positive object and a negative object pull together.

In a solid, the protons are locked in place. Only electrons move from one object to another. So an object becomes **negative by gaining electrons** and **positive by losing electrons**.

:::definition The elementary charge
Charge is measured in **coulombs** (C). The charge of one proton is the **elementary charge**:

$$e = 1.60 \times 10^{-19}\ \text{C}$$

An electron has a charge of $-e$. The charge of any object is a whole number of elementary charges:

$$q = Ne$$

where $N$ is the number of electrons that the object has gained or lost. We say that charge is **quantized**.
:::

One coulomb is a very large charge. The charge on a rubbed balloon is less than a millionth of a coulomb, so charges are often given in microcoulombs ($1\ \mu\text{C} = 10^{-6}$ C) or nanocoulombs ($1\ \text{nC} = 10^{-9}$ C).

:::example Counting the extra electrons
A balloon has a charge of $-4.8 \times 10^{-8}$ C. How many excess electrons does it carry?
@step Each excess electron adds a charge of size $1.60 \times 10^{-19}$ C.
@step $N = \dfrac{q}{e} = \dfrac{4.8 \times 10^{-8}}{1.60 \times 10^{-19}}$.
@answer $N = 3.0 \times 10^{11}$ electrons, which is three hundred billion.
:::

:::example The charge from a number of electrons
A glass rod loses $2.5 \times 10^{10}$ electrons when it is rubbed with silk. What is its charge?
@step $q = Ne = 2.5 \times 10^{10} \times 1.60 \times 10^{-19} = 4.0 \times 10^{-9}$ C.
@step The rod lost electrons, so it has more protons than electrons.
@answer The charge of the rod is $+4.0 \times 10^{-9}$ C, or +4.0 nC.
:::

:::definition Conservation of charge
Charge is never created or destroyed. It only moves from one object to another, so the total charge of an isolated system stays the same.
:::

When the glass rod above lost its electrons, the silk gained them. The silk therefore has a charge of −4.0 nC, and the total charge of the rod and the silk is still zero.

**Conductors** are materials, such as metals, in which some electrons are free to move through the whole object. **Insulators**, such as plastic, glass and rubber, hold their electrons in place. A charge put on an insulator stays where it is put. A charge put on a conductor spreads over its surface.

:::mistake Saying that a positive object has gained protons
Protons do not move from one solid object to another. A positive object has **lost electrons**. Always describe charging in terms of electrons moving.
:::

## Charging objects

There are three ways to charge an object. In every one, the only thing that moves is electrons.

### Charging by friction

When two different materials are rubbed together, electrons move from the one that holds them less strongly to the one that holds them more strongly. An ebonite rod rubbed with fur takes electrons from the fur. The rod becomes negative, and the fur becomes positive by exactly the same amount.

### Charging by contact

When a charged object touches a neutral conductor, they share the charge. A negative rod passes some of its extra electrons to a metal sphere. A positive rod draws some electrons off the sphere. Either way, the sphere ends with the **same sign** as the rod.

Two identical metal spheres that touch share their total charge equally.

:::example Two spheres that touch
Two identical metal spheres on insulating stands carry charges of +12 nC and −4 nC. They are touched together and moved apart. What is the charge of each?
@step Charge is conserved. Total charge: $12 + (-4) = 8$ nC.
@step The spheres are identical, so each takes half: $\dfrac{8}{2} = 4$ nC.
@answer Each sphere has a charge of +4 nC.
:::

### Charging by induction

A charged object can charge a conductor without touching it.

1. Hold a negative rod near a neutral metal sphere. The rod repels electrons to the far side of the sphere. The sphere is still neutral, but its charge is separated.
2. Connect the sphere to the ground, for example by touching it with a finger. Electrons escape from the sphere to the ground.
3. Remove the ground connection. The sphere is now short of electrons, and they cannot return.
4. Take the rod away. The sphere is left with a positive charge, which spreads over its surface.

The sphere ends with the **opposite sign** to the rod, and the rod loses none of its own charge.

| Method | What happens | Sign of the object's charge |
|---|---|---|
| Friction | Electrons move between two rubbed materials | One becomes positive, the other negative |
| Contact | The object shares the charge of the charged one | The same as the charged object |
| Induction | The charged object pushes electrons to or from the ground, without touching | Opposite to the charged object |

The same shifting of charge explains why a charged comb picks up neutral bits of paper. The comb pulls the unlike charge in the paper a little closer and pushes the like charge a little further away, so the attraction wins.

:::example Three spheres in turn
Three identical metal spheres on insulating stands have these charges: A has +16 nC, and B and C are neutral. A is touched to B and moved away. Then B is touched to C and moved away. Find the final charge of each sphere.
@step A touches B: the total is 16 nC, so A and B have 8 nC each.
@step B touches C: the total is $8 + 0 = 8$ nC, so B and C have 4 nC each.
@step Check: $8 + 4 + 4 = 16$ nC, the charge we started with.
@answer A has +8 nC, B has +4 nC and C has +4 nC.
:::

:::mistake Removing the rod before the ground connection
In charging by induction the order matters. If the rod is taken away while the sphere is still grounded, the electrons simply flow back and the sphere ends up neutral. Remove the ground connection first, and then the rod.
:::

:::summary
- There are two kinds of charge. Like charges repel, and unlike charges attract.
- Only electrons move: an object that gains electrons is negative, and one that loses electrons is positive.
- Charge is quantized: $q = Ne$, with $e = 1.60 \times 10^{-19}$ C.
- Charge is conserved: the total charge of an isolated system does not change.
- Friction moves electrons between two materials. Contact gives an object the same sign as the charged object. Induction gives it the opposite sign.
- Identical conductors that touch share their total charge equally.
:::
