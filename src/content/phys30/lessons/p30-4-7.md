Weigh two protons and two neutrons one at a time, and add up the four masses. Then weigh a helium nucleus, which is made of exactly those four particles. The helium nucleus is lighter. Some mass has gone missing, and it went missing when the nucleus was put together. Einstein's equation $E = mc^2$ says where it went: it left as energy. That missing mass is the key to the energy of the Sun, of a nuclear power station, and of every radioactive decay in the last two lessons.

## Mass defect and binding energy

In 1905 Einstein showed that mass and energy are two forms of the same thing.

:::definition Mass and energy
$$E = mc^2$$

- $E$ is the energy, in joules, that is equivalent to a mass $m$, in kilograms;
- $c$ is the speed of light, $3.00 \times 10^{8}$ m/s.
:::

Since $c^2$ is $9.00 \times 10^{16}$, a very small mass is equivalent to a very large energy.

:::example The energy of two grams
In a nuclear power station, 2.0 g of mass is changed into energy. Find the energy released.
@step Change the units: $m = 2.0 \times 10^{-3}$ kg.
@step $E = mc^2 = 2.0 \times 10^{-3} \times (3.00 \times 10^{8})^2$.
@answer $E = 1.80 \times 10^{14}$ J, about as much as burning 6000 tonnes of coal.
:::

### A handy unit

Nuclear masses are measured in atomic mass units, and nuclear energies in megaelectronvolts. Putting 1 u into $E = mc^2$ gives a conversion that saves a lot of work: **a mass of 1 u is equivalent to 931.5 MeV of energy**.

### Mass defect

:::definition Mass defect and binding energy
- The **mass defect** of a nucleus, $\Delta m$, is the mass of its separate protons and neutrons less the mass of the nucleus itself.
- The **binding energy** is the energy equivalent to the mass defect. It is the energy that was released when the nucleus formed, and so the energy that must be supplied to pull the nucleus apart into separate nucleons.

$$\Delta m = Z\,m_p + (A - Z)\,m_n - m_{\text{nucleus}} \qquad E_b = \Delta m \times 931.5\ \text{MeV}$$

Here $m_p = 1.0073$ u is the mass of a proton and $m_n = 1.0087$ u is the mass of a neutron.
:::

:::example The binding energy of helium-4
A nucleus of helium-4, ${}^{4}_{2}\text{He}$, has a mass of 4.0015 u. Find its mass defect and its binding energy.
@step Separate nucleons: $2 \times 1.0073 + 2 \times 1.0087 = 4.0320$ u.
@step Mass defect: $4.0320 - 4.0015 = 0.0305$ u.
@step Binding energy: $0.0305 \times 931.5$.
@answer The mass defect is 0.0305 u and the binding energy is 28.4 MeV.
:::

The masses differ only in the second or third decimal place, so keep all four decimal places all the way through. Rounding early destroys the answer.

:::example The same nucleus in kilograms and joules
Find the mass defect of the helium-4 nucleus in kilograms, and its binding energy in joules. One atomic mass unit is $1.66 \times 10^{-27}$ kg.
@step $\Delta m = 0.0305 \times 1.66 \times 10^{-27} = 5.06 \times 10^{-29}$ kg.
@step $E = \Delta m\,c^2 = 5.06 \times 10^{-29} \times (3.00 \times 10^{8})^2$.
@answer $\Delta m = 5.06 \times 10^{-29}$ kg and $E = 4.56 \times 10^{-12}$ J.
:::

### Binding energy for each nucleon

A large nucleus has more binding energy than a small one simply because it has more nucleons. To compare how tightly nuclei are held together, divide the binding energy by the mass number. The nucleus with **more binding energy for each nucleon** is more tightly bound, and more stable.

:::example Which nucleus is more tightly bound?
A nucleus of iron-56, ${}^{56}_{26}\text{Fe}$, has a mass of 55.9207 u. A nucleus of uranium-235, ${}^{235}_{92}\text{U}$, has a mass of 234.9935 u. Find the binding energy for each nucleon in each nucleus.
@step Iron-56: separate nucleons $26 \times 1.0073 + 30 \times 1.0087 = 56.4508$ u, so the mass defect is $56.4508 - 55.9207 = 0.5301$ u.
@step Binding energy $0.5301 \times 931.5 = 493.79$ MeV, or $\dfrac{493.79}{56} = 8.82$ MeV for each nucleon.
@step Uranium-235: separate nucleons $92 \times 1.0073 + 143 \times 1.0087 = 236.9157$ u, so the mass defect is $236.9157 - 234.9935 = 1.9222$ u.
@step Binding energy $1.9222 \times 931.5 = 1790.53$ MeV, or $\dfrac{1790.53}{235} = 7.62$ MeV for each nucleon.
@answer Iron-56 has 8.82 MeV for each nucleon and uranium-235 has 7.62 MeV, so iron-56 is more tightly bound.
:::

Any change that moves nuclei **towards the peak** releases energy, and there are two ways to do it, which the second half of this lesson describes.

### Mass that becomes power

A source of nuclear energy loses mass all the time. Power is energy each second, so $E = mc^2$ gives the mass lost each second.

:::example The mass that the Sun loses
The Sun gives out energy at a rate of $3.8 \times 10^{26}$ W, all of it from fusion. How much mass does it lose each second?
@step In one second the Sun gives out $3.8 \times 10^{26}$ J.
@step $m = \dfrac{E}{c^2} = \dfrac{3.8 \times 10^{26}}{(3.00 \times 10^{8})^2}$.
@answer $4.22 \times 10^{9}$ kg each second, which is more than four million tonnes.
:::

:::example The mass that a reactor loses
The reactor of a power station releases energy at a steady rate of 3000 MW. How much mass is changed into energy in one day, which is $86\,400$ s?
@step Energy in one day: $3000 \times 10^{6} \times 86\,400 = 2.592 \times 10^{14}$ J.
@step $m = \dfrac{E}{c^2} = \dfrac{2.592 \times 10^{14}}{9.00 \times 10^{16}} = 2.88 \times 10^{-3}$ kg.
@answer 2.88 g in a day.
:::

:::mistake Rounding the masses
A mass defect is a small difference between two large numbers. With the masses rounded to 2 decimal places, helium-4 would seem to have a mass defect of 0.04 u in place of 0.0305 u, and the binding energy would be wrong by almost a third. Keep 4 decimal places.
:::

## Fission and fusion

### Fission

:::definition Nuclear fission
Fission is the splitting of a large nucleus into two smaller nuclei, with the release of energy.
:::

In a reactor, a nucleus of uranium-235 absorbs a slow neutron. The nucleus becomes unstable and splits into two nuclei of middle mass, and two or three free neutrons fly out. The pieces are closer to the peak of the graph than uranium was, so they are more tightly bound, their total mass is less, and the difference appears as energy.

A fission equation balances like every other nuclear equation: the top numbers balance, and the bottom numbers balance. A neutron is written ${}^{1}_{0}\text{n}$.

:::example Counting the neutrons
A nucleus of uranium-235, ${}^{235}_{92}\text{U}$, absorbs a neutron and splits into barium-141, ${}^{141}_{56}\text{Ba}$, and krypton-92, ${}^{92}_{36}\text{Kr}$. How many neutrons are released?
@step Before: $235 + 1 = 236$ nucleons.
@step In the two new nuclei: $141 + 92 = 233$.
@answer $236 - 233 = 3$ neutrons are released. The charges balance too: $92 = 56 + 36$.
:::

:::example The missing product
A nucleus of uranium-235 absorbs a neutron and splits into tellurium-134, ${}^{134}_{52}\text{Te}$, a second nucleus, and 3 free neutrons. Find the second nucleus.
@step Mass number: $235 + 1 - 134 - 3 = 99$.
@step Atomic number: $92 - 52 = 40$, which is zirconium.
@answer Zirconium-99, ${}^{99}_{40}\text{Zr}$.
:::

### The chain reaction

Each fission releases neutrons, and each of those neutrons can cause another fission. This is a **chain reaction**. If, on average, exactly one neutron from each fission goes on to cause another, the reaction carries on at a steady rate. This is what a reactor does. If more than one does, the rate grows very quickly, which is what happens in a bomb.

A reactor has three parts that manage the neutrons.

- The **fuel rods** hold the uranium.
- The **moderator**, often water or graphite, slows the neutrons down. Slow neutrons are captured by uranium-235 much more readily than fast ones, so they are more likely to cause fission.
- The **control rods** absorb neutrons. Pushing them further in absorbs more neutrons and slows the reaction, and pulling them out speeds it up.

The energy of the fission heats water into steam, which turns turbines, as in any other power station. The products of fission are strongly radioactive, and some have long half-lives, so the used fuel has to be stored safely for a very long time.

### Fusion

:::definition Nuclear fusion
Fusion is the joining of two small nuclei to make a larger one, with the release of energy.
:::

Fusion moves light nuclei up the steep left side of the graph, towards the peak. The Sun's energy comes from the fusion of hydrogen nuclei into helium.

Nuclei are positive, so they repel each other. To fuse, they must move fast enough to overcome the repulsion and come within reach of the strong force, about $10^{-15}$ m. That takes a temperature of millions of degrees, which is found at the centre of a star. Holding a gas that hot for long enough is the great difficulty of building a fusion power station on the Earth.

:::example A fusion equation
In this reaction, X stands for a particle: ${}^{2}_{1}\text{H} + {}^{3}_{1}\text{H} \rightarrow {}^{4}_{2}\text{He} + \text{X}$. Find the mass number and the atomic number of X.
@step Mass number: $2 + 3 - 4 = 1$.
@step Atomic number: $1 + 1 - 2 = 0$.
@answer X has a mass number of 1 and no charge, so it is a neutron, ${}^{1}_{0}\text{n}$.
:::

### The energy of a reaction

The energy released in any nuclear reaction comes from the mass that is lost. Add up the masses before, add up the masses after, and change the difference into energy.

:::example The energy of a fusion reaction
Hydrogen-2 and hydrogen-3 fuse: ${}^{2}_{1}\text{H} + {}^{3}_{1}\text{H} \rightarrow {}^{4}_{2}\text{He} + {}^{1}_{0}\text{n}$. The masses are: hydrogen-2, 2.0141 u; hydrogen-3, 3.0160 u; helium-4, 4.0026 u; neutron, 1.0087 u. Find the mass that is lost and the energy that is released.
@step Before: $2.0141 + 3.0160 = 5.0301$ u.
@step After: $4.0026 + 1.0087 = 5.0113$ u.
@step Mass lost: $5.0301 - 5.0113 = 0.0188$ u.
@answer 0.0188 u is lost, and $0.0188 \times 931.5 = 17.5$ MeV is released.
:::

:::example The energy of a fission
Uranium-235 absorbs a neutron and splits: ${}^{235}_{92}\text{U} + {}^{1}_{0}\text{n} \rightarrow {}^{141}_{56}\text{Ba} + {}^{92}_{36}\text{Kr} + 3\,{}^{1}_{0}\text{n}$. The masses are: uranium-235, 235.0439 u; barium-141, 140.9144 u; krypton-92, 91.9262 u; neutron, 1.0087 u. Find the mass that is lost and the energy that is released.
@step Before: $235.0439 + 1.0087 = 236.0526$ u.
@step After: $140.9144 + 91.9262 + 3 \times 1.0087 = 235.8667$ u.
@step Mass lost: $236.0526 - 235.8667 = 0.1859$ u.
@answer 0.1859 u is lost, and $0.1859 \times 931.5 = 173.2$ MeV is released.
:::

One fission releases about ten times as much energy as one fusion, but it also uses about fifty times as much mass of fuel. Kilogram for kilogram, fusion releases several times more energy than fission, and both release millions of times more than burning.

### From one nucleus to a power station

:::example The energy of many fissions
Each fission of a uranium-235 nucleus releases about 200 MeV ($1\ \text{MeV} = 1.60 \times 10^{-13}$ J). One gram of uranium-235 contains $2.56 \times 10^{21}$ nuclei. Find the energy released when all of them undergo fission.
@step One fission: $200 \times 1.60 \times 10^{-13} = 3.20 \times 10^{-11}$ J.
@step $2.56 \times 10^{21} \times 3.20 \times 10^{-11}$.
@answer $8.19 \times 10^{10}$ J from one gram, about as much as burning 3 tonnes of coal.
:::

:::example Fissions each second
A reactor releases energy at a rate of 1000 MW, and each fission releases about 200 MeV. How many fissions take place each second?
@step One fission: $3.20 \times 10^{-11}$ J. The reactor releases $1000 \times 10^{6}$ J each second.
@step $\dfrac{1000 \times 10^{6}}{3.20 \times 10^{-11}}$.
@answer $3.13 \times 10^{19}$ fissions each second.
:::

:::mistake Mixing up fission and fusion
Fission splits a heavy nucleus, such as uranium. Fusion joins light nuclei, such as hydrogen. Both release energy for the same reason: the products are more tightly bound and have less mass.
:::

:::summary
- Mass and energy are equivalent: $E = mc^2$. A mass of 1 u is equivalent to 931.5 MeV.
- The mass defect of a nucleus is the mass of its separate nucleons less the mass of the nucleus. The binding energy is the energy equivalent to it.
- The binding energy for each nucleon measures how tightly a nucleus is bound. It is greatest for nuclei near iron and nickel.
- Fission splits a large nucleus, and fusion joins small nuclei. Both move towards the peak of the graph, so both release energy.
- In a nuclear reaction the energy released is the mass lost times 931.5 MeV for each atomic mass unit.
- A chain reaction is possible because each fission releases neutrons. A moderator slows the neutrons, and control rods absorb them.
- Fusion needs millions of degrees, so that nuclei move fast enough to overcome their repulsion. It is the source of the energy of the Sun.
:::
