Lift a book on to a shelf and you do work against gravity. The work is stored as gravitational potential energy, and the book gives it back as kinetic energy if it falls. Charges in an electric field behave the same way. Push a positive charge towards a positive plate and you do work against the electric force. That work is stored as **electric potential energy**, and the charge gives it back as kinetic energy when it is let go. A battery, a spark and the beam of an X-ray tube all depend on this.

## Potential difference and energy

The energy stored depends on how much charge is moved: twice the charge needs twice the work. So it is useful to give the energy **for each coulomb**.

:::definition Potential difference
The **potential difference** between two points is the change in electric potential energy for each coulomb of charge that moves between them:

$$\Delta V = \dfrac{\Delta E}{q}$$

It is measured in **volts** (V). One volt is one joule per coulomb. Potential difference is often called **voltage**.
:::

:::example The energy from a battery
How much energy does a 12 V battery give to 30 C of charge that passes through it?
@step Rearrange: $\Delta E = q\Delta V$.
@step $\Delta E = 30 \times 12$.
@answer $\Delta E = 360$ J.
:::

:::example Finding a potential difference
It takes $4.5 \times 10^{-4}$ J of work to move a charge of +1.5 μC from point B to point A. What is the potential difference between the points?
@step $\Delta V = \dfrac{\Delta E}{q} = \dfrac{4.5 \times 10^{-4}}{1.5 \times 10^{-6}}$.
@answer $\Delta V = 300$ V. Point A is at the higher potential, because work was needed to take a positive charge there.
:::

A positive charge that is free to move goes from high potential to low potential, as a ball rolls downhill, and loses electric potential energy as it speeds up. A negative charge does the opposite: an electron is pushed from low potential to high potential, and that is the direction in which it loses potential energy.

The signs look after themselves if you use $\Delta E = q\Delta V$ with the sign of the charge, and take $\Delta V$ as the potential at the end minus the potential at the start.

:::example A change in potential energy, with its sign
Point A is at a potential of 400 V, and point B is at 100 V. A charge of −2.0 μC moves from A to B. Find the change in its electric potential energy.
@step $\Delta V = V_B - V_A = 100 - 400 = -300$ V.
@step $\Delta E = q\Delta V = (-2.0\ \mu\text{C}) \times (-300\ \text{V}) = +600\ \mu\text{J}$.
@answer The charge gains 600 μJ of potential energy. Something must do work to move a negative charge to a lower potential.
:::

### The electronvolt

The energies of single particles are tiny in joules, so physicists use a smaller unit.

:::definition The electronvolt
One **electronvolt** (eV) is the energy that one elementary charge gains when it moves through a potential difference of one volt:

$$1\ \text{eV} = 1.60 \times 10^{-19}\ \text{J}$$
:::

An electron that is accelerated through 5000 V gains 5000 eV. No calculation is needed. A particle with two elementary charges, such as an alpha particle, gains twice as many electronvolts as the volts it moves through.

:::example Joules and electronvolts
An electron is accelerated from rest through a potential difference of 5000 V. Find the kinetic energy it gains, in electronvolts and in joules.
@step One elementary charge moved through 5000 V gains 5000 eV.
@step In joules: $5000 \times 1.60 \times 10^{-19} = 8.0 \times 10^{-16}$ J.
@answer 5000 eV, or 5.0 keV, which is $8.0 \times 10^{-16}$ J.
:::

:::mistake Calling the electronvolt a unit of voltage
The electronvolt has "volt" in its name, but it is a unit of **energy**, like the joule. The volt measures energy for each coulomb.
:::

## The field between parallel plates

Connect two flat metal plates to the terminals of a battery, and one becomes positive and the other negative. Between them the field lines are straight and evenly spaced: the field is uniform.

Suppose a charge $q$ moves from one plate to the other, a distance $d$. The force on it is $qE$ all the way, so the work done is $qEd$. But the work is also $q\Delta V$. Setting these equal, the charge cancels.

:::definition The field between parallel plates
$$E = \dfrac{\Delta V}{d}$$

- $\Delta V$ is the potential difference between the plates, in volts;
- $d$ is the distance between the plates, in metres.

This gives the field in volts per metre (V/m), which is the same unit as the newton per coulomb: 1 V/m = 1 N/C.
:::

:::example The field between two plates
Two parallel plates are 3.0 cm apart, with a potential difference of 600 V between them. Find the field between the plates, and the force on a proton there. A proton has a charge of $+1.60 \times 10^{-19}$ C.
@step Change to metres: $d = 0.030$ m.
@step $E = \dfrac{\Delta V}{d} = \dfrac{600}{0.030} = 20\,000$ V/m.
@step $F = qE = 1.60 \times 10^{-19} \times 20\,000 = 3.2 \times 10^{-15}$ N.
@answer The field is 20 000 V/m, and the force on the proton is $3.2 \times 10^{-15}$ N towards the negative plate. The force is the same wherever the proton is between the plates.
:::

Because the field is uniform, the potential changes evenly from one plate to the other. A charge that crosses a third of the gap moves through a third of the potential difference.

:::example Part of the way across
The proton in the last example moves 1.0 cm in the direction of the field. How much kinetic energy does it gain?
@step It crosses $\dfrac{1.0}{3.0}$ of the gap, so it moves through $\dfrac{1.0}{3.0} \times 600 = 200$ V.
@step One elementary charge moved through 200 V gains 200 eV.
@step In joules: $200 \times 1.60 \times 10^{-19}$.
@answer 200 eV, which is $3.2 \times 10^{-17}$ J.
:::

:::mistake Leaving the plate separation in centimetres
$E = \dfrac{\Delta V}{d}$ needs the distance in metres. With 600 V across 3.0 cm, the field is $\dfrac{600}{0.030} = 20\,000$ V/m, not $\dfrac{600}{3.0} = 200$.
:::

:::summary
- Potential difference is the change in electric potential energy for each coulomb: $\Delta V = \dfrac{\Delta E}{q}$. One volt is one joule per coulomb.
- A charge that moves through a potential difference gains or loses the energy $\Delta E = q\Delta V$.
- One electronvolt is $1.60 \times 10^{-19}$ J: the energy one elementary charge gains from one volt.
- A free positive charge moves towards lower potential, and a free negative charge towards higher potential.
- Between parallel plates the field is uniform: $E = \dfrac{\Delta V}{d}$, in V/m, which equals N/C.
:::
