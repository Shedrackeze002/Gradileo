In 1785 Charles Coulomb hung a small charged ball on a fine thread, brought a second charged ball near it, and measured how far the thread twisted. From that twist he found how the force between two charges depends on their sizes and on the distance between them. His result has the same shape as Newton's law of gravitation, which you met in Grade 11.

## Coulomb's law

:::definition Coulomb's law
The electric force between two point charges is proportional to the product of the charges, and inversely proportional to the square of the distance between them:

$$F = \dfrac{kq_1q_2}{r^2}$$

- $q_1$ and $q_2$ are the charges, in coulombs;
- $r$ is the distance between their centres, in metres;
- $k = 8.99 \times 10^{9}\ \text{N·m}^2/\text{C}^2$ is Coulomb's constant.

The force acts along the line that joins the charges. It is a repulsion for like charges and an attraction for unlike charges.
:::

Use the **sizes** of the charges in the formula, without their signs, to find the size of the force. Then use the signs to decide its direction. By Newton's third law, each charge feels a force of the same size, in opposite directions.

:::example The force between two charged spheres
Two small spheres carry charges of +3.0 μC and −5.0 μC. Their centres are 0.30 m apart. Find the force that each exerts on the other.
@step Change to coulombs: $3.0 \times 10^{-6}$ C and $5.0 \times 10^{-6}$ C.
@step $F = \dfrac{kq_1q_2}{r^2} = \dfrac{8.99 \times 10^{9} \times 3.0 \times 10^{-6} \times 5.0 \times 10^{-6}}{0.30^2}$.
@step $F = \dfrac{0.13485}{0.09} = 1.50$ N.
@answer Each sphere pulls the other with a force of 1.50 N. The charges are unlike, so the force is an attraction.
:::

### An inverse square law

Because the distance is squared, the force changes quickly with distance. At twice the distance the force is $\dfrac{1}{2^2} = \dfrac{1}{4}$ as large. At three times the distance it is $\dfrac{1}{9}$ as large. At half the distance it is 4 times as large.

| Change | The force is multiplied by |
|---|---|
| One charge is doubled | 2 |
| Both charges are doubled | 4 |
| The distance is doubled | $\dfrac{1}{4}$ |
| The distance is halved | 4 |
| One charge is doubled and the distance is doubled | $\dfrac{1}{2}$ |

:::example Finding the distance
Two point charges of 2.0 μC and 4.0 μC repel each other with a force of 0.90 N. How far apart are they?
@step Rearrange Coulomb's law: $r = \sqrt{\dfrac{kq_1q_2}{F}}$.
@step $r = \sqrt{\dfrac{8.99 \times 10^{9} \times 2.0 \times 10^{-6} \times 4.0 \times 10^{-6}}{0.90}} = \sqrt{0.0799}$.
@answer $r = 0.283$ m.
:::

:::example The force inside a hydrogen atom
In a hydrogen atom, the electron is about $5.29 \times 10^{-11}$ m from the proton. Each has a charge of size $1.60 \times 10^{-19}$ C. Find the electric force between them.
@step $F = \dfrac{kq_1q_2}{r^2} = \dfrac{8.99 \times 10^{9} \times (1.60 \times 10^{-19})^2}{(5.29 \times 10^{-11})^2}$.
@answer $F = 8.22 \times 10^{-8}$ N, an attraction. It is tiny, but the electron's mass is tinier still, so the force gives it a huge acceleration.
:::

Coulomb's law and Newton's law of gravitation have the same form, but they differ in two ways. Gravity only attracts, while the electric force can attract or repel. The electric force is also far stronger: between the electron and the proton of a hydrogen atom it is about $10^{39}$ times the gravitational force.

:::mistake Forgetting to square the distance, or leaving it in centimetres
The distance must be in metres, and it is squared. A separation of 30 cm goes into the formula as $0.30^2 = 0.09$, not as 0.30 and not as $30^2$.
:::

## The net electric force

When a charge has more than one other charge near it, each of the others exerts its own force on it, as if the rest were not there. The net force is the vector sum of these forces.

Work through such a problem in these steps.

1. Find the size of each force with Coulomb's law.
2. Decide the direction of each force: like charges repel, and unlike charges attract.
3. Add the forces as vectors. Along one line, give each force a sign. At right angles, use the Pythagorean theorem and the tangent.

:::example Three charges on a line
Charge A (+2.0 μC) is 0.20 m west of charge B (+3.0 μC). Charge C (+6.0 μC) is 0.40 m east of B. Find the net force on B. Take east as positive.
@step A on B: $F = \dfrac{8.99 \times 10^{9} \times 2.0 \times 10^{-6} \times 3.0 \times 10^{-6}}{0.20^2} = 1.35$ N. Both are positive, so A pushes B away from it, to the east: $+1.35$ N.
@step C on B: $F = \dfrac{8.99 \times 10^{9} \times 6.0 \times 10^{-6} \times 3.0 \times 10^{-6}}{0.40^2} = 1.01$ N. Both are positive, so C pushes B away from it, to the west: $-1.01$ N.
@step Net force: $(+1.35) + (-1.01) = +0.34$ N.
@answer The net force on B is 0.34 N east.
:::

:::example Three charges at a right angle
Charge A (+5.0 μC) is at the corner of a right angle. Charge B (+2.0 μC) is 0.30 m east of A, and charge C (−3.0 μC) is 0.30 m north of A. Find the net force on A.
@step B on A: $F = \dfrac{8.99 \times 10^{9} \times 5.0 \times 10^{-6} \times 2.0 \times 10^{-6}}{0.30^2} = 0.999$ N. Like charges, so A is pushed away from B: west.
@step C on A: $F = \dfrac{8.99 \times 10^{9} \times 5.0 \times 10^{-6} \times 3.0 \times 10^{-6}}{0.30^2} = 1.498$ N. Unlike charges, so A is pulled towards C: north.
@step The two forces are at right angles: $F = \sqrt{0.999^2 + 1.498^2} = 1.80$ N.
@step $\theta = \tan^{-1}\left(\dfrac{1.498}{0.999}\right) = 56.3°$ from the east-west line.
@answer The net force on A is 1.80 N, in a direction 56.3° north of west.
:::

:::mistake Adding forces at right angles as plain numbers
In the last example, $0.999 + 1.498 = 2.497$ N is not the net force. Forces are vectors. Two forces at right angles add like the two shorter sides of a right triangle.
:::

:::summary
- Coulomb's law: $F = \dfrac{kq_1q_2}{r^2}$, with $k = 8.99 \times 10^{9}\ \text{N·m}^2/\text{C}^2$, the charges in coulombs and the distance in metres.
- Use the sizes of the charges for the size of the force, and their signs for its direction: like charges repel, and unlike charges attract.
- The force follows an inverse square law: twice the distance gives a quarter of the force.
- Each of two charges feels a force of the same size, in opposite directions.
- With several charges, find each force separately and add them as vectors.
:::
