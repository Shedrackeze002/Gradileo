For more than a century, physicists argued about what light is. Isaac Newton pictured a stream of tiny particles. Christiaan Huygens pictured a wave. Rays, mirrors and lenses could not settle it, since both pictures explain reflection and refraction. In 1801 Thomas Young settled it with a card and two narrow slits. He showed that light added to light can make darkness, and only waves can do that.

## Young's double-slit experiment

### Diffraction

When a wave passes through a gap or round an edge, it spreads out into the region behind. This spreading is **diffraction**. Water waves spread out after they pass through a harbour mouth, and sound bends round a doorway. The spreading is greatest when the gap is about as wide as the wavelength.

The wavelength of light is less than a thousandth of a millimetre, so light only spreads noticeably through a very narrow slit. This is why we do not see light bend round corners in everyday life, and why shadows look sharp.

### Interference

In Grade 11 you saw that two waves that meet add together. Where crest meets crest, the waves add to make a larger wave: this is **constructive** interference. Where crest meets trough, they cancel: this is **destructive** interference.

Young passed light of a single colour through two narrow slits, close together. The light diffracted at each slit, so the two beams spread out and overlapped on a screen. The screen did not show two bright lines. It showed a row of bright and dark bands, called **fringes**.

The two slits act as two sources that are in step. Light from the two slits travels different distances to reach a point on the screen, and the **path difference** decides what happens there.

- At a **bright** fringe, the path difference is a whole number of wavelengths: $0$, $\lambda$, $2\lambda$, and so on. Crests arrive together.
- At a **dark** fringe, the path difference is an odd number of half wavelengths: $\tfrac{1}{2}\lambda$, $1\tfrac{1}{2}\lambda$, $2\tfrac{1}{2}\lambda$, and so on. A crest arrives with a trough.

The central bright fringe is the same distance from both slits, so its path difference is zero. The $n$th bright fringe from the centre has a path difference of $n\lambda$, and the $n$th dark fringe has $\left(n - \tfrac{1}{2}\right)\lambda$.

Particles cannot cancel one another, so a stream of particles through two slits would make two bright lines. The dark fringes were the evidence that light is a wave.

### The double-slit equations

:::definition Double-slit interference
The bright fringes are at angles $\theta$ from the straight-through direction, where

$$d \sin\theta = n\lambda$$

Near the centre of the pattern the fringes are evenly spaced, and the distance from one bright fringe to the next is

$$\Delta x = \dfrac{\lambda L}{d}$$

- $d$ is the distance between the two slits;
- $\lambda$ is the wavelength of the light;
- $n$ is the number of the fringe, counted from 0 at the centre;
- $L$ is the distance from the slits to the screen;
- $\Delta x$ is the fringe spacing.
:::

The $n$th bright fringe is $n$ spacings from the centre, at $x = \dfrac{n\lambda L}{d}$. Change every length to metres before you calculate.

The equation shows how to make the fringes wider apart: use light of a longer wavelength, move the screen further away, or bring the slits closer together. Red light gives wider fringes than blue light.

:::example Measuring the wavelength of light
Light passes through two slits that are 0.25 mm apart and falls on a screen 2.0 m away. The bright fringes are 4.8 mm apart. Find the wavelength of the light.
@step $\Delta x = \dfrac{\lambda L}{d}$, so $\lambda = \dfrac{\Delta x \, d}{L}$.
@step $\lambda = \dfrac{4.8 \times 10^{-3} \times 0.25 \times 10^{-3}}{2.0} = 6.0 \times 10^{-7}$ m.
@answer The wavelength is 600 nm. This is orange light.
:::

:::example Using a fringe further out
Two slits are 0.50 mm apart, and the screen is 2.5 m away. The third bright fringe is 7.5 mm from the central bright fringe. Find the wavelength.
@step $x = \dfrac{n\lambda L}{d}$, so $\lambda = \dfrac{xd}{nL}$.
@step $\lambda = \dfrac{7.5 \times 10^{-3} \times 0.50 \times 10^{-3}}{3 \times 2.5} = 5.0 \times 10^{-7}$ m.
@answer The wavelength is 500 nm.
:::

:::example The angle of a fringe
Light of wavelength 600 nm passes through two slits that are $4.0 \times 10^{-6}$ m apart. At what angle is the second bright fringe?
@step $\sin\theta = \dfrac{n\lambda}{d} = \dfrac{2 \times 600 \times 10^{-9}}{4.0 \times 10^{-6}} = 0.300$.
@answer $\theta = 17.5°$.
:::

## Diffraction gratings

The fringes of a double slit are faint and broad, so their positions are hard to measure. A **diffraction grating** does the same job far better. It is a sheet with thousands of parallel slits, evenly spaced, often several hundred in each millimetre.

Light from all of the slits arrives in step only at the exact angles where the path difference between neighbouring slits is a whole number of wavelengths. At every other angle, the light from the many slits cancels. So a grating gives bright lines that are sharp and narrow, with darkness between them, and they are spread over large angles.

:::definition The grating equation
$$d \sin\theta = n\lambda$$

- $d$ is the distance between neighbouring slits. A grating with $N$ lines in each metre has $d = \dfrac{1}{N}$;
- $\theta$ is the angle of the bright line from the straight-through direction;
- $n$ is the **order** of the line: 0 for the central line, 1 for the first line on each side, 2 for the second, and so on.
:::

The angles are not small, so the equation with $\sin\theta$ must be used, and not the fringe-spacing shortcut. A bright line at angle $\theta$ strikes a screen a distance $L$ away at $x = L\tan\theta$ from the centre.

:::example The slit spacing and the first two orders
A grating has 500 lines in each millimetre. Light of wavelength 600 nm passes through it. Find the angles of the first and second bright lines.
@step There are $500 \times 10^{3}$ lines in each metre, so $d = \dfrac{1}{5.00 \times 10^{5}} = 2.00 \times 10^{-6}$ m.
@step First order: $\sin\theta = \dfrac{\lambda}{d} = \dfrac{600 \times 10^{-9}}{2.00 \times 10^{-6}} = 0.300$, so $\theta = 17.5°$.
@step Second order: $\sin\theta = \dfrac{2\lambda}{d} = 0.600$, so $\theta = 36.9°$.
@answer The first-order lines are at 17.5° and the second-order lines are at 36.9°, on each side of the centre.
:::

:::example How many lines?
For the grating and the light of the last example, what is the highest order that appears, and how many bright lines are there in all?
@step $\sin\theta$ cannot be more than 1, so $n \leq \dfrac{d}{\lambda} = \dfrac{2.00 \times 10^{-6}}{600 \times 10^{-9}} = 3.33$.
@step The highest whole number is 3.
@answer The highest order is the third. There are three lines on each side and one in the centre, so 7 bright lines in all.
:::

:::example Finding a wavelength with a grating
A grating has 300 lines in each millimetre. The first-order line of a lamp is at 10.0°. Find the wavelength of its light.
@step $d = \dfrac{1}{3.00 \times 10^{5}} = 3.33 \times 10^{-6}$ m.
@step $\lambda = d \sin\theta = 3.33 \times 10^{-6} \times \sin 10.0°$.
@answer $\lambda = 5.79 \times 10^{-7}$ m, which is 579 nm.
:::

### Gratings and spectra

The angle of a bright line depends on the wavelength, so a grating separates colours. When white light passes through a grating, the central line is white, since every colour has a path difference of zero there. On each side, each order is a spectrum. Red light has the longest wavelength, so it is turned through the largest angle, and violet through the smallest. This is the opposite of a prism, which bends violet the most.

A grating with more lines in each millimetre has a smaller $d$, so it spreads the colours over larger angles.

A **spectroscope** uses a grating to measure the wavelengths in the light from a hot gas or a star. Each element gives out its own set of wavelengths, so the spectrum shows what the source is made of. You will use this in Unit 4.

The equation needs $d$, the distance between slits, in metres. Change the number of lines to lines in each metre, then take its reciprocal.

:::summary
- Diffraction is the spreading of a wave after it passes through a gap or round an edge.
- Two slits make bright fringes where the path difference is $n\lambda$, and dark fringes where it is $\left(n - \tfrac{1}{2}\right)\lambda$.
- Young's fringes showed that light is a wave.
- $d \sin\theta = n\lambda$ for the bright fringes, and near the centre $\Delta x = \dfrac{\lambda L}{d}$.
- A diffraction grating has many evenly spaced slits. It gives sharp bright lines where $d \sin\theta = n\lambda$, with $d = \dfrac{1}{N}$.
- A grating turns red light through a larger angle than violet, and is used to measure wavelengths in a spectroscope.
:::
