Nobody can say when one particular unstable nucleus will decay. It might be in the next second, or not for a thousand years, and nothing we do to it makes any difference. Yet a sample holds such a huge number of nuclei that their behaviour together is perfectly regular, in the same way that nobody can predict one toss of a coin but everybody can predict that about half of a million tosses will be heads. This regular behaviour gives every radioactive isotope its own clock, and the clock can date a piece of wood, a rock, or the Earth itself.

## Half-life

Decay is **random**. Each nucleus of an isotope has the same chance of decaying in each second, whatever its age. So in any given time, the same **fraction** of the nuclei that are left will decay.

:::definition Half-life
The half-life of a radioactive isotope is the time for half of the nuclei in a sample to decay.
:::

After one half-life, half of the nuclei are left. After a second half-life, half of those are left, which is one quarter of the original number. The amount never falls by a fixed number each time. It falls by a fixed fraction.

| Half-lives passed | Fraction left | Percentage left |
|---|---|---|
| 0 | 1 | 100% |
| 1 | $\dfrac{1}{2}$ | 50% |
| 2 | $\dfrac{1}{4}$ | 25% |
| 3 | $\dfrac{1}{8}$ | 12.5% |
| 4 | $\dfrac{1}{16}$ | 6.25% |
| 5 | $\dfrac{1}{32}$ | 3.125% |

Half-lives cover an enormous range, from a tiny fraction of a second to billions of years. The half-life of an isotope does not depend on temperature, pressure or chemical state. Decay happens in the nucleus, which is not affected by what the electrons or the surroundings are doing.

:::definition The decay equation
$$N = N_0 \left(\dfrac{1}{2}\right)^{t/T}$$

- $N_0$ is the amount at the start, and $N$ is the amount left after a time $t$;
- $T$ is the half-life, in the same unit as $t$;
- $\dfrac{t}{T}$ is the number of half-lives that have passed.

The amount can be a number of nuclei, a mass, or a percentage.
:::

:::example A whole number of half-lives
Iodine-131 has a half-life of 8 days. A sample contains 80 mg of iodine-131. How much is left after 24 days?
@step $\dfrac{t}{T} = \dfrac{24}{8} = 3$ half-lives.
@step $N = 80 \times \left(\dfrac{1}{2}\right)^{3} = 80 \times \dfrac{1}{8}$.
@answer 10 mg.
:::

:::example The percentage left
Caesium-137 has a half-life of 30 years. What percentage of the caesium-137 in a sample is still there after 150 years?
@step $\dfrac{150}{30} = 5$ half-lives.
@step $100\% \times \left(\dfrac{1}{2}\right)^{5}$.
@answer 3.13%, to 2 decimal places.
:::

:::example How much has decayed
Cobalt-60 has a half-life of 5.3 years. A sample contains 64 g of cobalt-60. How much of it has decayed after 10.6 years?
@step $\dfrac{10.6}{5.3} = 2$ half-lives, so $64 \times \dfrac{1}{4} = 16$ g is left.
@step Decayed: $64 - 16$.
@answer 48 g has decayed.
:::

### Working backwards

:::example Finding the half-life
The activity of a sample falls from 400 Bq to 50 Bq in 45 hours. Find the half-life of the isotope.
@step $\dfrac{400}{50} = 8 = 2^3$, so 3 half-lives have passed.
@step $T = \dfrac{45}{3}$.
@answer The half-life is 15 hours.
:::

:::example The time to fall to a given fraction
Strontium-90 has a half-life of 29 years. How long does it take for the activity of a sample to fall to one sixteenth of its starting value?
@step One sixteenth is $\left(\dfrac{1}{2}\right)^{4}$, so 4 half-lives must pass.
@step $4 \times 29$.
@answer 116 years.
:::

### Any length of time

The decay equation also works when the time is not a whole number of half-lives. Work out $\dfrac{t}{T}$ first, and then raise 0.5 to that power with a calculator.

:::example A time between half-lives
Cobalt-60 has a half-life of 5.3 years. A sample contains 200 g of cobalt-60. How much is left after 12 years?
@step $\dfrac{t}{T} = \dfrac{12}{5.3} = 2.2642$.
@step $N = 200 \times \left(\dfrac{1}{2}\right)^{2.2642} = 200 \times 0.20817$.
@answer 41.63 g. This is between the 50 g left after two half-lives and the 25 g left after three, as it should be.
:::

:::example The amount at an earlier time
Iodine-131 has a half-life of 8 days. A sample now contains 25 mg of iodine-131. How much did it contain 20 days ago?
@step $\dfrac{t}{T} = \dfrac{20}{8} = 2.5$.
@step $25 = N_0 \times \left(\dfrac{1}{2}\right)^{2.5}$, so $N_0 = \dfrac{25}{0.1768}$.
@answer 141.4 mg.
:::

:::mistake Taking away the same amount each half-life
After two half-lives a sample has not all gone. Half is left after the first, and half of that half after the second, so one quarter remains.
:::

## Activity and radioactive dating

We cannot count the nuclei in a sample, but we can count the decays.

:::definition Activity
The activity of a source is the number of its nuclei that decay each second. It is measured in **becquerels**: 1 Bq is one decay each second.
:::

The activity is proportional to the number of nuclei that have not yet decayed. So the activity halves every half-life, in step with the number of nuclei, and the decay equation holds for it as well:

$$A = A_0 \left(\dfrac{1}{2}\right)^{t/T}$$

### Allowing for the background

A detector counts the background radiation along with the source. Subtract the background before you use the half-life, and add it back at the end if the question asks what the detector will read.

:::example A count rate with background
A detector near a source reads 500 counts per minute. With the source taken away it reads 20 counts per minute. The source has a half-life of 6 hours. What will the detector read, with the source in place, after 12 hours?
@step From the source alone: $500 - 20 = 480$ counts per minute.
@step After 2 half-lives: $480 \times \dfrac{1}{4} = 120$ counts per minute.
@step With the background: $120 + 20$.
@answer 140 counts per minute.
:::

### Carbon dating

Cosmic rays make a little carbon-14 in the upper air all the time. Plants take it in with the rest of their carbon, and animals eat the plants, so every living thing holds a small, steady share of carbon-14. When it dies, it stops taking in carbon, and the carbon-14 that it holds decays with a half-life of 5730 years. The less carbon-14 that is left, the longer ago it died.

:::example An age in whole half-lives
The carbon in living things gives 20.0 decays per minute for each gram. The carbon in a piece of wood from an old boat gives 2.5 decays per minute for each gram. Carbon-14 has a half-life of 5730 years. How old is the wood?
@step $\dfrac{20.0}{2.5} = 8 = 2^3$, so 3 half-lives have passed.
@step Age $= 3 \times 5730$.
@answer $17\,190$ years.
:::

When the ratio is not a power of 2, logarithms find the number of half-lives. If $\dfrac{A_0}{A} = 2^n$, then

$$n = \dfrac{\log (A_0 / A)}{\log 2}$$

:::example An age with logarithms
The carbon in living things gives 20.0 decays per minute for each gram. The carbon in the charcoal of an old campfire gives 12.50 decays per minute for each gram. Find the age of the charcoal, to the nearest 10 years.
@step Number of half-lives: $n = \dfrac{\log (20.0/12.50)}{\log 2} = \dfrac{\log 1.6}{\log 2} = 0.6781$.
@step Age $= 0.6781 \times 5730 = 3885$ years.
@answer About 3890 years.
:::

Carbon dating works for things that were once alive, up to about $50\,000$ years old. After that, too little carbon-14 is left to measure. Rocks are dated in the same way with isotopes of much longer half-life, such as uranium-238, with a half-life of 4.5 billion years.

### Half-life in medicine

A tracer for the body should have a half-life long enough to do its job and short enough that it is soon gone.

:::example The activity of a medical isotope
Technetium-99m has a half-life of 6 hours. A hospital receives a sample with an activity of 400 MBq. What is its activity 15 hours later?
@step $\dfrac{t}{T} = \dfrac{15}{6} = 2.5$.
@step $A = 400 \times \left(\dfrac{1}{2}\right)^{2.5} = 400 \times 0.1768$.
@answer 70.7 MBq.
:::

:::example A half-life with logarithms
The activity of a sample falls from 800 Bq to 300 Bq in 10 hours. Find the half-life of the isotope, to 1 decimal place.
@step Number of half-lives: $n = \dfrac{\log (800/300)}{\log 2} = 1.4150$.
@step $T = \dfrac{10}{1.4150}$.
@answer The half-life is 7.1 hours.
:::

:::mistake Forgetting the background
A reading of 500 counts per minute with a background of 20 means 480 from the source. Only the 480 halves with each half-life. The background stays the same.
:::

:::summary
- Decay is random for one nucleus, but a large sample follows a regular pattern.
- The half-life is the time for half of the nuclei in a sample to decay. It does not depend on temperature, pressure or chemical state.
- After $n$ half-lives the fraction left is $\left(\dfrac{1}{2}\right)^{n}$.
- For any time: $N = N_0 \left(\dfrac{1}{2}\right)^{t/T}$, where $T$ is the half-life.
- The activity of a source is the number of decays each second, in becquerels. It halves every half-life too.
- Subtract the background count before using the half-life.
- Carbon-14, with a half-life of 5730 years, dates things that were once alive. The number of half-lives is $\dfrac{\log (A_0/A)}{\log 2}$.
:::
