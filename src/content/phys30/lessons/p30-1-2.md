A cricketer who catches a fast ball draws the hands back as the ball arrives. A parachutist bends the knees on landing. A car has a bonnet that is built to crumple. All three do the same thing: they make a change in momentum take longer, so that the force is smaller. This lesson shows why that works.

## Impulse

Start from Newton's second law, $F = ma$, for a steady net force. The acceleration is the change in velocity divided by the time, so

$$F = \dfrac{m(v - u)}{\Delta t}$$

Multiply both sides by the time. The right side is then the change in momentum.

:::definition Impulse
The **impulse** of a force is the force multiplied by the time for which it acts. The impulse of the net force on an object equals the change in its momentum:

$$F\Delta t = \Delta p = m(v - u)$$

Impulse is measured in newton seconds (N·s). One newton second is the same as one kilogram metre per second, the unit of momentum.
:::

So a force changes momentum, and how much it changes it depends on two things: how large the force is, and how long it acts. A small force for a long time can give the same impulse as a large force for a short time.

:::example The force of a kick
A boot kicks a 0.43 kg soccer ball that is at rest. The ball leaves at 25 m/s, and the boot touches the ball for 10 ms. What is the average force on the ball?
@step Change in momentum: $\Delta p = m(v - u) = 0.43 \times (25 - 0) = 10.75$ kg·m/s.
@step Time: $\Delta t = 10\ \text{ms} = 0.010$ s.
@step $F = \dfrac{\Delta p}{\Delta t} = \dfrac{10.75}{0.010}$.
@answer $F = 1075$ N.
:::

When a ball is hit back the way it came, its velocity changes sign, as in the last lesson. The change in momentum is then large, and so is the force.

:::example A ball that is hit back
A 0.145 kg baseball moving at 40 m/s is hit straight back by a bat and leaves at 50 m/s. The contact lasts 2 ms. What is the size of the average force on the ball?
@step Take the direction in which the ball leaves as positive: $u = -40$ m/s and $v = +50$ m/s.
@step $\Delta p = m(v - u) = 0.145 \times (50 - (-40)) = 0.145 \times 90 = 13.05$ kg·m/s.
@step $F = \dfrac{\Delta p}{\Delta t} = \dfrac{13.05}{0.002}$.
@answer $F = 6525$ N.
:::

The same equation gives a time when you know the force.

:::example The time to stop
A 1200 kg car moving at 25 m/s brakes with a steady net force of 6000 N. How long does it take to stop?
@step The car must lose all of its momentum: $mv = 1200 \times 25 = 30\,000$ kg·m/s.
@step $\Delta t = \dfrac{\Delta p}{F} = \dfrac{30\,000}{6000}$.
@answer $\Delta t = 5$ s.
:::

### Making a stop safer

In a crash, a passenger must lose all of the momentum that the passenger had. Nothing can change that. What a designer can change is the time. From $F = \dfrac{\Delta p}{\Delta t}$, the same change in momentum over a longer time needs a smaller force.

:::example With and without an airbag
A 70 kg passenger moving at 15 m/s is brought to rest in a crash. An airbag makes the stop last 0.25 s. Without it, the passenger hits the dashboard and stops in 0.01 s. What is the average force on the passenger in each case?
@step The change in momentum is the same in both: $70 \times 15 = 1050$ kg·m/s.
@step With the airbag: $F = \dfrac{1050}{0.25} = 4200$ N.
@step Without it: $F = \dfrac{1050}{0.01} = 105\,000$ N.
@answer 4200 N with the airbag and 105 000 N without. The stop takes 25 times as long, so the force is 25 times smaller.
:::

| Safety design | How it lengthens the stop |
|---|---|
| airbag and seat belt | they give way a little, and slow the passenger over a longer time |
| crumple zone | the front of the car folds up, so the cabin slows more gently |
| helmet | the foam lining crushes, so the head takes longer to stop |
| bending your knees on landing | your body keeps moving down while your legs slow it |

The same idea works the other way in sport. A golfer or a tennis player "follows through" to keep the club or racket on the ball for as long as possible. The force acts for a longer time, so the impulse is greater and the ball leaves faster.

:::mistake Saying that an airbag reduces the impulse
The impulse on the passenger is the change in the passenger's momentum, and that is the same with or without an airbag. The airbag does not reduce the impulse. It spreads the same impulse over a longer time, and that is what reduces the force.
:::

## Force-time graphs

A real force is seldom steady. When a bat hits a ball, the force rises from zero as the ball is squashed, reaches a peak and falls back to zero as the ball leaves. A graph of force against time shows this.

For a steady force, the impulse $F\Delta t$ is the height of the graph multiplied by its width: the area of a rectangle. The same is true of any force.

:::definition Impulse from a graph
The impulse of a force is the **area under its force-time graph**. This area equals the change in momentum of the object.
:::

:::figure

:::

:::example The area of a triangle
The graph above shows the net force on a 15 kg cart that starts at rest. What impulse does the force give the cart, and how fast is the cart moving at the end?
@step The area is a triangle: $\dfrac{1}{2} \times \text{base} \times \text{height} = \dfrac{1}{2} \times 6 \times 30 = 90$ N·s.
@step The impulse equals the change in momentum: $90 = m(v - u) = 15 \times (v - 0)$.
@step $v = \dfrac{90}{15}$.
@answer The impulse is 90 N·s, and the cart moves at 6 m/s.
:::

Collisions are short, so their graphs often have a time axis in milliseconds. Change the time to seconds before you use the area.

:::example A racket and a ball
The graph above shows the force of a racket on a 0.058 kg tennis ball that starts at rest. Find the impulse on the ball, the speed at which it leaves, and the average force.
@step The area is a trapezoid. Its parallel sides are 6 ms and 2 ms long, and its height is 500 N.
@step Area $= \dfrac{1}{2} \times (6 + 2) \times 500 = 2000$ N·ms $= 2.0$ N·s.
@step Speed: $v = \dfrac{\text{impulse}}{m} = \dfrac{2.0}{0.058} = 34.5$ m/s.
@step Average force: the steady force that would give the same impulse in the same time, $\dfrac{2.0}{0.006} = 333$ N.
@answer The impulse is 2.0 N·s, the ball leaves at 34.5 m/s, and the average force is 333 N.
:::

The average force, 333 N, is less than the peak force of 500 N. When a question about a collision asks for "the force", it means this average.

:::mistake Reading the impulse off the height of the graph
The peak of a force-time graph is the greatest force, not the impulse. The impulse is the whole area under the graph, so a low, wide graph can give more impulse than a tall, narrow one.
:::

:::summary
- Impulse is force × time, in N·s. It equals the change in momentum: $F\Delta t = m(v - u)$.
- For the same change in momentum, a longer time means a smaller force. Airbags, crumple zones and helmets all lengthen the stop.
- The impulse is the area under a force-time graph.
- Change milliseconds to seconds before you calculate.
- The average force is the impulse ÷ the time.
:::
