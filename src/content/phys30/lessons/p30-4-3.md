Rutherford's atom had two problems. Its electrons should spiral into the nucleus, and it had nothing to say about the light that atoms give out. That light was a real puzzle. A glowing gas does not shine with every colour, as a hot wire does. It gives out a few sharp colours and nothing in between, and each element has its own set. In 1913 Niels Bohr joined Rutherford's nucleus to the photon of Unit 3 and explained the colours of hydrogen almost exactly.

## Energy levels

Bohr kept the nucleus and the orbiting electron, and added three bold assumptions.

- The electron can move only in certain **allowed orbits**. Each one has a fixed energy, called an **energy level**.
- While the electron stays in an allowed orbit, it does **not** radiate energy, so the atom is stable.
- The atom gives out or takes in energy only when the electron **jumps** from one level to another. The energy leaves or arrives as a single photon.

We say that the energy of the atom is **quantized**: the atom can have only certain energies, and none in between. The levels are numbered $n = 1, 2, 3, \ldots$ from the lowest upwards.

:::definition The energy levels of hydrogen
$$E_n = -\dfrac{13.6}{n^2}\ \text{eV}$$

- $E_n$ is the energy of the atom when its electron is in level $n$, in electronvolts;
- $n = 1$ is the **ground state**, the lowest level. The higher levels are **excited states**.
:::

| Level | Energy |
|---|---|
| $n = 1$ (ground state) | $-13.6$ eV |
| $n = 2$ | $-3.40$ eV |
| $n = 3$ | $-1.51$ eV |
| $n = 4$ | $-0.85$ eV |
| $n = 5$ | $-0.544$ eV |
| $n = \infty$ (electron free) | 0 |

The energies are **negative** because the electron is bound to the nucleus. Zero is the energy of an electron that has just been freed, and energy must be supplied to lift a bound electron up to zero. As $n$ increases the levels get closer together and approach zero.

:::example The energy of a level
Find the energy of a hydrogen atom with its electron in the level $n = 4$.
@step $E_4 = -\dfrac{13.6}{4^2} = -\dfrac{13.6}{16}$.
@answer $E_4 = -0.85$ eV.
:::

The energy needed to free the electron completely is the **ionization energy**. From the ground state of hydrogen it is 13.6 eV. An atom that has lost an electron is an **ion**.

:::example Ionizing an excited atom
How much energy is needed to free the electron of a hydrogen atom that is in the level $n = 2$?
@step The electron must be lifted from $E_2 = -3.40$ eV to 0.
@answer 3.40 eV.
:::

Bohr's model also gives the size of each orbit: $r_n = n^2 r_1$, where $r_1 = 5.29 \times 10^{-11}$ m is the radius of the smallest orbit.

:::example The radius of an orbit
Find the radius of the orbit of the electron in the level $n = 3$ of hydrogen.
@step $r_3 = 3^2 \times 5.29 \times 10^{-11}$.
@answer $r_3 = 4.76 \times 10^{-10}$ m, nine times the radius of the smallest orbit.
:::

### Jumps between levels

When the electron drops from a higher level to a lower one, the atom gives out one photon. Its energy is the difference between the two levels.

:::definition The photon of a transition
$$E_{\text{photon}} = E_{\text{higher}} - E_{\text{lower}} = hf = \dfrac{hc}{\lambda}$$

- $h = 6.63 \times 10^{-34}$ J·s and $c = 3.00 \times 10^{8}$ m/s;
- change the energy to joules before you find $f$ or $\lambda$: $1\ \text{eV} = 1.60 \times 10^{-19}$ J.
:::

The same rule works backwards. To lift the electron from a lower level to a higher one, the atom must **absorb** exactly the difference in energy.

:::example The red line of hydrogen
The electron of a hydrogen atom drops from $n = 3$ to $n = 2$. Find the energy of the photon, in electronvolts, and its wavelength.
@step $E = E_3 - E_2 = -1.51 - (-3.40) = 1.89$ eV.
@step In joules: $1.89 \times 1.60 \times 10^{-19} = 3.024 \times 10^{-19}$ J.
@step $\lambda = \dfrac{hc}{E} = \dfrac{6.63 \times 10^{-34} \times 3.00 \times 10^{8}}{3.024 \times 10^{-19}} = 6.58 \times 10^{-7}$ m.
@answer The photon has 1.89 eV and a wavelength of 658 nm, which is red light. This is the red line that hydrogen gives out.
:::

:::example A jump to the ground state
The electron of a hydrogen atom drops from $n = 2$ to $n = 1$. Find the energy of the photon and its wavelength.
@step $E = -3.40 - (-13.6) = 10.2$ eV, which is $1.63 \times 10^{-18}$ J.
@step $\lambda = \dfrac{6.63 \times 10^{-34} \times 3.00 \times 10^{8}}{1.63 \times 10^{-18}}$.
@answer 10.2 eV and 122 nm. This is ultraviolet, since the jump is much larger.
:::

:::example Energy and frequency
A hydrogen atom absorbs a photon, and its electron rises from $n = 2$ to $n = 4$. Find the energy of the photon, in joules, and its frequency.
@step $E = -0.85 - (-3.40) = 2.55$ eV.
@step In joules: $2.55 \times 1.60 \times 10^{-19} = 4.08 \times 10^{-19}$ J.
@step $f = \dfrac{E}{h} = \dfrac{4.08 \times 10^{-19}}{6.63 \times 10^{-34}}$.
@answer $E = 4.08 \times 10^{-19}$ J and $f = 6.15 \times 10^{14}$ Hz.
:::

:::example Finding the level
A hydrogen atom in its ground state absorbs a photon with an energy of 12.75 eV. Which level does the electron reach?
@step The new energy is $-13.6 + 12.75 = -0.85$ eV.
@step $-\dfrac{13.6}{n^2} = -0.85$, so $n^2 = \dfrac{13.6}{0.85} = 16$.
@answer $n = 4$.
:::

The same method works for any atom once its levels are known, even though the formula $-\dfrac{13.6}{n^2}$ belongs to hydrogen alone.

:::example Another atom
The four lowest energy levels of an atom are at $-10.4$, $-5.5$, $-3.7$ and $-1.6$ eV. An electron drops from the $-3.7$ eV level to the $-5.5$ eV level. Find the energy of the photon and its wavelength.
@step $E = -3.7 - (-5.5) = 1.8$ eV, which is $2.88 \times 10^{-19}$ J.
@step $\lambda = \dfrac{6.63 \times 10^{-34} \times 3.00 \times 10^{8}}{2.88 \times 10^{-19}} = 6.91 \times 10^{-7}$ m.
@answer 1.8 eV and 691 nm.
:::

### How many lines?

An electron in a high level can drop to the ground state in one step, or in several smaller steps. Each pair of levels gives one possible photon energy, so a gas whose atoms are excited to level $n$ can give out $\dfrac{n(n-1)}{2}$ different photon energies.

:::example Counting the lines
The atoms of a sample of hydrogen are excited to the level $n = 4$. How many different photon energies can the gas give out, and which is the largest?
@step The possible jumps are 4 to 3, 4 to 2, 4 to 1, 3 to 2, 3 to 1 and 2 to 1: $\dfrac{4 \times 3}{2} = 6$.
@step The largest is from $n = 4$ to $n = 1$: $-0.85 - (-13.6)$.
@answer 6 photon energies, and the largest is 12.75 eV.
:::

If the electron drops from $n = 4$ to $n = 2$ in one step, it gives out one photon of 2.55 eV. If it drops to $n = 3$ first and then to $n = 2$, it gives out two smaller photons, of 0.66 eV and 1.89 eV, which add up to the same 2.55 eV.

:::mistake Losing the minus signs
The energies of the levels are negative. Subtract with care: from $n = 3$ to $n = 2$ the photon has $-1.51 - (-3.40) = 1.89$ eV. The energy of a photon is always positive.
:::

## Spectra

A **spectrum** is the light of a source spread out by a prism or a diffraction grating, so that each wavelength falls in a different place. There are three kinds.

| Spectrum | Source | What is seen |
|---|---|---|
| **Continuous** | A hot solid, such as the filament of a lamp | Every colour, with no gaps |
| **Emission (bright-line)** | A glowing gas at low pressure, such as the neon in a sign | A few bright lines of certain colours on a dark background |
| **Absorption (dark-line)** | White light that has passed through a cool gas | A continuous spectrum crossed by dark lines |

### How the Bohr model explains them

In a glowing gas, collisions and the electric current lift electrons to higher levels. Each electron soon drops back, and gives out a photon whose energy is the gap between two levels. Only certain gaps exist, so only certain wavelengths appear. These are the **bright lines**.

When white light passes through a cool gas, an atom can absorb a photon only if its energy **exactly matches** a gap between two of its levels. A photon with a little less energy, or a little more, passes straight through. The matching photons are removed from the beam, and the excited atoms soon give the energy out again in all directions, so dark lines are left in the light that carries straight on.

The atoms absorb photons with exactly the energies that they can give out. This is why the dark lines of an element are at the same wavelengths as its bright lines.

Each element has its own set of energy levels, and so its own set of lines, like a fingerprint. This is how astronomers know which elements are in the outer layers of the Sun and the stars: they read the dark lines in the spectrum of the light.

### The series of hydrogen

The lines of hydrogen fall into groups, called series, according to the level where the electron ends.

| Series | The electron drops to | Part of the spectrum |
|---|---|---|
| Lyman | $n = 1$ | Ultraviolet |
| Balmer | $n = 2$ | Visible |
| Paschen | $n = 3$ | Infrared |

Jumps to the ground state are the largest, so they give ultraviolet photons. The visible lines are the Balmer series: red from $n = 3$, blue-green from $n = 4$, and violet from $n = 5$ and $n = 6$. A small jump, such as from $n = 4$ to $n = 3$, gives a photon of low energy and long wavelength.

Since the levels crowd together as $n$ gets larger, the lines of one series crowd closer together towards the short-wavelength end.

A photon with more than 13.6 eV can free the electron from the ground state altogether. The atom is ionized, and the electron carries off the extra energy as kinetic energy, just as in the photoelectric effect.

### Why the orbits are allowed

Bohr could not say why only some orbits are allowed. De Broglie's matter waves, from the last lesson of Unit 3, gave a reason. The electron in an orbit behaves as a wave, and the wave must join up with itself smoothly, so a **whole number of wavelengths** must fit round the orbit. It is a standing wave, like the ones on a string in Grade 11, bent into a circle. Orbits where the wave does not fit cancel themselves out.

### Where the model falls short

The Bohr model gives the right spectrum only for hydrogen and for ions with a single electron. It cannot predict the lines of atoms with two or more electrons, or explain why some lines are brighter than others. It was replaced in the 1920s by the **quantum mechanical model**, in which the electron is described by a wave that gives the chance of finding the electron at each place. The fixed orbits are gone, but the idea of energy levels, and of photons given out in jumps between them, remains.

:::mistake Expecting an atom to absorb any photon
An atom does not absorb a photon that falls between its levels. It absorbs only a photon whose energy matches a gap exactly, or one with enough energy to free the electron.
:::

:::summary
- Bohr's model: the electron of an atom can have only certain energies, and it does not radiate while it stays in one level.
- For hydrogen, $E_n = -\dfrac{13.6}{n^2}$ eV. The ground state is $n = 1$, and 13.6 eV is needed to ionize the atom from it.
- A jump between levels gives out or absorbs one photon: $E_{\text{photon}} = E_{\text{higher}} - E_{\text{lower}} = hf = \dfrac{hc}{\lambda}$.
- A hot solid gives a continuous spectrum, a glowing gas gives bright lines, and white light through a cool gas shows dark lines at the same wavelengths.
- Each element has its own lines, which is how the elements in the Sun and the stars are identified.
- Jumps to $n = 1$ in hydrogen give ultraviolet light, and jumps to $n = 2$, the Balmer series, give the visible lines.
- The model works only for atoms with one electron. Matter waves explain the allowed orbits, and the quantum mechanical model replaced them.
:::
