Drop a steel ball on a steel plate, and it bounces back almost to the height it fell from. Drop a ball of clay, and it stops dead with a thud. Momentum is conserved in both collisions. What differs is the kinetic energy. The steel ball keeps nearly all of its kinetic energy, and the clay loses all of it. This lesson sorts collisions by what happens to their kinetic energy.

## Kinetic energy in collisions

Recall that the kinetic energy of a moving object is $E_k = \dfrac{1}{2}mv^2$. It is a scalar, so the total kinetic energy of a system is the plain sum of the values for each object, with no signs for direction.

:::definition Elastic and inelastic collisions
- In an **elastic collision** the total kinetic energy is the same after the collision as before it.
- In an **inelastic collision** some kinetic energy is lost. It becomes thermal energy and sound, and it does the work of bending the objects.
- In a **completely inelastic collision** the objects stick together. This loses as much kinetic energy as the conservation of momentum allows.

The total momentum is conserved in all three.
:::

| Kind of collision | Momentum | Kinetic energy | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| elastic | conserved | conserved | two steel balls, two air molecules |
| inelastic | conserved | partly lost | a football bouncing on grass |
| completely inelastic | conserved | as much lost as possible | two cars that lock together |

Collisions between everyday objects are never perfectly elastic. Hard, springy objects such as billiard balls come close.

:::example The energy lost when carts stick
A 2 kg cart moving at 6 m/s hits a 4 kg cart at rest, and the two stick together. How much kinetic energy is lost?
@step Momentum gives the speed after the collision: $2 \times 6 = (2 + 4)v$, so $v = 2$ m/s.
@step Before: $E_k = \dfrac{1}{2} \times 2 \times 6^2 = 36$ J.
@step After: $E_k = \dfrac{1}{2} \times 6 \times 2^2 = 12$ J.
@answer $36 - 12 = 24$ J is lost. That is two thirds of the kinetic energy.
:::

To find out what kind of collision took place, work out the total kinetic energy before and after, and compare.

:::example Elastic or inelastic?
Ball A, of mass 1 kg, moves east at 8 m/s and hits ball B, of mass 3 kg, which is at rest. Just after the collision, ball A moves west at 4 m/s and ball B moves east at 4 m/s. Is the collision elastic?
@step Check the momentum first, with east as positive. Before: $1 \times 8 = 8$ kg·m/s. After: $1 \times (-4) + 3 \times 4 = 8$ kg·m/s. It is conserved, as it must be.
@step Kinetic energy before: $\dfrac{1}{2} \times 1 \times 8^2 = 32$ J.
@step Kinetic energy after: $\dfrac{1}{2} \times 1 \times 4^2 + \dfrac{1}{2} \times 3 \times 4^2 = 8 + 24 = 32$ J.
@answer The total kinetic energy is unchanged, so the collision is elastic.
:::

Notice that the velocity of ball A after the collision is negative, but its kinetic energy is not. Squaring the velocity removes the sign.

### The ballistic pendulum

A bullet is too fast to time with a stopwatch. A **ballistic pendulum** measures its speed with a ruler. The bullet is fired into a heavy block that hangs on strings, and the block swings up. The event has two stages, and each obeys a different law.

- **The impact.** The bullet stops inside the block. This is a completely inelastic collision: momentum is conserved, but most of the kinetic energy is lost.
- **The swing.** The block and bullet rise together. Now mechanical energy is conserved: their kinetic energy becomes gravitational potential energy, so $\dfrac{1}{2}v^2 = gh$.

:::example The speed of a bullet
A 10 g bullet is fired into a 2 kg block of wood that hangs at rest. The bullet stays in the block, and the block rises 5 cm. Use $g = 9.81\ \text{m/s}^2$. How fast was the bullet moving?
@step The swing gives the speed just after the impact: $v = \sqrt{2gh} = \sqrt{2 \times 9.81 \times 0.05} = 0.990$ m/s.
@step The impact conserves momentum: $0.010 \times u = (2 + 0.010) \times 0.990$.
@step $u = \dfrac{2.010 \times 0.990}{0.010}$.
@answer $u = 199$ m/s.
:::

The bullet arrives with about 198 J of kinetic energy, and the block and bullet leave with less than 1 J. More than 99% of the kinetic energy has become thermal energy in the wood.

:::mistake Using conservation of energy across the impact
It is tempting to say that the kinetic energy of the bullet becomes the potential energy of the block, and to skip the momentum step. That gives a bullet speed of about 14 m/s, which is far too slow, because it ignores the energy lost as the bullet ploughs into the wood. Use momentum for the impact, and energy only for the swing.
:::

## Elastic collisions

An elastic collision conserves two things at once: the total momentum and the total kinetic energy. Two equations are enough to find both velocities after the collision. For a head-on collision with a target that is at rest, solving the two equations together gives a pair of formulas.

:::definition Elastic head-on collision with a target at rest
An object of mass $m_1$ moving at a velocity $u$ makes an elastic head-on collision with an object of mass $m_2$ at rest. Their velocities just after the collision are

$$v_1 = \dfrac{m_1 - m_2}{m_1 + m_2}\,u \qquad v_2 = \dfrac{2m_1}{m_1 + m_2}\,u$$
:::

:::example A light ball hits a heavier one
A 2 kg ball moving east at 6 m/s makes an elastic head-on collision with a 6 kg ball at rest. Find the velocity of each ball just after the collision.
@step $v_1 = \dfrac{2 - 6}{2 + 6} \times 6 = \dfrac{-4}{8} \times 6 = -3$ m/s.
@step $v_2 = \dfrac{2 \times 2}{2 + 6} \times 6 = \dfrac{4}{8} \times 6 = +3$ m/s.
@answer The 2 kg ball bounces back west at 3 m/s, and the 6 kg ball moves east at 3 m/s.
:::

You can check the answer. Momentum: $2 \times 6 = 12$ before, and $2 \times (-3) + 6 \times 3 = 12$ after. Kinetic energy: 36 J before, and $9 + 27 = 36$ J after. The 6 kg ball has taken 27 J of the 36 J, which is 75% of the kinetic energy.

The formulas explain three cases that are worth remembering.

| The collision | What happens |
|---|---|
| equal masses | the moving object stops, and the target leaves with the velocity $u$ |
| a heavy object hits a light one at rest | the heavy object hardly slows, and the light one leaves at almost $2u$ |
| a light object hits a heavy one at rest | the light object bounces back at almost the speed it came in with, and the heavy one hardly moves |

The first case is the one you see on a billiard table, and in a row of hanging steel balls where one ball swings in and one swings out at the far end.

Elastic head-on collisions share one more property. The speed at which the two objects **move apart** after the collision equals the speed at which they **approached** each other before it. In the example above, the balls approached at 6 m/s, and they separate at $3 - (-3) = 6$ m/s. This is true even when both objects are moving before the collision.

:::example Both balls moving
Ball A, of mass 3 kg, moves east at 6 m/s and catches up with ball B, of mass 2 kg, which moves east at 1 m/s. The collision is elastic, and just after it ball A moves east at 2 m/s. Find the velocity of ball B, and check the speed of separation.
@step Before: $3 \times 6 + 2 \times 1 = 20$ kg·m/s.
@step After: ball A has $3 \times 2 = 6$ kg·m/s, so ball B has $20 - 6 = 14$ kg·m/s, and $v_B = \dfrac{14}{2} = 7$ m/s.
@step Speed of approach: $6 - 1 = 5$ m/s. Speed of separation: $7 - 2 = 5$ m/s.
@answer Ball B moves east at 7 m/s. The balls separate at 5 m/s, the speed at which they approached.
:::

:::mistake Using the elastic formulas for every collision
The two formulas work only for an elastic, head-on collision with a target at rest. If the objects stick together, or the question says that kinetic energy is lost, use the conservation of momentum alone.
:::

:::summary
- Momentum is conserved in every collision. Kinetic energy is conserved only in an elastic collision.
- In an inelastic collision kinetic energy becomes thermal energy, sound and the work of bending. Objects that stick together lose the most.
- To classify a collision, compare the total of $\dfrac{1}{2}mv^2$ before and after.
- Ballistic pendulum: momentum for the impact, energy for the swing.
- Elastic and head-on, target at rest: $v_1 = \dfrac{m_1 - m_2}{m_1 + m_2}\,u$ and $v_2 = \dfrac{2m_1}{m_1 + m_2}\,u$.
- In an elastic head-on collision, the speed of separation equals the speed of approach.
:::
