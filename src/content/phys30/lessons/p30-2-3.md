Two charges push or pull on each other without touching. How does one charge "know" that the other is there? Michael Faraday's answer was that a charge changes the space around it. It sets up an **electric field**, and any other charge that sits in the field feels a force. The same idea explained gravity in Grade 11: the Earth sets up a gravitational field, and a mass in that field has a weight.

## Electric field strength

To measure a gravitational field we put a mass in it and divide the force by the mass, which gives newtons per kilogram. An electric field is measured the same way, with a small positive **test charge**.

:::definition Electric field strength
The **electric field strength** at a point is the electric force on each coulomb of charge placed there:

$$E = \dfrac{F}{q}$$

It is measured in newtons per coulomb (N/C). The field is a vector. Its direction is the direction of the force on a **positive** test charge.
:::

:::example Measuring a field
A small test charge of +2.0 μC feels a force of 0.030 N towards the east at a point in an electric field. What is the field there?
@step $E = \dfrac{F}{q} = \dfrac{0.030}{2.0 \times 10^{-6}}$.
@answer $E = 15\,000$ N/C, towards the east.
:::

Once the field strength at a point is known, the force on any charge placed there is $F = qE$. A positive charge is pushed along the field. A negative charge is pushed the opposite way.

:::example The force on an electron
An electron, with a charge of $-1.60 \times 10^{-19}$ C, is in an electric field of 2500 N/C that points north. Find the force on it.
@step $F = qE = 1.60 \times 10^{-19} \times 2500 = 4.0 \times 10^{-16}$ N.
@step The electron is negative, so the force is opposite to the field.
@answer $4.0 \times 10^{-16}$ N, towards the south.
:::

### The field of a point charge

Put a test charge $q$ at a distance $r$ from a point charge $Q$. Coulomb's law gives the force as $F = \dfrac{kQq}{r^2}$. Dividing by the test charge leaves the field of the charge $Q$ alone:

$$E = \dfrac{kQ}{r^2}$$

The test charge has cancelled. The field belongs to the charge that makes it, and it is there whether or not anything is placed in it. The field points away from a positive charge and towards a negative one, and it follows an inverse square law.

:::example The field near a charged sphere
Find the electric field 0.50 m from a small sphere that carries a charge of +4.0 μC.
@step $E = \dfrac{kQ}{r^2} = \dfrac{8.99 \times 10^{9} \times 4.0 \times 10^{-6}}{0.50^2}$.
@step $E = \dfrac{35\,960}{0.25} = 143\,840$ N/C.
@answer $E = 1.44 \times 10^{5}$ N/C, pointing away from the sphere.
:::

:::mistake Using the test charge in the field of a point charge
In $E = \dfrac{kQ}{r^2}$, $Q$ is the charge that **makes** the field. In $E = \dfrac{F}{q}$ and $F = qE$, $q$ is the charge that **feels** the field. Keep the two apart.
:::

The rules for field lines are these.

- They point away from positive charges and towards negative charges.
- Where the lines are close together the field is strong, and where they are far apart it is weak.
- They never cross, because the field at a point has only one direction.
- Between two large parallel plates with opposite charges, the lines are straight, parallel and evenly spaced. The field there is **uniform**: it has the same strength and direction everywhere.

### Adding fields

Each charge makes its own field, as if the others were not there. The field at a point is the vector sum of the fields of all the charges.

:::example The field halfway between two charges
Charge A (+6.0 μC) and charge B (+2.0 μC) are 0.60 m apart. Find the electric field at the point halfway between them.
@step The midpoint is 0.30 m from each charge.
@step Field of A: $\dfrac{8.99 \times 10^{9} \times 6.0 \times 10^{-6}}{0.30^2} = 5.99 \times 10^{5}$ N/C. A is positive, so its field points away from A, towards B.
@step Field of B: $\dfrac{8.99 \times 10^{9} \times 2.0 \times 10^{-6}}{0.30^2} = 2.00 \times 10^{5}$ N/C. B is positive, so its field points away from B, towards A.
@step The fields are opposite, so subtract: $5.99 \times 10^{5} - 2.00 \times 10^{5} = 4.00 \times 10^{5}$ N/C, in the direction of the larger one.
@answer $4.00 \times 10^{5}$ N/C, towards charge B.
:::

If charge B were negative instead, its field at the midpoint would also point towards B, and the two fields would add to $7.99 \times 10^{5}$ N/C.

Between two like charges there is always one point where their fields cancel exactly. It lies nearer the smaller charge.

:::example Where the field is zero
Charge A (+2.0 μC) and charge B (+8.0 μC) are 0.90 m apart. Where on the line between them is the electric field zero?
@step Let the point be a distance $x$ from A. It is then $0.90 - x$ from B.
@step The fields are equal in size there: $\dfrac{k \times 2.0}{x^2} = \dfrac{k \times 8.0}{(0.90 - x)^2}$.
@step Take the square root of both sides: $\dfrac{0.90 - x}{x} = \sqrt{\dfrac{8.0}{2.0}} = 2$.
@step $0.90 - x = 2x$, so $3x = 0.90$.
@answer The field is zero 0.30 m from A, which is 0.60 m from B.
:::

When the fields of two charges are at right angles, add them as you added forces at right angles in the last lesson: the Pythagorean theorem gives the size, and the tangent gives the direction.

:::mistake Giving a negative charge a field that points away from it
The field shows the force on a positive test charge. A negative charge attracts a positive test charge, so its field points **towards** it.
:::

:::summary
- Electric field strength is the force on each coulomb: $E = \dfrac{F}{q}$, in N/C. Its direction is that of the force on a positive charge.
- The force on a charge in a field is $F = qE$: along the field for a positive charge, and against it for a negative one.
- The field of a point charge is $E = \dfrac{kQ}{r^2}$, away from a positive charge and towards a negative one.
- Field lines never cross, and they are closest together where the field is strongest.
- The field between parallel charged plates is uniform.
- The fields of several charges add as vectors.
:::
