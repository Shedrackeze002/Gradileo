The light from this screen, the signal that brought the page to your phone, the warmth of the Sun on your face and the X-rays a dentist uses are all the same kind of thing. Each is an **electromagnetic wave**, and they differ only in their wavelength. This unit is about what these waves are, how they behave, and the surprise that waited for physicists when they looked at light very closely.

## Electromagnetic waves

In Unit 2 you met two facts. A changing magnetic field makes an electric field: that is induction. And a current, which is moving charge, makes a magnetic field. In the 1860s James Clerk Maxwell put these together. He showed that a changing electric field also makes a magnetic field, so the two can keep each other going:

- a changing electric field makes a changing magnetic field,
- which makes a changing electric field, and so on.

The result is a wave of electric and magnetic fields that travels away from its source. The two fields are at right angles to each other and to the direction of travel, so the wave is **transverse**. It is not a vibration of any material. The fields themselves are what waves, so an electromagnetic wave can cross empty space.

:::definition Electromagnetic waves
An **electromagnetic wave** is made of a changing electric field and a changing magnetic field, at right angles to each other and to the direction in which the wave travels. It is produced by an electric charge that **accelerates**.
:::

A charge at rest has a steady electric field, and a charge that moves steadily has steady fields too. Neither sends out a wave. Only a charge that speeds up, slows down or changes direction radiates. In the aerial of a radio transmitter, an alternating current drives electrons up and down, and the frequency of the wave is the frequency of the current.

Maxwell worked out the speed of his waves from electric and magnetic constants alone, and it came to $3.00 \times 10^{8}$ m/s. This was the measured speed of light. He concluded that light is an electromagnetic wave. In 1887 Heinrich Hertz made the first electromagnetic waves in a laboratory, with a spark, and detected them across the room. They were radio waves.

| Visible light | 400 nm to 700 nm | Very hot objects, lamps and lasers | Seeing |
| Ultraviolet | 10 nm to 400 nm | The Sun, very hot objects | Killing germs |
| X-rays | 0.01 nm to 10 nm | Fast electrons stopped by a metal | Images of bones |
| Gamma rays | Less than 0.01 nm | The nuclei of radioactive atoms | Treating cancer |

Visible light is a narrow band in the middle, from red, at about 700 nm, to violet, at about 400 nm. Infrared means "below red" and ultraviolet means "beyond violet".

All of these waves travel through a vacuum at the same speed. Since they differ in wavelength, they differ in frequency: the shorter the wavelength, the higher the frequency. The high-frequency end of the spectrum, from ultraviolet to gamma rays, carries enough energy to knock electrons out of atoms. This **ionizing** radiation can damage living cells.

Light can also be **polarized**. A polarizing filter, like the lens of some sunglasses, passes only vibrations in one direction. Only a transverse wave has a direction of vibration across its path, so polarization is evidence that light is transverse.

Radio waves, light and gamma rays all travel through a vacuum at $3.00 \times 10^{8}$ m/s. They differ in wavelength and frequency, not in speed.

## The speed of light

The speed of light in a vacuum has its own symbol, $c$:

$$c = 3.00 \times 10^{8}\ \text{m/s}$$

The universal wave equation of Grade 11, $v = f\lambda$, holds for electromagnetic waves, with $c$ as the speed.

:::definition The wave equation for light
$$c = f\lambda$$

- $f$ is the frequency, in hertz;
- $\lambda$ is the wavelength, in metres.
:::

:::example The wavelength of a radio wave
A radio station broadcasts at a frequency of 98.5 MHz. Find the wavelength of its waves.
@step Change to hertz: $f = 98.5 \times 10^{6}$ Hz.
@step $\lambda = \dfrac{c}{f} = \dfrac{3.00 \times 10^{8}}{98.5 \times 10^{6}}$.
@answer $\lambda = 3.05$ m.
:::

:::example The frequency of green light
A beam of green light has a wavelength of 550 nm. Find its frequency.
@step Change to metres: $\lambda = 550 \times 10^{-9}$ m.
@step $f = \dfrac{c}{\lambda} = \dfrac{3.00 \times 10^{8}}{550 \times 10^{-9}}$.
@answer $f = 5.45 \times 10^{14}$ Hz. The fields of the wave change direction more than a million billion times each second.
:::

### Light takes time to travel

Light is so fast that it seems to arrive at once, but over large distances the delay is easy to notice.

:::example Light from the Sun
The Sun is $1.50 \times 10^{11}$ m from the Earth. How long does its light take to reach us?
@step $t = \dfrac{d}{c} = \dfrac{1.50 \times 10^{11}}{3.00 \times 10^{8}}$.
@answer $t = 500$ s, which is 8 minutes and 20 seconds. We see the Sun as it was 8 minutes ago.
:::

Radar measures distance in this way. A pulse of microwaves is sent out, and the time until its echo returns is measured. The pulse travels there and back, so the distance to the object is half of the distance the pulse covers.

:::example A radar echo
A radar pulse returns to its station 400 μs after it was sent. How far away is the object that reflected it?
@step The pulse travels $ct = 3.00 \times 10^{8} \times 400 \times 10^{-6} = 120\,000$ m.
@step That is the distance there and back, so halve it.
@answer The object is 60 000 m away, which is 60 km.
:::

### Measuring the speed of light

To measure $c$ on the Earth, a very short time must be measured. In the 1920s Albert Michelson did it with a rotating mirror that had eight faces. Light reflected from one face, travelled to a fixed mirror on a mountain 35 km away, and came back. If the mirror was at rest, the returning light reflected from another face into the detector. When the mirror was spinning, the faces had moved by the time the light returned, and the light missed the detector.

Michelson slowly raised the speed of the mirror. The light appeared in the detector again when the mirror turned exactly one eighth of a revolution during the round trip, so that the next face stood where the first had been. The time for the round trip was then one eighth of the time for one revolution.

:::example A rotating-mirror measurement
In an experiment like Michelson's, the mirror has 8 faces and the fixed mirror is 35 km away. The lowest frequency of rotation at which light reaches the detector is 536 revolutions per second. Find the speed of light.
@step One revolution takes $\dfrac{1}{536}$ s, so one eighth of a revolution takes $t = \dfrac{1}{8 \times 536}$ s.
@step In that time the light travels there and back: $2d = 70\,000$ m.
@step $c = \dfrac{2d}{t} = 70\,000 \times 8 \times 536$.
@answer $c = 3.00 \times 10^{8}$ m/s.
:::

:::mistake Forgetting that an echo travels there and back
A radar pulse, like an echo of sound, covers twice the distance to the object. Halve the distance, or halve the time, before you give the answer.
:::

:::summary
- An electromagnetic wave is a changing electric field and a changing magnetic field, at right angles to each other and to the direction of travel. It is transverse and needs no material.
- Electromagnetic waves are produced by charges that accelerate.
- The spectrum, from the longest wavelength: radio waves, microwaves, infrared, visible light, ultraviolet, X-rays, gamma rays.
- All electromagnetic waves travel through a vacuum at $c = 3.00 \times 10^{8}$ m/s, and $c = f\lambda$.
- Visible light runs from red, at about 700 nm, to violet, at about 400 nm.
- The speed of light can be measured by timing a round trip, as Michelson did with a rotating mirror.
:::
