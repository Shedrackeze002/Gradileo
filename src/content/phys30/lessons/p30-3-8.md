This unit has reached an odd place. Young's fringes prove that light is a wave. The photoelectric effect and the Compton effect prove that light is a stream of particles. Both sets of experiments are sound. In 1924 a French student, Louis de Broglie, made the puzzle deeper with a bold guess: if waves can behave as particles, then perhaps particles, such as electrons, can behave as waves.

## Matter waves

De Broglie started from the momentum of a photon, $p = \dfrac{h}{\lambda}$. Turned round, it gives the wavelength of a photon from its momentum. He proposed that the same equation gives a wavelength to **any** moving particle.

:::definition The de Broglie wavelength
$$\lambda = \dfrac{h}{p} = \dfrac{h}{mv}$$

- $\lambda$ is the wavelength of the particle;
- $h$ is Planck's constant, $6.63 \times 10^{-34}$ J·s;
- $p$ is the momentum of the particle, which is its mass $m$ times its speed $v$.
:::

The greater the momentum, the shorter the wavelength. An electron that moves faster has a shorter wavelength. A proton has about 1800 times the mass of an electron, so at the same speed its wavelength is about 1800 times shorter.

:::example The wavelength of an electron
Find the de Broglie wavelength of an electron that moves at $2.0 \times 10^{6}$ m/s. The mass of an electron is $9.11 \times 10^{-31}$ kg.
@step $\lambda = \dfrac{h}{mv} = \dfrac{6.63 \times 10^{-34}}{9.11 \times 10^{-31} \times 2.0 \times 10^{6}}$.
@answer $\lambda = 3.64 \times 10^{-10}$ m. This is about the size of an atom, and about the wavelength of X-rays.
:::

:::example The wavelength of a baseball
Find the de Broglie wavelength of a baseball with a mass of 0.145 kg that moves at 40 m/s.
@step $\lambda = \dfrac{h}{mv} = \dfrac{6.63 \times 10^{-34}}{0.145 \times 40}$.
@answer $\lambda = 1.14 \times 10^{-34}$ m. This is far smaller than the nucleus of an atom, which is about $10^{-15}$ m across.
:::

The baseball shows why we never notice the wave behaviour of everyday objects. A wave only diffracts noticeably at a gap about as wide as its wavelength, and nothing has a gap of $10^{-34}$ m. The wavelength of an electron is another matter: it is close to the spacing of the atoms in a crystal.

:::example The speed for a given wavelength
At what speed does an electron have a de Broglie wavelength of 0.50 nm?
@step $\lambda = \dfrac{h}{mv}$, so $v = \dfrac{h}{m\lambda} = \dfrac{6.63 \times 10^{-34}}{9.11 \times 10^{-31} \times 0.50 \times 10^{-9}}$.
@answer $v = 1.46 \times 10^{6}$ m/s.
:::

### Electrons that have been accelerated

In Unit 2, an electron that is accelerated from rest through a potential difference $V$ gains a kinetic energy of $eV$. Find its speed from $eV = \dfrac{1}{2}mv^2$, and then its wavelength.

:::example An electron accelerated through 150 V
An electron is accelerated from rest through a potential difference of 150 V. Find its speed and its de Broglie wavelength. The charge of an electron has a size of $1.60 \times 10^{-19}$ C.
@step $eV = \dfrac{1}{2}mv^2$, so $v = \sqrt{\dfrac{2eV}{m}} = \sqrt{\dfrac{2 \times 1.60 \times 10^{-19} \times 150}{9.11 \times 10^{-31}}} = 7.26 \times 10^{6}$ m/s.
@step $\lambda = \dfrac{h}{mv} = \dfrac{6.63 \times 10^{-34}}{9.11 \times 10^{-31} \times 7.26 \times 10^{6}}$.
@answer The speed is $7.26 \times 10^{6}$ m/s and the wavelength is $1.00 \times 10^{-10}$ m.
:::

When the kinetic energy is given, the momentum can be found in one step. Since $E_k = \dfrac{1}{2}mv^2$ and $p = mv$, we have $E_k = \dfrac{p^2}{2m}$, so $p = \sqrt{2mE_k}$.

:::example From kinetic energy to wavelength
An electron has a kinetic energy of 54 eV. Find its momentum and its de Broglie wavelength.
@step In joules: $E_k = 54 \times 1.60 \times 10^{-19} = 8.64 \times 10^{-18}$ J.
@step $p = \sqrt{2mE_k} = \sqrt{2 \times 9.11 \times 10^{-31} \times 8.64 \times 10^{-18}} = 3.97 \times 10^{-24}$ kg·m/s.
@step $\lambda = \dfrac{h}{p} = \dfrac{6.63 \times 10^{-34}}{3.97 \times 10^{-24}}$.
@answer The momentum is $3.97 \times 10^{-24}$ kg·m/s and the wavelength is $1.67 \times 10^{-10}$ m.
:::

:::mistake Using the speed of light for a particle
$c = f\lambda$ and $E = \dfrac{hc}{\lambda}$ are for photons. An electron does not travel at $c$. For a particle with mass, use $\lambda = \dfrac{h}{mv}$ with its own speed.
:::

## Wave-particle duality

### Electrons diffract

De Broglie's idea was a guess with no evidence. Three years later the evidence came. In 1927 Clinton Davisson and Lester Germer fired a beam of electrons with a kinetic energy of 54 eV at a crystal of nickel. The rows of atoms in the crystal acted as a diffraction grating, and the electrons came off strongly at certain angles and hardly at all between them. This is a diffraction pattern, and only waves diffract. The wavelength worked out from the pattern was $1.67 \times 10^{-10}$ m, the value that de Broglie's equation gives in the example above.

In the same year George Thomson passed electrons through a thin metal foil and photographed rings, just like the rings that X-rays make. If the electrons are accelerated through a larger potential difference, they move faster, their wavelength is shorter, and the rings shrink.

A crystal works where a doorway does not, since the spacing of its atoms is about the same as the wavelength of the electrons.

An **electron microscope** puts this to use. No microscope can show detail much finer than the wavelength it uses. Fast electrons have a wavelength thousands of times shorter than that of visible light, so an electron microscope can show viruses and even single atoms.

### Two sets of evidence

| Experiment | What it shows |
|---|---|
| Young's double slit, diffraction gratings | Light behaves as a wave |
| Polarization | Light is a transverse wave |
| The photoelectric effect | Light delivers energy as particles |
| The Compton effect | Light carries momentum as particles |
| Electron beams bent by electric and magnetic fields, Millikan's oil drops | The electron behaves as a particle, with mass and charge |
| Davisson and Germer, Thomson's rings | The electron behaves as a wave |

Neither picture is enough alone. Light and matter both show wave behaviour in some experiments and particle behaviour in others. This is **wave-particle duality**.

As a rule, light behaves as a wave while it **travels**: it diffracts, interferes and refracts. It behaves as a particle when it **gives up or takes in energy**: a whole photon is absorbed or emitted at one place. The particle side shows most clearly at high frequencies, where each photon has a large energy.

A photon and an electron that have the same wavelength have the same momentum, since $p = \dfrac{h}{\lambda}$ for both. Fired at the same crystal, they give diffraction patterns with the same spacing. They do not have the same speed or the same energy.

### One at a time

The strangest experiment sends light through a double slit so dimly that only one photon is in the apparatus at a time. Each photon arrives at the detector as a single dot, at one place, as a particle would. The dots seem scattered at random. Yet as thousands of dots build up, they form the bright and dark fringes of an interference pattern. Electrons sent one at a time through two very narrow slits do exactly the same.

No single photon is split between the slits, and there is no second photon for it to interfere with. The modern view is that the **wave** describes the **chance** of finding the particle at each place. Where the wave is strong, at a bright fringe, many photons land. Where the waves cancel, at a dark fringe, none do. The wave tells us where the particles are likely to go, and the particle is what is detected.

This idea is the starting point of **quantum mechanics**, the theory of atoms that you will meet in Unit 4.

:::mistake Thinking that light is sometimes a wave and sometimes a particle
Light does not switch from one thing to another. It is a single kind of thing that does not fit either of our everyday pictures. Which behaviour we see depends on the experiment we do.
:::

:::summary
- De Broglie proposed that every moving particle has a wavelength: $\lambda = \dfrac{h}{p} = \dfrac{h}{mv}$.
- The wavelength of an everyday object is far too small to detect. The wavelength of an electron is about the size of an atom.
- Davisson and Germer, and Thomson, showed that electrons diffract from crystals, which confirmed de Broglie's equation.
- An electron microscope shows fine detail because fast electrons have very short wavelengths.
- Light and matter both show wave behaviour and particle behaviour: this is wave-particle duality.
- With one photon or one electron at a time, single dots build up into an interference pattern. The wave gives the chance of finding the particle at each place.
:::
