Two cars meet at a crossroads, one driving east and one driving north. They collide, lock together and slide off towards the north-east. An accident investigator measures the skid marks and works backwards to the speeds of the two cars. The tool for this is the conservation of momentum, used once for each direction.

## Momentum at right angles

Momentum is a vector. So far the vectors have pointed along one line, and a plus or minus sign was enough. In two dimensions we treat momentum as we treated displacement and velocity in Grade 11: we split it into **components**.

:::definition Conservation of momentum in two dimensions
The total momentum of an isolated system is conserved **in each direction separately**:

- the total east-west momentum is the same after a collision as before it;
- the total north-south momentum is the same after a collision as before it.
:::

A momentum $p$ in a direction $\theta$ north of east has an east component $p\cos\theta$ and a north component $p\sin\theta$.

:::example The components of a momentum
A 0.17 kg hockey puck moves at 20 m/s in a direction 30° north of east. Find the east and north components of its momentum.
@step $p = mv = 0.17 \times 20 = 3.4$ kg·m/s.
@step East: $3.4\cos 30° = 2.94$ kg·m/s.
@step North: $3.4\sin 30° = 1.70$ kg·m/s.
@answer 2.94 kg·m/s east and 1.70 kg·m/s north.
:::

Going the other way, two components at right angles give the size of the total with the Pythagorean theorem, and its direction with the tangent.

Now return to the crossroads. Before the collision, one car carries all of the east momentum and the other carries all of the north momentum. After it, the wreck carries both.

:::example A crash at a crossroads
A 1200 kg car moving east at 20 m/s collides with an 1800 kg car moving north at 10 m/s. The cars lock together. Find their speed and direction just after the collision.
@step East momentum: $1200 \times 20 = 24\,000$ kg·m/s. North momentum: $1800 \times 10 = 18\,000$ kg·m/s.
@step Total: $p = \sqrt{24\,000^2 + 18\,000^2} = 30\,000$ kg·m/s.
@step The wreck has a mass of $1200 + 1800 = 3000$ kg, so $v = \dfrac{30\,000}{3000} = 10$ m/s.
@step Direction: $\theta = \tan^{-1}\left(\dfrac{18\,000}{24\,000}\right) = 36.9°$.
@answer 10 m/s, in a direction 36.9° north of east.
:::

An investigator usually has the problem the other way round: the direction of the skid is known, and a speed before the collision is not.

:::example Working back from the skid marks
A 1500 kg car moving east at 12 m/s collides with a 1000 kg car moving north. The cars lock together and slide off in a direction 40° north of east. How fast was the northbound car moving?
@step East momentum: $1500 \times 12 = 18\,000$ kg·m/s. The wreck keeps all of it.
@step In the triangle of momenta, $\tan 40° = \dfrac{\text{north momentum}}{\text{east momentum}}$, so the north momentum is $18\,000 \times \tan 40° = 15\,100$ kg·m/s.
@step All of the north momentum came from the northbound car: $v = \dfrac{15\,100}{1000}$.
@answer The northbound car was moving at 15.1 m/s.
:::

Kinetic energy is not a vector, so it is never split into components. When two objects stick together after a collision at right angles, find the kinetic energy lost exactly as in the last lesson: add up $\dfrac{1}{2}mv^2$ before, and subtract $\dfrac{1}{2}mv^2$ of the combined object after.

:::mistake Adding momenta at right angles as plain numbers
In the crash above, $24\,000 + 18\,000 = 42\,000$ kg·m/s is not the total momentum. The two momenta are at right angles, so they add like the two shorter sides of a right triangle: the total is 30 000 kg·m/s.
:::

## Glancing collisions and explosions

Most collisions are not head-on. A billiard ball that clips the edge of another is turned to one side, and the ball it hit goes off to the other side. This is a **glancing collision**.

Suppose ball A moves east and strikes ball B, which is at rest. Before the collision there is no north-south momentum at all. So after it, the north momentum of one ball must cancel the south momentum of the other. If ball A goes off north of east, ball B must go off south of east.

Work through a glancing collision in these steps.

1. Resolve each known momentum into east and north components.
2. East: the components after the collision add up to the east momentum before it.
3. North: the components after the collision add up to the north momentum before it, which is often zero.
4. Put the two components of the unknown momentum together, with the Pythagorean theorem and the tangent.

:::example A glancing collision
Ball A, of mass 0.5 kg, moves east at 4 m/s and strikes ball B, of mass 0.5 kg, which is at rest. After the collision, ball A moves at 3 m/s in a direction 30° north of east. Find the speed and direction of ball B.
@step Before: $0.5 \times 4 = 2.0$ kg·m/s east, and nothing north.
@step Ball A after: east $0.5 \times 3 \times \cos 30° = 1.299$ kg·m/s, north $0.5 \times 3 \times \sin 30° = 0.750$ kg·m/s.
@step Ball B, east: $2.0 - 1.299 = 0.701$ kg·m/s. Ball B, north: $0 - 0.750 = -0.750$ kg·m/s, which is 0.750 kg·m/s south.
@step $p_B = \sqrt{0.701^2 + 0.750^2} = 1.027$ kg·m/s, so $v_B = \dfrac{1.027}{0.5} = 2.05$ m/s.
@step $\theta = \tan^{-1}\left(\dfrac{0.750}{0.701}\right) = 46.9°$.
@answer Ball B moves at 2.05 m/s, in a direction 46.9° south of east.
:::

Was this collision elastic? Before it, the kinetic energy was $\dfrac{1}{2} \times 0.5 \times 4^2 = 4.0$ J. After it, the total is $\dfrac{1}{2} \times 0.5 \times 3^2 + \dfrac{1}{2} \times 0.5 \times 2.05^2 = 2.25 + 1.05 = 3.30$ J. So 0.70 J was lost, and the collision was inelastic. When two objects of equal mass do collide elastically, with one at rest and not head-on, their paths after the collision are always at 90° to each other.

Explosions follow the same rule. If an object at rest breaks into pieces, the momenta of all the pieces add to zero, in each direction.

:::example An explosion into three pieces
A shell at rest explodes into three pieces. A 2 kg piece moves east at 6 m/s, and a 3 kg piece moves north at 3 m/s. The third piece has a mass of 1.5 kg. Find its speed and direction.
@step East: $2 \times 6 = 12$ kg·m/s. North: $3 \times 3 = 9$ kg·m/s.
@step The total must stay zero, so the third piece has 12 kg·m/s west and 9 kg·m/s south.
@step $p = \sqrt{12^2 + 9^2} = 15$ kg·m/s, so $v = \dfrac{15}{1.5} = 10$ m/s.
@step $\theta = \tan^{-1}\left(\dfrac{9}{12}\right) = 36.9°$.
@answer The third piece moves at 10 m/s, in a direction 36.9° south of west.
:::

:::mistake Conserving only the momentum along the first direction
In a glancing collision it is easy to balance the east momentum and forget the north. Both must balance. The north-south momentum was zero before the collision, so the two objects must leave on opposite sides of the original path.
:::

:::summary
- Momentum is conserved in each direction separately: balance the east-west momentum and the north-south momentum.
- A momentum $p$ at an angle $\theta$ north of east has components $p\cos\theta$ east and $p\sin\theta$ north.
- Momenta at right angles add with the Pythagorean theorem, and the tangent gives the direction.
- In a glancing collision with a target at rest, the two objects leave on opposite sides of the original path.
- The pieces of an explosion from rest have momenta that add to zero.
- Kinetic energy has no direction: never split it into components.
:::
