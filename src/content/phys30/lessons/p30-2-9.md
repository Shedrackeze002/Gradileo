A current is a stream of moving charges, and a magnetic field pushes on moving charges. So a wire that carries a current across a magnetic field is pushed sideways. This is the **motor effect**. It turns every electric motor, from the one that spins a phone's vibration alert to the ones that drive an electric train.

## The force on a current-carrying wire

The field pushes on each of the moving charges in the wire, and the charges pass the push on to the wire itself. The total force depends on the field, the current and the length of wire that is in the field.

:::definition The magnetic force on a wire
A straight wire of length $L$ that carries a current $I$ at right angles to a magnetic field $B$ feels a force

$$F = BIL$$

If the wire makes an angle $\theta$ with the field,

$$F = BIL\sin\theta$$

The field is in teslas, the current in amperes and the length in metres.
:::

The force is greatest when the wire is at right angles to the field, and zero when the wire lies along the field.

:::example The force on a wire
A straight wire 0.50 m long carries a current of 4.0 A at right angles to a magnetic field of 0.30 T. Find the magnetic force on it.
@step $F = BIL = 0.30 \times 4.0 \times 0.50$.
@answer $F = 0.60$ N.
:::

:::example A wire at an angle
The same wire is turned so that it makes an angle of 30° with the field. Find the force now.
@step $F = BIL\sin\theta = 0.60 \times \sin 30°$.
@answer $F = 0.30$ N.
:::

The formula can be rearranged for any one of its quantities. A 0.25 m wire that feels a force of 0.15 N when it carries 3.0 A across a field is in a field of $B = \dfrac{F}{IL} = \dfrac{0.15}{3.0 \times 0.25} = 0.20$ T.

### The direction of the force

The right-hand rule of the last lesson still works, because conventional current is the direction in which positive charge moves.

:::definition The right-hand rule for the force on a wire
Hold the right hand flat. Point the thumb along the conventional current and the fingers along the magnetic field. The palm pushes in the direction of the force.
:::

A wire that carries a current east through a field that points north is pushed straight up. Reverse the current, or reverse the field, and the force reverses. Reverse both, and the force stays as it was.

### Balancing a force against a weight

A large enough current can make the magnetic force on a horizontal wire equal to its weight, so that the wire floats.

:::example A wire that floats
A straight horizontal wire has a mass of 20 g and a length of 0.50 m. It lies at right angles to a horizontal magnetic field of 0.40 T. What current makes the magnetic force on the wire just balance its weight? Use $g = 9.81$ m/s².
@step Change to kilograms: $m = 0.020$ kg.
@step For balance, $BIL = mg$, so $I = \dfrac{mg}{BL}$.
@step $I = \dfrac{0.020 \times 9.81}{0.40 \times 0.50} = \dfrac{0.1962}{0.20}$.
@answer $I = 0.98$ A.
:::

The same balance measures a magnetic field. Stand a magnet on an electronic balance and hold a wire, fixed in place, in the field between its poles. Switch on a current so that the field pushes the wire **up**. By Newton's third law the wire pushes the magnet **down** with an equal force, and the reading of the balance rises. The extra reading gives the force, and the force gives the field.

:::example Measuring a field with a balance
A magnet sits on an electronic balance. A horizontal wire is held between its poles, at right angles to the field, with 10 cm of the wire in the field. When a current of 2.0 A is switched on, the reading of the balance rises by 1.02 g. Find the magnetic field strength between the poles. Use $g = 9.81$ m/s².
@step The extra reading is the weight of 1.02 g: $F = mg = 0.00102 \times 9.81 = 0.0100$ N.
@step $B = \dfrac{F}{IL} = \dfrac{0.0100}{2.0 \times 0.10}$.
@answer $B = 0.050$ T.
:::

:::mistake Using the whole length of the wire
$L$ is only the length of wire that is **inside the field**. A long wire that passes through a magnet 10 cm wide has $L = 0.10$ m, whatever its full length.
:::

## The electric motor

A straight wire is pushed once and leaves the field. To get continuous motion, bend the wire into a rectangular coil and mount it on an axle between the poles of a magnet. A motor changes electrical energy to kinetic energy.

The current goes along one side of the coil and comes back along the other. The two sides carry current in opposite directions through the same field, so the forces on them are opposite: one side is pushed up and the other down. The two forces together turn the coil.

### The commutator

There is a difficulty. When the coil has turned to the upright position, the two forces still point up and down, but now they act along the same line, through the axle. They pull the coil apart and do not turn it. If the coil swings past this position, the forces turn it back. A coil connected by two fixed wires would rock about the upright position and stop there.

The **split-ring commutator** solves this. It is a metal ring cut into two halves, one joined to each end of the coil, and it turns with the coil. Two fixed carbon **brushes** press on it and carry the current between the circuit and the turning coil. Each time the coil passes the upright position, each half of the ring moves on to the other brush. This reverses the current in the coil every half turn, so the forces always turn the coil the same way.

- The turning effect is greatest when the plane of the coil is parallel to the field, because the two forces are then furthest apart.
- The turning effect is zero when the plane of the coil is at right angles to the field.
- Real motors have several coils at different angles on the same axle, so that some coil is always in a good position and the motor runs smoothly.
- To make a motor turn the other way, reverse the current or reverse the field, but not both.

### The force on the side of a coil

A coil has many turns, and each turn of a side is a wire that feels a force $BIL$. For a coil of $N$ turns, the force on one side is

$$F = NBIL$$

:::example The force on one side of a motor coil
The coil of a motor has 100 turns. One side of it, 4.0 cm long, lies at right angles to a magnetic field of 0.20 T. Find the force on that side when the current in the coil is 1.5 A.
@step Change to metres: $L = 0.040$ m.
@step $F = NBIL = 100 \times 0.20 \times 1.5 \times 0.040$.
@answer $F = 1.2$ N.
:::

The force is proportional to each of $N$, $B$ and $I$. If the current in this coil is doubled, and the magnet is changed for one with three times the field, the force becomes $2 \times 3 = 6$ times as large: 7.2 N. A motor is made more powerful by a larger current, more turns or a stronger magnet.

:::mistake Saying that the commutator changes the supply to alternating current
The supply to the motor is still direct current. The commutator reverses the current **in the coil** every half turn, and it does this so that the coil keeps turning the same way.
:::

:::summary
- The motor effect: a wire that carries a current across a magnetic field feels a force, $F = BIL\sin\theta$, which is $F = BIL$ at right angles.
- Right hand: thumb along the conventional current, fingers along the field, and the palm pushes along the force.
- A wire floats when $BIL = mg$.
- In a motor, the forces on the two sides of the coil are opposite, so they turn it.
- The split-ring commutator reverses the current in the coil every half turn, which keeps the coil turning the same way.
- The force on one side of a coil of $N$ turns is $F = NBIL$.
:::
