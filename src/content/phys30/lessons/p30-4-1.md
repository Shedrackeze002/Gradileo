For most of the 1800s the atom was thought to be the smallest piece of matter, a tiny solid ball that could not be divided. Then physicists began to pass electricity through glass tubes with most of the air pumped out, and saw a strange glow that crossed the tube. In 1897 J. J. Thomson showed that the glow was a stream of particles far lighter than any atom. The atom had parts, and the first of them, the electron, had been found.

## Cathode rays

A **cathode ray tube** is a sealed glass tube with a metal plate at each end and almost all of the air pumped out. A high voltage is connected across the plates. The negative plate is the **cathode** and the positive plate is the **anode**. When the voltage is high enough, invisible rays leave the cathode, cross the tube and make the glass at the far end glow. They were named **cathode rays**.

The air has to be pumped out so that the rays can cross the tube without striking many gas molecules on the way.

Experiments showed how the rays behave.

- They travel in straight lines **from the cathode towards the anode**, and an object in the way casts a sharp shadow.
- A light paddle wheel in their path begins to turn, so the rays are made of particles that have **mass** and carry **momentum**.
- They bend **towards a positive plate** and away from a negative one, so the particles carry a **negative charge**.
- A magnet deflects them, at right angles to the field and to their motion, exactly as the hand rule of Unit 2 predicts for moving negative charges.
- They are the same whatever metal the cathode is made of, and whatever gas is left in the tube.

Light and X-rays pass between charged plates without bending, since they are electromagnetic waves and carry no charge. Cathode rays bend, so they cannot be waves of that kind.

:::definition Cathode rays
Cathode rays are streams of fast **electrons**. Each electron has a negative charge and a mass about 1800 times smaller than the mass of the lightest atom.
:::

### What Thomson concluded

Thomson measured the ratio of the charge of the particles to their mass. He found the same ratio for every cathode metal and every gas, so the particles had to be a part of **every** atom. The ratio was also about 1800 times larger than the ratio for a hydrogen ion, which is the lightest ion. The two carry charges of the same size, so the new particle had to have only about one 1800th of the mass.

This was a problem for the old idea of the atom as a ball that cannot be divided. Atoms have smaller parts inside them.

Atoms are neutral, so an atom that contains negative electrons must also contain an equal amount of positive charge. Thomson pictured the atom as a ball of positive charge with the electrons dotted through it, like raisins in a bun. This is often called the **plum pudding model**. The next lesson shows how it was tested, and why it failed.

:::mistake Sending the rays the wrong way
Cathode rays leave the negative plate, the cathode, and travel towards the positive plate, the anode. They are negative, so between two charged plates they bend towards the positive one.
:::

## The charge-to-mass ratio

Thomson could not weigh one electron or measure its charge. What he could do was steer the beam with fields, and the way a charged particle steers depends on the ratio of its charge to its mass, $\dfrac{q}{m}$. He found it in two steps, and both use ideas from Unit 2.

### Step 1: the speed of the beam

Thomson passed the beam through an electric field and a magnetic field at the same time, set at right angles so that the two forces on each electron pointed in opposite directions. He adjusted the fields until the beam went **straight**. The two forces were then equal in size.

$$qE = qvB \quad \text{so} \quad v = \dfrac{E}{B}$$

The charge cancels, so the speed comes from the two field strengths alone. If the magnetic field is made twice as strong, the electric field must be made twice as strong as well to keep the same beam straight.

### Step 2: the circle in the magnetic field

With the electric field turned off, only the magnetic force acts. It is always at right angles to the motion, so the beam bends into a **circle**. The magnetic force is the centripetal force.

$$qvB = \dfrac{mv^2}{r} \quad \text{so} \quad \dfrac{q}{m} = \dfrac{v}{Br}$$

:::definition The charge-to-mass ratio
$$\dfrac{q}{m} = \dfrac{v}{Br}$$

- $\dfrac{q}{m}$ is the charge-to-mass ratio of the particle, in coulombs per kilogram (C/kg);
- $v$ is its speed, in m/s, and $B$ is the magnetic field, in teslas;
- $r$ is the radius of its circular path, in metres.
:::

:::example Thomson's two steps
A beam of cathode rays passes straight through an electric field of $3.00 \times 10^{4}$ V/m crossed with a magnetic field of 1.50 mT. With the electric field turned off, the beam moves in a circle with a radius of 7.58 cm. Find the speed of the particles and their charge-to-mass ratio.
@step Straight beam: $v = \dfrac{E}{B} = \dfrac{3.00 \times 10^{4}}{1.50 \times 10^{-3}} = 2.00 \times 10^{7}$ m/s.
@step Change the units: $r = 0.0758$ m.
@step $\dfrac{q}{m} = \dfrac{v}{Br} = \dfrac{2.00 \times 10^{7}}{1.50 \times 10^{-3} \times 0.0758}$.
@answer The speed is $2.00 \times 10^{7}$ m/s and the ratio is $1.76 \times 10^{11}$ C/kg.
:::

### Telling particles apart

Each kind of particle has its own charge-to-mass ratio, so the ratio works like a name tag.

| Particle | Charge-to-mass ratio |
|---|---|
| Electron | $1.76 \times 10^{11}$ C/kg |
| Proton | $9.58 \times 10^{7}$ C/kg |
| Alpha particle | $4.82 \times 10^{7}$ C/kg |

The radius of the circle is $r = \dfrac{v}{B\,(q/m)}$. At the same speed in the same field, the particle with the **largest** ratio moves in the **smallest** circle. A proton has a ratio about 1800 times smaller than an electron, so it moves in a much larger circle, and since its charge is positive it curves the opposite way.

:::example Which particle is it?
A beam of unknown particles moves at $3.00 \times 10^{6}$ m/s at right angles to a magnetic field of 0.250 T, and follows a circle with a radius of 12.5 cm. Find the charge-to-mass ratio and say what the particles are.
@step $\dfrac{q}{m} = \dfrac{v}{Br} = \dfrac{3.00 \times 10^{6}}{0.250 \times 0.125}$.
@step $\dfrac{q}{m} = 9.60 \times 10^{7}$ C/kg.
@answer The ratio matches the proton in the table, so the particles are protons.
:::

### From the ratio to the mass

Thomson's equations hold the charge and the mass only as a ratio, so his experiment could not give either one by itself. In 1909 Robert Millikan measured the charge of the electron with his oil drops, as Unit 2 described. With the charge known, the mass follows.

:::example The mass of the electron
The charge-to-mass ratio of the electron is $1.76 \times 10^{11}$ C/kg, and its charge has a size of $1.60 \times 10^{-19}$ C. Find its mass.
@step $m = \dfrac{q}{q/m} = \dfrac{1.60 \times 10^{-19}}{1.76 \times 10^{11}}$.
@answer $m = 9.09 \times 10^{-31}$ kg. More exact measurements give $9.11 \times 10^{-31}$ kg, the value used in earlier units.
:::

:::example The mass of an ion
A beam of positive ions has a charge-to-mass ratio of $4.82 \times 10^{7}$ C/kg, and each ion carries a charge of $3.20 \times 10^{-19}$ C. Find the mass of one ion.
@step $m = \dfrac{q}{q/m} = \dfrac{3.20 \times 10^{-19}}{4.82 \times 10^{7}}$.
@answer $m = 6.64 \times 10^{-27}$ kg. This is an alpha particle.
:::

### A beam that is speeded up from rest

Sometimes the speed is not measured. The particles start from rest and are accelerated through a potential difference $V$, so they gain a kinetic energy $qV = \dfrac{1}{2}mv^2$. Putting this together with $\dfrac{q}{m} = \dfrac{v}{Br}$ removes the speed.

$$\dfrac{q}{m} = \dfrac{2V}{B^2 r^2}$$

:::example A ratio without the speed
Particles are accelerated from rest through 500 V and then enter a magnetic field of 1.00 mT at right angles. They move in a circle with a radius of 7.54 cm. Find their charge-to-mass ratio.
@step $\dfrac{q}{m} = \dfrac{2V}{B^2 r^2} = \dfrac{2 \times 500}{(1.00 \times 10^{-3})^2 \times 0.0754^2}$.
@step The bottom line is $1.00 \times 10^{-6} \times 5.685 \times 10^{-3} = 5.685 \times 10^{-9}$.
@answer $\dfrac{q}{m} = 1.76 \times 10^{11}$ C/kg, so the particles are electrons.
:::

:::mistake Leaving the radius in centimetres
The radius of the circle is usually given in centimetres and the field in milliteslas. Change them to metres and teslas before you use $\dfrac{q}{m} = \dfrac{v}{Br}$.
:::

:::summary
- Cathode rays are streams of fast electrons. They travel from the cathode to the anode, carry momentum, and bend towards a positive plate.
- Thomson found the same charge-to-mass ratio for every cathode and every gas, so electrons are a part of every atom.
- A beam passes straight through crossed fields when $v = \dfrac{E}{B}$.
- In a magnetic field alone the beam moves in a circle, and $\dfrac{q}{m} = \dfrac{v}{Br}$.
- For a beam accelerated from rest through a potential difference $V$: $\dfrac{q}{m} = \dfrac{2V}{B^2 r^2}$.
- The electron has $\dfrac{q}{m} = 1.76 \times 10^{11}$ C/kg. With Millikan's charge, this gives its mass, about $9.1 \times 10^{-31}$ kg.
- Thomson pictured the atom as a ball of positive charge with electrons dotted through it.
:::
