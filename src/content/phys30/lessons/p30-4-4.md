Rutherford's nucleus holds all of the positive charge of an atom in a space a hundred thousand times smaller than the atom. That raises a hard question. Positive charges repel, and Coulomb's law says that the repulsion grows enormously as the charges get closer. So what is inside the nucleus, and what stops it from flying apart? This lesson describes the particles of the nucleus, the way a nucleus is written down, and the force that holds it together.

## Nuclides and isotopes

A nucleus is made of two kinds of particle.

| Particle | Charge | Mass |
|---|---|---|
| **Proton** | $+e$ | about 1 u |
| **Neutron** | 0 | about 1 u, a little more than the proton |

A proton or a neutron is called a **nucleon**. The atomic mass unit, u, is a convenient unit for such small masses: $1\ \text{u} = 1.66 \times 10^{-27}$ kg. An electron has a mass of only about $\dfrac{1}{1800}$ u, so nearly all of the mass of an atom is in its nucleus.

The neutron was not found until 1932, long after the proton. It has no charge, so electric and magnetic fields do not deflect it, and it leaves no trail of ions behind it. James Chadwick found it through the protons that it knocked out of wax.

:::definition Atomic number and mass number
- The **atomic number**, $Z$, is the number of protons in the nucleus. It decides which element the atom is.
- The **mass number**, $A$, is the number of nucleons: protons and neutrons together.
- The number of neutrons is $N = A - Z$.

A nucleus is written ${}^{A}_{Z}\text{X}$, with the mass number above the atomic number and the chemical symbol after them. It can also be named by its element and mass number, such as carbon-14.
:::

:::example Reading a nucleus
The nucleus of an atom of uranium-238 is written ${}^{238}_{92}\text{U}$. How many protons and how many neutrons does it contain?
@step Protons: the atomic number, 92.
@step Neutrons: $A - Z = 238 - 92$.
@answer 92 protons and 146 neutrons.
:::

:::example Writing a nucleus
A nucleus contains 26 protons and 30 neutrons. Find its atomic number and its mass number.
@step The atomic number is the number of protons, 26. This is iron.
@step The mass number is $26 + 30$.
@answer $Z = 26$ and $A = 56$: the nucleus is iron-56, ${}^{56}_{26}\text{Fe}$.
:::

### Atoms and ions

A neutral atom has as many electrons as protons. An ion has gained or lost electrons, and the nucleus is not changed at all. A charge of $+2e$ means two fewer electrons than protons, and a charge of $-e$ means one more.

:::example The particles of an ion
An ion of copper-63 has a charge of $+2e$ and the nucleus ${}^{63}_{29}\text{Cu}$. How many protons, neutrons and electrons does it have?
@step Protons: 29. Neutrons: $63 - 29 = 34$.
@step Electrons: two fewer than the protons, $29 - 2$.
@answer 29 protons, 34 neutrons and 27 electrons.
:::

### Isotopes

:::definition Isotopes
Isotopes are atoms of the same element with different numbers of neutrons. They have the **same atomic number** and **different mass numbers**.
:::

Carbon-12, ${}^{12}_{6}\text{C}$, and carbon-14, ${}^{14}_{6}\text{C}$, are isotopes. Both have 6 protons, and they have 6 and 8 neutrons. The isotopes of an element have the same number of protons, and so the same number of electrons, which is why they have the same chemical behaviour. Their nuclei differ, though, and one isotope can be stable while another is radioactive.

To pick out an isotope, look for the same number at the bottom and a different number at the top. ${}^{29}_{12}\text{X}$ is an isotope of ${}^{26}_{12}\text{X}$. ${}^{26}_{13}\text{X}$ is not, since it has a different atomic number and is a different element.

### The average mass of an element

Most elements are a mixture of isotopes. The mass in the periodic table is the average over all of the atoms, with each isotope counted according to its share.

:::example An average mass
Chlorine has two isotopes, with mass numbers 35 and 37. In a sample, 75.8% of the atoms are chlorine-35. Take the mass of each atom, in atomic mass units, to be equal to its mass number. Find the average mass of an atom.
@step The heavier isotope makes up $100 - 75.8 = 24.2\%$.
@step Average mass $= 0.758 \times 35 + 0.242 \times 37$.
@answer 35.48 u, which is why the periodic table gives chlorine a mass close to 35.5.
:::

:::example Finding the share of each isotope
Boron has two isotopes, with mass numbers 10 and 11, and the average mass of its atoms is 10.80 u. Take the mass of each atom to be equal to its mass number. What percentage of the atoms are boron-10?
@step Let $x$ be the fraction that is boron-10. Then $10x + 11(1 - x) = 10.80$.
@step $11 - x = 10.80$, so $x = 0.20$.
@answer 20.0% of the atoms are boron-10, and 80.0% are boron-11.
:::

:::mistake Reading the mass number as the number of neutrons
The top number counts protons and neutrons together. Subtract the atomic number to find the neutrons: carbon-14 has $14 - 6 = 8$ neutrons.
:::

## The strong nuclear force and the size of a nucleus

The protons in a nucleus are extremely close together, so they repel one another with great force.

:::example Two protons in a nucleus
Two protons in a nucleus are 2.0 fm apart ($1\ \text{fm} = 10^{-15}$ m). Find the electric force with which they repel each other.
@step $F = \dfrac{kq_1q_2}{r^2} = \dfrac{8.99 \times 10^{9} \times (1.60 \times 10^{-19})^2}{(2.0 \times 10^{-15})^2}$.
@answer $F = 57.5$ N. This is about the weight of a 6 kg mass, acting on a particle of $1.67 \times 10^{-27}$ kg.
:::

Gravity between the two protons is far too weak to hold them. Something much stronger must pull them together.

:::definition The strong nuclear force
The strong nuclear force is an attraction between nucleons. It acts between **any two nucleons**: two protons, two neutrons, or a proton and a neutron. It has a very **short range**, and acts only between nucleons that are almost touching, about $10^{-15}$ m apart.
:::

At $1 \times 10^{-15}$ m, the strong attraction between two protons is much larger than their electric repulsion, so the nucleus holds together. At $1 \times 10^{-10}$ m, the width of an atom, the strong force has almost no effect and only the electric repulsion is left. This is why we never notice the strong force outside the nucleus.

### Why neutrons are needed

A neutron feels the strong force but carries no charge. Each extra neutron adds strong attraction without adding electric repulsion, so neutrons act as a kind of glue.

Light stable nuclei have about as many neutrons as protons. Heavy stable nuclei need more neutrons than protons, since the repulsion of so many protons has to be balanced. Lead-208 has 82 protons and 126 neutrons.

There is a limit. The electric repulsion acts across the whole nucleus, so every proton repels every other proton. The strong force only reaches the neighbouring nucleons. As a nucleus grows, the total repulsion grows faster than the attraction, and at last no number of neutrons can hold the nucleus together. This is why all very large nuclei, such as those of uranium, are unstable. The next lesson shows what an unstable nucleus does.

### The size of a nucleus

Scattering experiments like Rutherford's show that nucleons are packed equally closely in every nucleus, like marbles in a bag. The volume of a nucleus is then proportional to the number of nucleons, and its radius is proportional to the cube root of the mass number.

:::definition The radius of a nucleus
$$r = 1.2 \times 10^{-15} \times \sqrt[3]{A}\ \text{metres}$$

- $r$ is the radius of the nucleus;
- $A$ is its mass number;
- $1.2 \times 10^{-15}$ m, or 1.2 fm, is about the radius of a single nucleon.
:::

:::example The radius of a gold nucleus
Find the radius of a nucleus of gold-197.
@step $\sqrt[3]{197} = 5.819$.
@step $r = 1.2 \times 10^{-15} \times 5.819$.
@answer $r = 6.98 \times 10^{-15}$ m, about 7 fm. The alpha particles of the last lesson stopped about six times further out than this, so they never touched the nucleus.
:::

:::example Comparing two nuclei
How many times larger is the radius of a nucleus of uranium-238 than the radius of a nucleus of helium-4?
@step The radius is proportional to $\sqrt[3]{A}$, so $\dfrac{r_1}{r_2} = \sqrt[3]{\dfrac{238}{4}} = \sqrt[3]{59.5}$.
@answer 3.90 times. The uranium nucleus has almost 60 times as many nucleons, but its radius is less than 4 times as large.
:::

:::example From the radius to the mass number
A nucleus has a radius of 4.8 fm. Use $r = 1.2 \times \sqrt[3]{A}$ fm to estimate its mass number.
@step $\sqrt[3]{A} = \dfrac{4.8}{1.2} = 4.0$.
@step $A = 4.0^3$.
@answer The mass number is 64.
:::

Since the nucleons are packed equally closely, a small nucleus and a large nucleus have about the same density. That density is enormous: a teaspoon of nuclear matter would have a mass of about a billion tonnes.

:::mistake Thinking that the strong force only acts on protons
The strong force acts between any two nucleons, with or without charge. It is the electric force that acts only between the protons.
:::

:::summary
- A nucleus is made of protons and neutrons, which are called nucleons.
- The atomic number $Z$ is the number of protons, the mass number $A$ is the number of nucleons, and $N = A - Z$ is the number of neutrons.
- A nucleus is written ${}^{A}_{Z}\text{X}$. An ion has gained or lost electrons, and its nucleus is unchanged.
- Isotopes have the same atomic number and different mass numbers. They behave the same chemically.
- The average mass of an element is found from the share of each isotope.
- The strong nuclear force attracts any two nucleons, but only over about $10^{-15}$ m. It overcomes the electric repulsion of the protons.
- Heavy nuclei need more neutrons than protons, and very large nuclei are unstable.
- The radius of a nucleus is about $r = 1.2 \times 10^{-15} \times \sqrt[3]{A}$ m, and all nuclei have about the same density.
:::
