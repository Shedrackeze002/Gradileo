A compass needle swings round to point north, wherever on the Earth you stand. Sailors used this for hundreds of years before anyone could explain it. The explanation is a third kind of field. A mass sets up a gravitational field and a charge sets up an electric field. A magnet sets up a **magnetic field**, and so, as Hans Christian Oersted found by accident in 1820, does an electric current.

## Magnets and magnetic fields

Every magnet has two **poles**, called north and south. The north pole is the end that points towards the geographic north when the magnet is free to turn.

- Like poles repel: two north poles push apart, and so do two south poles.
- Unlike poles attract: a north pole and a south pole pull together.

Poles always come in pairs. Cut a bar magnet in half and you do not get a separate north pole and south pole. You get two complete magnets, each with both poles. This is one way in which magnetism differs from electricity, where a positive charge can exist without a negative one.

### The magnetic field

The space round a magnet, where it exerts a force on other magnets, holds a magnetic field. Its strength has the symbol $B$ and is measured in **teslas** (T). The field of the Earth at the surface is about 0.00005 T, and a strong laboratory magnet reaches about 1 T.

The direction of the field at a point is the direction in which the north pole of a small compass would point there. Field lines show the pattern.

- Outside a magnet the field lines run from the north pole round to the south pole.
- Inside the magnet they run from the south pole back to the north pole, so every line is a closed loop. Electric field lines are different: they begin and end on charges.
- The lines are closest together near the poles, where the field is strongest.
- The lines never cross.

Between a north pole and a south pole that face each other, the field runs straight across the gap and is nearly uniform. Between two like poles that face each other, the fields oppose, and at the point halfway between them they cancel.

### Why some materials are magnetic

Iron, nickel and cobalt are **ferromagnetic**: a magnet attracts them strongly. Copper, aluminium and gold are not. A piece of iron is made of tiny regions called **domains**, and each domain is a small magnet. In an ordinary bar of iron the domains point in all directions, and their fields cancel. An outside magnetic field turns more of the domains to point the same way, and the bar becomes a magnet.

This explains several things at once.

- A steel paper clip is attracted to either pole of a magnet. The magnet lines up the domains of the clip, which makes it a temporary magnet with the unlike pole nearest.
- Heating a magnet strongly, or hammering it, shakes the domains out of line and weakens it.
- Soft iron loses its magnetism as soon as the outside field is removed, so it is used for the cores of electromagnets. Steel keeps its magnetism, so it is used for permanent magnets.

### The magnetic field of the Earth

The Earth behaves as if a huge bar magnet lay inside it. The north pole of a compass needle is pulled towards the geographic north, and unlike poles attract, so the pole of the Earth's magnet that lies near the geographic north is a magnetic **south** pole.

The Earth's field also protects us. A magnetic field exerts a force only on a charge that moves across it. Charged particles from the Sun are made to spiral along the field lines, which come down near the poles. There they strike the upper air and make it glow: the aurora.

| Field | Made by | Acts on | Attracts or repels? |
|---|---|---|---|
| Gravitational | Mass | Any mass | Attracts only |
| Electric | Charge | Any charge | Both |
| Magnetic | Magnets and moving charges | Magnets and moving charges | Both |

:::mistake Calling the Earth's northern magnetic pole a north pole
The north pole of a compass points to it, so it must be a south magnetic pole. It is called the "north magnetic pole" only because of where it is on the map.
:::

## The magnetic field of a current

Oersted noticed that a compass needle near a wire turned when he switched on a current in the wire. A current produces a magnetic field. This was the first sign that electricity and magnetism are two sides of one thing: all magnetic fields come from moving charges.

The field lines of a long straight wire are circles centred on the wire. Their direction is given by a rule for the right hand.

:::definition The right-hand rule for a wire
Grip the wire with the right hand, with the thumb pointing along the **conventional current**, from positive to negative. The fingers curl round the wire in the direction of the magnetic field.
:::

Reverse the current, and the field circles the other way. For a wire that carries its current straight up through a table, the thumb points up and, seen from above, the fingers curl anticlockwise.

The field is stronger for a larger current, and weaker further from the wire:

$$B = \dfrac{\mu_0 I}{2\pi r}$$

- $I$ is the current, in amperes;
- $r$ is the distance from the wire, in metres;
- $\mu_0 = 4\pi \times 10^{-7}$ T·m/A is a constant, the permeability of free space.

:::example The field near a wire
A long straight wire carries a current of 5 A. Find the magnetic field strength 2 cm from the wire.
@step Change to metres: $r = 0.02$ m.
@step $B = \dfrac{\mu_0 I}{2\pi r} = \dfrac{4\pi \times 10^{-7} \times 5}{2\pi \times 0.02}$.
@step The $\pi$ cancels: $B = \dfrac{2 \times 10^{-7} \times 5}{0.02}$.
@answer $B = 5.0 \times 10^{-5}$ T, about the same as the field of the Earth.
:::

:::example Finding the current
The magnetic field strength 3.0 cm from a long straight wire is $4.0 \times 10^{-5}$ T. What is the current in the wire?
@step Rearrange: $I = \dfrac{2\pi rB}{\mu_0}$.
@step $I = \dfrac{2\pi \times 0.030 \times 4.0 \times 10^{-5}}{4\pi \times 10^{-7}}$.
@answer $I = 6.0$ A.
:::

### The solenoid

One wire gives a weak field. Wind the wire into a long coil, a **solenoid**, and the fields of all the turns add. Outside, the field of a solenoid looks like the field of a bar magnet, with a north pole at one end and a south pole at the other. Inside, the field is strong and nearly uniform:

$$B = \dfrac{\mu_0 NI}{L}$$

where $N$ is the number of turns and $L$ is the length of the coil, in metres.

:::example The field inside a solenoid
A solenoid has 800 turns along a length of 40 cm, and carries a current of 2 A. Find the magnetic field strength inside it.
@step Change to metres: $L = 0.40$ m.
@step $B = \dfrac{\mu_0 NI}{L} = \dfrac{4\pi \times 10^{-7} \times 800 \times 2}{0.40}$.
@answer $B = 5.03 \times 10^{-3}$ T, a hundred times the field of the Earth.
:::

A solenoid with a soft iron core is an **electromagnet**. The core's domains line up with the field of the coil and make it hundreds of times stronger. An electromagnet is made stronger by a larger current, by more turns for each metre, or by adding the iron core, and it can be switched off.

:::mistake Using the direction of the electrons in the right-hand rule
The thumb points along the conventional current, from positive to negative. Electrons drift the opposite way. If a question gives the direction of the electron flow, reverse it first, or use the left hand.
:::

:::summary
- Like poles repel, and unlike poles attract. Poles always come in pairs.
- Magnetic field strength $B$ is measured in teslas. Field lines run from north to south outside a magnet, and form closed loops.
- Ferromagnetic materials contain domains. A magnet is a material whose domains are lined up.
- A current produces a magnetic field. Round a straight wire the field lines are circles, in the direction given by the right-hand rule.
- For a long straight wire, $B = \dfrac{\mu_0 I}{2\pi r}$. Inside a solenoid, $B = \dfrac{\mu_0 NI}{L}$.
- A magnetic field exerts a force only on magnets and on charges that move across it.
:::
