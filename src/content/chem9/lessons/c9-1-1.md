In Grade 8 you met atoms as the smallest particles of an element. But atoms are not solid balls: they are built from even smaller particles. In this lesson you will meet the three particles inside every atom, and see how scientists slowly worked out the atom's structure from clever experiments.

## Protons, neutrons and electrons

:::definition The particles in an atom
An atom has a tiny, dense **nucleus** at its centre, containing **protons** and **neutrons**. **Electrons** move around the nucleus in **shells** (energy levels).

| Particle | Relative charge | Relative mass | Where it is |
|---|---|---|---|
| proton | +1 | 1 | nucleus |
| neutron | 0 | 1 | nucleus |
| electron | −1 | about $\dfrac{1}{2000}$ | shells around the nucleus |
:::

An atom has the **same number of protons and electrons**, so their charges cancel and the atom has no overall charge.

Almost all the mass of an atom is in the nucleus, because protons and neutrons are about 2000 times heavier than electrons. Yet the nucleus is tiny: if an atom were the size of a football stadium, the nucleus would be a pea on the centre spot. So most of an atom is **empty space**.

:::example Why no charge?
A carbon atom has 6 protons. How many electrons does it have, and why?
@step Each proton has a charge of +1 and each electron −1.
@step An atom has no overall charge, so the charges must cancel.
@answer 6 electrons: six +1 charges are cancelled by six −1 charges.
:::

:::mistake Neutrons are not negative
Neutrons have **no** charge. Only electrons are negative.
:::

## How the model of the atom changed

Nobody can see inside an atom, so scientists build **models** and change them when new evidence does not fit.

| Year | Scientist | Model |
|---|---|---|
| about 1803 | John Dalton | atoms are tiny solid spheres that cannot be divided |
| 1897 | J. J. Thomson | discovered the electron; **plum pudding** model: a ball of positive charge with electrons scattered through it |
| 1911 | Ernest Rutherford | **nuclear model**: a tiny, dense, positive nucleus with electrons around it |
| 1913 | Niels Bohr | electrons move in fixed **shells** at set distances from the nucleus |
| 1932 | James Chadwick | discovered the neutron in the nucleus |

:::definition The gold foil experiment
Rutherford's team fired positive alpha particles at a very thin sheet of gold. **Most went straight through**, so most of the atom is empty space. **A very few bounced back**, so there must be a tiny, dense, positive centre: the nucleus.
:::

:::example Evidence and models
Why could the plum pudding model not explain alpha particles bouncing back?
@step In the plum pudding model the positive charge is spread thinly through the whole atom.
@step Spread-out charge could only nudge a fast alpha particle slightly, never throw it back.
@answer Only a small, dense concentration of positive charge, a nucleus, could repel an alpha particle straight back.
:::

:::summary
- Protons (+1, mass 1) and neutrons (0, mass 1) are in the **nucleus**; electrons (−1, tiny mass) are in **shells**.
- An atom has equal numbers of protons and electrons, so it has no overall charge.
- Models of the atom changed with evidence: Dalton's spheres, Thomson's plum pudding, Rutherford's nucleus, Bohr's shells.
- The gold foil experiment showed that an atom is mostly empty space with a tiny, dense, positive nucleus.
:::
