Every poem has a voice. Sometimes it sounds like the poet; sometimes it is a character, an animal or even a tree. That voice is the **speaker**, and the attitude it takes is the **tone**.

## The speaker

:::definition Speaker
The **speaker** is the voice that speaks the words of a poem. The speaker may be close to the poet, or a character the poet has invented.
:::

If a poem begins *I am the old oak at the end of the lane*, the speaker is a tree, not the poet. Because poets so often invent speakers, it is safer to write “the speaker feels…” than “the poet feels…”.

> I’m nobody! Who are you?\
> Are you nobody, too?\
> Then there’s a pair of us — don’t tell!\
> They’d banish us, you know.
>
> How dreary to be somebody!\
> How public, like a frog\
> To tell your name the livelong day\
> To an admiring bog!
>
> — Emily Dickinson, “I’m Nobody! Who are you?” (as printed in 1891)

Dickinson’s speaker is happy to be a “nobody” and whispers to the reader as if sharing a secret. Being “somebody”, famous and public, sounds dreary to her.

:::example Getting to know a speaker
In Whitman’s “When I Heard the Learn’d Astronomer” (Lesson 2.4), the speaker sits through a lecture full of charts and figures.

@step He becomes “tired and sick”.
@step He slips outside and looks up “in perfect silence at the stars”.
@answer The speaker is someone who feels the wonder of the stars more than the facts about them.
:::

## Tone

:::definition Tone
**Tone** is the speaker’s attitude towards the subject: admiring, playful, bitter, weary, angry, thoughtful and so on.
:::

Tone comes from **word choice**, **punctuation** and what the speaker **praises or mocks**. Dickinson’s exclamation marks, her secret (*don’t tell!*) and her joke about a frog croaking its name to a bog make the tone **playful**, while *dreary* and *like a frog* mock the idea of fame.

> I shall be telling this with a sigh\
> Somewhere ages and ages hence:\
> Two roads diverged in a wood, and I—\
> I took the one less traveled by,\
> And that has made all the difference.
>
> — Robert Frost, “The Road Not Taken” (1916), last stanza

Is Frost’s sigh one of regret or of quiet satisfaction? The poem does not say. Its tone is **thoughtful** and open, which is one reason readers still argue about it.

:::mistake Tone and mood
**Tone** belongs to the speaker: it is their attitude. **Mood** belongs to the reader: it is the feeling the poem creates. A speaker can sound calm (tone) while the reader feels uneasy (mood).
:::

:::summary
- The **speaker** is the voice of the poem, and it is not always the poet.
- **Tone** is the speaker’s attitude to the subject.
- Word choice, punctuation and what is praised or mocked reveal the tone.
- Tone is the speaker’s attitude; mood is the reader’s feeling.
:::
