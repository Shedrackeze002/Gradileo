A poem can make you see a wheelbarrow shining in the rain, smell an autumn orchard or hear ice cracking around a ship. Language that appeals to the senses like this is called **imagery**, and it is one of the most powerful tools a poet has.

## Images for every sense

:::definition Imagery
**Imagery** is language that appeals to the five senses: **sight**, **sound**, **touch**, **smell** and **taste**. Each word picture is called an **image**.
:::

| Sense | Example |
|---|---|
| sight | *The scarlet kite blazed against the clear blue sky.* |
| sound | *The kettle shrieked, and the pans rattled on the stove.* |
| touch | *The icy railing burned my bare fingers.* |
| smell | *The kitchen was thick with cinnamon and warm bread.* |
| taste | *The lemon was so sour it made my whole face pucker.* |

Here is a famous poem made almost entirely of one image.

> so much depends\
> upon
>
> a red wheel\
> barrow
>
> glazed with rain\
> water
>
> beside the white\
> chickens
>
> — William Carlos Williams, “The Red Wheelbarrow” (1923)

Williams gives us colours (red, white), a texture (glazed) and a scene (the chickens beside the barrow). The poem asks us to *see* an ordinary farmyard so clearly that it becomes beautiful.

:::example Which sense?
> It cracked and growled, and roared and howled,\
> Like noises in a swound!
>
> — Samuel Taylor Coleridge, “The Rime of the Ancient Mariner”

@step Cracked, growled, roared and howled are all noises.
@step The poem is describing the ice around a ship, but we hear it rather than see it.
@answer Sound.

## What an image adds

An image is never there just to decorate a poem. Ask what **feeling** or **idea** it brings. Look at the opening of John Keats’s ode to autumn.

> Season of mists and mellow fruitfulness,\
> Close bosom-friend of the maturing sun;\
> Conspiring with him how to load and bless\
> With fruit the vines that round the thatch-eves run;
>
> — John Keats, “To Autumn” (1820), first lines

*Mellow fruitfulness*, *the maturing sun* and vines loaded *with fruit* pile up images of ripeness. Together they make autumn feel rich, warm and generous, not cold and sad.

:::example The effect of an image
> As idle as a painted ship\
> Upon a painted ocean.
>
> — Samuel Taylor Coleridge, “The Rime of the Ancient Mariner”

@step A ship in a painting never moves, however long you look at it.
@step The sailors’ ship is stuck in the same way, with no wind to move it.
@answer The image shows that the ship is becalmed and the sailors are helpless.
:::

:::mistake Imagery is not only about sight
Many students think *imagery* means *pictures*. An image can be a sound, a smell, a taste or a feeling on the skin. Always ask which sense is being used.
:::

:::summary
- **Imagery** is language that appeals to sight, sound, touch, smell or taste.
- An image helps the reader experience a scene, not just be told about it.
- Always ask what feeling or idea an image adds to the poem.
- Vivid images use precise, sensory details.
:::
