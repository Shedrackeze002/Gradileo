Long before most people could read, stories were sung. Shipwrecks, battles, ghosts and lost loves were turned into songs called **ballads** and passed from singer to singer. Poets have written ballads ever since, and you can still hear the old song in them.

## Features of a ballad

:::definition Ballad
A **ballad** is a poem or song that tells a story. It is a kind of **narrative poem**.
:::

Most ballads share these features.

- **Ballad stanzas**: quatrains (four lines) in which lines of **four beats** and **three beats** take turns.
- **Rhyme**: the second and fourth lines rhyme (**ABCB**); sometimes the first and third rhyme too.
- **A dramatic story**, often sad, strange or frightening.
- **Dialogue**, so that the characters speak for themselves.
- **Repetition** and **refrains** (repeated lines), which made the songs easy to remember and sing together.

The most famous literary ballad in English is Samuel Taylor Coleridge’s “The Rime of the Ancient Mariner” (first published in 1798). An old sailor stops a guest on his way to a wedding and tells him a terrible tale of a voyage.

> It is an ancient Mariner,\
> And he stoppeth one of three.\
> ‘By thy long grey beard and glittering eye,\
> Now wherefore stopp’st thou me?
>
> — Samuel Taylor Coleridge, “The Rime of the Ancient Mariner” (1834 text)

:::example Checking a ballad stanza
> Water, water, every where,\
> And all the boards did shrink;\
> Water, water, every where,\
> Nor any drop to drink.

@step Four lines: a quatrain.
@step WA-ter, WA-ter, EV-ery WHERE has four beats; and ALL the BOARDS did SHRINK has three.
@step The second and fourth lines rhyme: *shrink* and *drink*.
@answer It is a ballad stanza: four lines, 4-3-4-3 beats, rhyming ABCB.
:::

## Reading a ballad

Old ballads use some old words. A few to know:

| Old word | Meaning |
|---|---|
| thou, thee | you |
| thy | your |
| wherefore | why |
| stoppeth, stopp’st | stops |

So *Now wherefore stopp’st thou me?* means *Why do you stop me?* The wedding guest is speaking to the Mariner.

To follow a ballad, ask three questions of each stanza: **Who is speaking? What is happening? How does it feel?**

:::example Following the story
> Day after day, day after day,\
> We stuck, nor breath nor motion;\
> As idle as a painted ship\
> Upon a painted ocean.

@step Who is speaking? The Mariner (*we* are the sailors).
@step What is happening? There is no wind (*nor breath nor motion*), so the ship cannot move.
@step How does it feel? Slow, helpless and hopeless.
@answer The ship is becalmed, and the sailors are stuck.
:::

:::summary
- A **ballad** is a poem or song that tells a story.
- A **ballad stanza** has four lines, alternating four and three beats, and usually rhymes ABCB.
- Ballads use dialogue, repetition and refrains, because they began as songs.
- *Thou* means you, *thy* means your and *wherefore* means why.
:::
