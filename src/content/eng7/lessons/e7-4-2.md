Arguments do not only use reasons and evidence. They also use the *way* words are put together to move the reader. These choices are called **persuasive techniques**, and once you can spot them, you can decide for yourself whether to be persuaded.

## Five persuasive techniques

| Technique | What it is | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Rhetorical question** | a question asked for effect, with an obvious expected answer | *Do we really want our children breathing dirty air?* |
| **Rule of three** | three ideas in a row, which sounds complete and memorable | *Our new park will be safe, green and free.* |
| **Emotive language** | words chosen to stir strong feelings | *Helpless puppies abandoned on freezing streets* |
| **Direct address** | speaking straight to the reader as *you* | *You can make a difference.* |
| **Repetition** | repeating words or phrases for emphasis | *We will rebuild. We will recover. We will return.* |

Repeating the same words at the start of sentences, as in *We will… We will… We will…*, has a special name: **anaphora**. Speech writers love it.

:::example Naming the technique
> Is it fair that some students have no books at home? Is it fair that the library is closing? Of course it is not.

@step The writer asks questions but answers them at once.
@step The questions are not really asking; they lead the reader to *no*.
@answer Rhetorical questions (with repetition of *Is it fair*).
:::

## The effect on the reader

To explain a technique, say what it makes the reader **feel** or **think**.

- A **rhetorical question** leads readers to the answer the writer wants, so they feel they reached it themselves.
- **Emotive language** makes readers feel worried, angry or sorry, so they want to act.
- **Inclusive *we*** and **direct address** make readers feel part of the writer’s side.
- **Repetition** and the **rule of three** make ideas stick and sound strong.
- **A single story** about one person moves readers more than a number does.

:::example Explaining an effect
A charity letter describes one named child’s day in detail instead of giving figures.

@step Readers find it easier to care about one person than about a statistic.
@answer The single story makes readers feel more, so they are more likely to give.
:::

:::mistake Feelings are not proof
Emotion is not wrong in persuasion, but it is not evidence. When a text makes you feel strongly, notice the feeling, then ask: *what reasons and evidence back this up?*

- Persuasive techniques include **rhetorical questions**, the **rule of three**, **emotive language**, **direct address** and **repetition**.
- **Anaphora** is repeating words at the start of sentences.
- To explain a technique, say how it makes the reader feel or think.
- A careful reader notices the feeling, then checks the evidence.
