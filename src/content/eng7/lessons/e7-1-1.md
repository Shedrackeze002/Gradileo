Every story is driven by a **conflict**: a struggle that the main character has to face. In Grade 6 you named four kinds of conflict. In this lesson you will sort conflicts into two big families, internal and external, see how one kind can lead to the other, and learn how writers use conflict to build **suspense**.

## Internal and external conflict

:::definition Two families of conflict
- An **external conflict** is a struggle with something outside the character: another character, nature, society or technology.
- An **internal conflict** is a struggle inside the character’s own mind: a fear, a doubt, a hard choice or a feeling they must overcome.
:::

| Conflict | Family | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Character against character | external | two rivals both want to be captain |
| Character against nature | external | a family escapes a flood |
| Character against society | external | a girl protests against an unfair town rule |
| Character against technology | external | a robot vacuum that will not obey |
| Character against self | internal | a boy cannot decide whether to tell the truth |

The best stories usually have both. An external conflict, such as a bully’s threat, often *creates* an internal one: should the character tell someone, or stay silent? The outside problem makes us care, and the inside struggle makes us care even more.

:::example Finding both conflicts
> “Tell anyone and you’ll be sorry,” Leo hissed. All through maths, Ana stared at her book. If she told the teacher, it might stop. Or it might get much worse.

@step The external conflict is with another character: Leo threatens Ana.
@step The threat leads to a struggle inside Ana’s head: should she tell, or not?
@answer An external conflict (character against character) that creates an internal one (character against self).
:::

:::mistake The setting is not always the conflict
A story set at a race is not automatically about the race. If the passage spends its time inside a runner’s head, worrying about falling again, the main conflict is internal, even with rivals and heat around her.

## Building suspense

**Suspense** is the worry a reader feels about what will happen next. It is what keeps you reading “just one more page”. Writers build it in several ways.

:::definition Ways to build suspense
- **A cliffhanger**: stopping at a moment of danger or surprise, before we find out what happens.
- **Short, quick sentences**: they speed up the reading, like a racing heartbeat.
- **A question left unanswered**: a mystery that the writer holds back.
- **A ticking clock**: a deadline that is running out.
- **Slowing down a moment**: stretching a few seconds with tiny details, so the reader has to wait.
:::

:::example Short sentences
> The door creaked open. Sam froze. Footsteps. Closer. Closer.

@step The sentences get shorter and shorter, and two are single words.
@step Reading them feels like holding your breath.
@answer Short, quick sentences build the suspense.
:::

:::summary
- An **external conflict** is with something outside the character; an **internal conflict** happens in the character’s mind.
- An external conflict often creates an internal one, and good stories use both.
- **Suspense** is the reader’s worry about what comes next.
- Writers build suspense with cliffhangers, short sentences, unanswered questions, ticking clocks and slowed-down moments.
:::
