Newspaper columns, speeches, letters to the council and posts online all try to convince you of something. To judge them, you need to take an argument apart and see how it is built: what it claims, why, and what proof it offers.

## The parts of an argument

:::definition Claim, reasons and evidence
- The **claim** is the main point the writer wants you to accept.
- **Reasons** explain *why* the claim is true.
- **Evidence** is the proof that backs up each reason: facts, figures, examples and expert views.
- A **counterclaim** is an argument for the other side.
:::

> Our school should start lunch fifteen minutes later. First, many students are not hungry at 11:30, so food is wasted: last month the canteen threw away 40 kilograms of uneaten meals. Second, a later lunch would split the long afternoon into shorter blocks. Ms. Patel, the school nurse, says that students who eat later concentrate better in their final lessons.

| Part | In the passage |
|---|---|
| Claim | Lunch should start fifteen minutes later. |
| Reason 1 | Many students are not hungry at 11:30, so food is wasted. |
| Evidence | 40 kilograms of meals thrown away last month |
| Reason 2 | A later lunch would split the long afternoon. |
| Evidence | the nurse’s view that students concentrate better |

A fair reader also thinks of **counterclaims**. Someone might object that a later lunch would make the school day finish later. A strong argument answers objections like this.

## Kinds of evidence

| Kind | Example | Watch out for |
|---|---|---|
| **Statistic** | *72% of 500 cyclists felt safer with bike lanes.* | Who did the survey, and how many people? |
| **Expert opinion** | *A sleep scientist says teenagers need eight hours.* | Is the person an expert in *this* subject? |
| **Anecdote** | *My cousin said walking to school woke her up.* | One story may not be typical. |
| **Example** | *Cities such as Amsterdam have built thousands of kilometres of lanes.* | Are the examples typical, or hand-picked? |

Good evidence is **relevant** (it connects directly to the claim) and **reliable** (it comes from a trustworthy source). A famous singer quoted on climate science is not an expert, however famous; a survey about when students feel hungry *is* relevant to a claim about lunch times.

:::example Judging evidence
Claim: *Homework should be limited to one hour a night.*

@step A study of how many students own phones is about phones, not homework.
@step A study linking more than an hour of homework to worse sleep is directly about the amount of homework.
@answer The sleep study is the most relevant evidence.
:::

:::summary
- An argument has a **claim**, **reasons** and **evidence**; a **counterclaim** argues the other side.
- Evidence can be **statistics**, **expert opinion**, **anecdotes** or **examples**.
- Strong evidence is **relevant** to the claim and comes from a **reliable** source.
- An anecdote describes one person’s experience, so it is usually weaker than a fair statistic.
:::
