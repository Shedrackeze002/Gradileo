Read a poem aloud and you will feel a beat, like music. That beat is the poem’s **rhythm**, and poets choose it, together with sounds such as repetition and alliteration, to match what the poem is about.

## Stressed and unstressed syllables

Every word of more than one syllable has one syllable that we say more strongly. That syllable is **stressed**. We say GAR-den, to-DAY and ba-NA-na.

:::definition Rhythm
**Rhythm** is the pattern of **stressed** (strong) and **unstressed** (weak) syllables in a line of poetry. Each strong syllable is called a **beat**.
:::

To find the beats, say the line aloud and tap your finger on the strong syllables.

> Half a league, half a league,\
> Half a league onward,\
> All in the valley of Death\
> Rode the six hundred.
>
> — Alfred Tennyson, “The Charge of the Light Brigade” (1854), first lines

HALF a LEAGUE, HALF a LEAGUE has four beats, in a DUM-da-da pattern. That rhythm drums like horses’ hooves, which suits a poem about a cavalry charge.

:::example Counting beats
> Whenever the moon and stars are set,\
> Whenever the wind is high,\
> All night long in the dark and wet,\
> A man goes riding by.
>
> — Robert Louis Stevenson, “Windy Nights” (1885), first lines

@step Line 1: when-EV-er the MOON and STARS are SET has four beats.
@step Line 4: a MAN goes RID-ing BY has three beats.
@answer The lines take turns with four beats and three beats.
:::

## The iamb

The most common rhythm in English poetry is the **iamb**: an unstressed syllable followed by a stressed one, da-DUM, as in *to-DAY*. Five iambs in a line make **iambic pentameter** (*penta* means five), the rhythm of Shakespeare’s sonnets:

*shall I | com-PARE | thee TO | a SUM | mer’s DAY*

## Sound that matches meaning

Poets choose sounds as carefully as words. **Repetition** stresses an idea, **alliteration** (the same first sound) ties words together, and the length of sounds can speed a line up or slow it down.

> Water, water, every where,\
> And all the boards did shrink;\
> Water, water, every where,\
> Nor any drop to drink.
>
> — Samuel Taylor Coleridge, “The Rime of the Ancient Mariner”

The repeated *water* hammers home a cruel joke: the sailors are surrounded by water, but sea water cannot be drunk. Elsewhere, Coleridge’s line *Day after day, day after day* repeats long, slow sounds, so the line drags like the windless days it describes.

:::example Sound and sense
A poem about a racing train uses short words, a fast beat and many hard *k* and *t* sounds.

@step Short words and a quick beat make us read fast.
@step Hard consonants sound like metal clattering.
@answer The sounds imitate the speed and clatter of the train.
:::

:::summary
- A **stressed** syllable is said more strongly; each stressed syllable is a **beat**.
- **Rhythm** is the pattern of stressed and unstressed syllables.
- An **iamb** is da-DUM; five iambs make **iambic pentameter**.
- Rhythm, repetition and alliteration can match a poem’s meaning: fast for speed, slow for stillness.
:::
