A poem’s **form** is its shape: how many lines it has, how they are grouped, and whether it follows a pattern of rhyme and rhythm. In this lesson you will compare a very strict form, the **sonnet**, with a very free one, **free verse**.

## Telling forms apart

| Form | What to look for |
|---|---|
| **Sonnet** | 14 lines, usually in iambic pentameter, with a set rhyme scheme |
| **Ballad** | a story told in four-line stanzas, beats 4-3-4-3, rhyming ABCB |
| **Limerick** | 5 lines rhyming AABBA, bouncy and funny |
| **Free verse** | no regular rhyme scheme and no regular beat |

**Free verse** follows the rhythms of natural speech. Its lines can be long or short, shaped by the thought rather than a pattern. Walt Whitman was one of its great early writers.

> When I heard the learn’d astronomer,\
> When the proofs, the figures, were ranged in columns before me,\
> When I was shown the charts and diagrams, to add, divide, and measure them,\
> When I sitting heard the astronomer where he lectured with much applause in the lecture-room,\
> How soon unaccountable I became tired and sick,\
> Till rising and gliding out I wander’d off by myself,\
> In the mystical moist night-air, and from time to time,\
> Look’d up in perfect silence at the stars.
>
> — Walt Whitman, “When I Heard the Learn’d Astronomer” (1865)

There is no rhyme and no steady beat. But the form still matters: the long, piled-up “When…” lines feel like the crowded, tiring lecture, and the short, simple last line feels like the peace of looking at the stars.

## The Shakespearean sonnet

:::definition Shakespearean sonnet
A **Shakespearean sonnet** has 14 lines of iambic pentameter: three **quatrains** (four lines each) and a final rhyming **couplet**. Its rhyme scheme is **ABAB CDCD EFEF GG**.
:::

> Shall I compare thee to a summer’s day?\
> Thou art more lovely and more temperate:\
> Rough winds do shake the darling buds of May,\
> And summer’s lease hath all too short a date;\
> Sometime too hot the eye of heaven shines,\
> And often is his gold complexion dimm’d;\
> And every fair from fair sometime declines,\
> By chance or nature’s changing course untrimm’d;\
> But thy eternal summer shall not fade,\
> Nor lose possession of that fair thou ow’st;\
> Nor shall Death brag thou wander’st in his shade,\
> When in eternal lines to time thou grow’st:\
> So long as men can breathe or eyes can see,\
> So long lives this, and this gives life to thee.
>
> — William Shakespeare, Sonnet 18 (1609)

The first eight lines list summer’s faults: rough winds, a season that ends too soon (*summer’s lease hath all too short a date*), a sun that is sometimes too hot. Then, at line 9, the poem changes direction with *But*: the person’s “eternal summer shall not fade”. This change of direction is called the **turn** (or *volta*). The closing couplet explains why: as long as people read this poem, it keeps the person alive.

:::example Reading the rhyme scheme
@step Lines 1 to 4 end *day, temperate, May, date*: A B A B.
@step Lines 5 to 8 end *shines, dimm’d, declines, untrimm’d*: C D C D.
@step Lines 13 and 14 end *see, thee*: G G.
@answer ABAB CDCD EFEF GG, the pattern of a Shakespearean sonnet.
:::

:::mistake Rhymes that look wrong
Some rhymes in old poems, such as *temperate* and *date*, do not sound perfect today. Pronunciation has changed since 1609, and poets also used near rhymes on purpose. Go by the pattern of line endings.
:::

:::summary
- A poem’s **form** is its shape: lines, stanzas, rhyme and rhythm.
- **Free verse** has no regular rhyme or beat, but its line lengths still carry meaning.
- A **Shakespearean sonnet** is three quatrains and a couplet, rhyming ABAB CDCD EFEF GG.
- Many sonnets have a **turn**, where the argument changes direction.
:::
