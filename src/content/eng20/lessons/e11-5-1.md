Three newspapers report the same morning: hospital cleaners and porters stopped work for one day in a dispute over pay. Their headlines read:

- *Hospital Workers Strike for One Day Over Pay*
- *Exhausted Hospital Staff Forced to Strike After Years of Pay Freezes*
- *Striking Staff Abandon Patients in Pay Grab*

None of them is false, yet a reader of each would come away with a different picture of the workers. Journalists cannot report everything, so every report involves choices, and those choices **frame** the event. In this lesson you will recognize framing in news reports and compare headlines.

:::note All invented
Every news story, headline, outlet and person in this unit is invented for Gradileo, so you can study the techniques without taking sides on real events.
:::

## Framing

:::definition Framing
**Framing** is the way a report’s choices (what to include, what to leave out, what to put first, which words and which pictures to use) shape how readers understand an event, even when every fact in it is true.
:::

| Technique | The choice it makes | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Selection of sources** | which people and quotations to include | a strike story that quotes four managers and no workers |
| **Omission** | which relevant facts to leave out | a medicine’s benefits, without its side effects |
| **Placement** | what comes first, and what is buried at the end | a marathon story that leads with one injury and mentions 20,000 safe finishers in its last line |
| **Word choice** | neutral or loaded words for the same thing | “passionate supporters” or “a rowdy mob” |
| **Choice of image** | which photo readers see first | a careful speech illustrated with the speaker mid-sneeze |

Readers notice the beginning of a story most, so placement matters as much as content. Many people read only the headline and the first sentence.

:::example Finding what a report leaves out
The facts of a story:
- Bus fares will rise by 20% in March.
- The money will pay for forty new electric buses.
- Fares have not risen for eight years.
- Students and pensioners will still travel free.

A report says: *Bus passengers face a 20% fare hike in March, and some are already vowing to abandon the buses for their cars. Officials say the money will pay for forty new electric buses.*

@step Check each fact against the report. The rise, its date and the electric buses all appear.
@step Two facts are missing: fares have not risen for eight years, and students and pensioners still travel free.
@step Notice the loaded words, too: a “hike”, and passengers “vowing to abandon” the buses.
@answer The report leaves out the facts that would make the rise seem fairer, so readers see it as a steep blow to everyone.
:::

:::mistake “Biased” does not mean “false”
A framed report can be accurate in every sentence. Framing works through what is chosen, not through lies, which is why it is easy to miss. To spot it, ask what else could have been said and why it was not.
:::

## Comparing headlines

A headline is the most-read part of any story, and the most compressed. Its few words often carry the whole frame.

| Headline | Effect |
|---|---|
| *Mayor Raises Property Tax 3% to Fund Parks* | **neutral**: states what happened in plain words |
| *Mayor Invests in Greener Parks for Every Neighbourhood* | **favourable**: “invests” and “greener” present a gift to the town |
| *Mayor Hits Homeowners With Tax Hike* | **unfavourable**: “hits” and “hike” present an attack on people’s wallets |

To compare headlines, find the words that judge rather than report. Verbs (*hits*, *abandon*, *invests*) and adjectives (*exhausted*, *brave*) usually carry the judgment.

:::tip Rewrite it neutrally
To test a headline, try rewriting it with plain words. If “Riverside Crumble Again in Cup Final Flop” becomes “Riverside FC Loses Cup Final 2–1”, the words you removed (*crumble*, *again*, *flop*) were doing the judging.

:::summary
- **Framing** is how a report’s choices shape readers’ understanding: selection of sources, omission, placement, word choice and choice of image.
- A framed report can be true in every sentence; look for what was chosen, left out, put first or buried.
- Compare headlines by finding the words that judge rather than report. A neutral headline states what happened in plain words.
:::
