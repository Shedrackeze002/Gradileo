A job application that asks *Who should I contact?* will be understood. One that asks *Whom should I contact?* shows the reader that the writer knows the conventions of formal English. Usage is the set of choices careful writers make between words that are easy to mix up. In this lesson you will practise the most common ones, and then make sure every pronoun you write points clearly to the right word.

## Usage in formal writing

### Who and whom

*Who* is a subject, like *he*; *whom* is an object, like *him*. To choose, look only at the clause the word belongs to and try *he* or *him* in its place.

- *The artist **who** painted the mural is from Accra.* (*she* painted it, so *who*)
- *The scientist **whom** we interviewed works at the observatory.* (we interviewed *her*, so *whom*)
- *To **whom** should I address the letter?* (address it to *him*, so *whom*)

:::mistake Words in between
*The candidate **who** I think will win is Kenji.* The words *I think* are an aside: lift them out and *who* is the subject of *will win*. In the same way, write *Give the prize to **whoever** finishes first*, because *whoever* is the subject of *finishes*, even though the clause comes after *to*.
:::

### Lie and lay

| Meaning | Present | Past | Has … |
|---|---|---|---|
| **lie**: to rest or recline (no object) | lie, lies | lay | lain |
| **lay**: to put something down (needs an object) | lay, lays | laid | laid |

*The cat lies in the sun. Yesterday I lay down for a nap.* But: *Please lay the books on the table. Ravi laid the map on the table.* The past of *lie* is *lay*, which is why the two are so easily confused.

### Fewer and less, between and among

Use **fewer** for things you can count one by one (*fewer students*, *fewer mistakes*) and **less** for amounts (*less time*, *less sugar*). Use **between** for two people or things (*divided between the two winners*) and **among** for a whole group (*the rumour spread among the students*).

### Comparisons, and good and well

- Compare **two** with *-er* or *more*, and **three or more** with *-est* or *most*: *Of the two sisters, Grace is the **taller**.* Never double them: *stronger*, not *more stronger*.
- When something is compared with its own group, add *else* or *other*: *Amara runs faster than anyone **else** in her class.* Without *else*, she would have to be faster than herself.
- *Good* is an adjective; *well* is an adverb: *Samuel played **well**.* After linking verbs such as *is*, *seems*, *smells* and *feels*, use the adjective: *The soup smells **good**.*

:::note Formal and informal
In conversation, *Who did you invite?* and *Of the two, she is the tallest* are common and natural. In formal writing, use *whom* and the comparative. Fixed expressions such as *less than ten kilometres* are standard everywhere.
:::

## Clear pronoun reference

:::definition Antecedent
A pronoun stands in for a noun called its **antecedent**. In *Hana lost her keys*, the antecedent of *her* is *Hana*. Every pronoun should point clearly to one antecedent.
:::

Pronoun reference goes wrong in three ways.

- **Ambiguous**: the pronoun could mean two nouns. *When the glass hit the table, **it** broke.* (The glass or the table?)
- **Vague**: *this*, *that* or *it* points at a whole idea, not a noun. *The council cut bus routes and raised fares. **This** angered residents.* (The cuts, the fares, or both?)
- **Missing**: the pronoun has no antecedent at all. *At the clinic, **they** told me to wait.* (Who?) *In the newspaper, **it** says the bridge will close.* (What does?)

:::example Fixing an ambiguous pronoun
*Priya told Yuki that she had won the prize.* The writer means that Yuki won.
@step Find the pronoun and its possible antecedents: *she* could be Priya or Yuki.
@step Decide what the writer means: Yuki won.
@step Rebuild the sentence so only one reading is possible, by naming the person or quoting the words.
@answer *Priya said to Yuki, “You have won the prize.”*
:::

:::tip Name the noun
The quickest fix is often to replace the pronoun with the noun it means (*the glass broke*), or to add a noun after *this* (*These changes angered residents*).
:::

:::note Singular “they”
*Each student should bring their own lunch* uses *they* for one person whose gender is unknown or not given, and it is correct. *They* is a problem only when the reader cannot tell who it means.
:::

:::summary
- Use *who* for a subject and *whom* for an object: test with *he* and *him* inside the word’s own clause.
- *Lie* (lay, lain) means to rest; *lay* (laid, laid) means to put something down.
- Use *fewer* for things you count and *less* for amounts; *between* for two and *among* for a group.
- Compare two with *-er*, three or more with *-est*; add *else* or *other* when something is compared with its own group; use *well* for how something is done and *good* after linking verbs.
- Every pronoun needs one clear antecedent: fix ambiguous, vague and missing references by naming the noun or rebuilding the sentence.
:::
