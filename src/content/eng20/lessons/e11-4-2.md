In 1846, Henry David Thoreau spent a night in jail for refusing to pay a tax to a government that allowed slavery and was fighting a war he believed unjust. He explained himself in a lecture that became the essay “Civil Disobedience”:

> Under a government which imprisons any unjustly, the true place for a just man is also a prison.
>
> — Henry David Thoreau, “Civil Disobedience” (1849)

The sentence is powerful partly because of what lies behind it: who said it, when, to whom and why. **Rhetorical analysis** studies both: the situation a text answers, and the choices a writer makes to move an audience. In this lesson you will describe a text’s rhetorical situation and explain why a writer makes a particular choice.

## The rhetorical situation

Every persuasive text is written by someone, at some moment, for someone, to do something. A useful way to remember the parts is **SOAPSTone**: Speaker, Occasion, Audience, Purpose, Subject and Tone.

:::definition The rhetorical situation
- **Speaker**: the person or group whose voice the text speaks in. This may be a persona the writer adopts.
- **Occasion**: the event or situation that prompted the text.
- **Audience**: the person or group the text is meant to reach.
- **Purpose**: what the writer wants the text to do to its audience: to inform, persuade, honour, warn, apologize, mock.
- **Subject**: what the text is about.
- **Tone**: the writer’s attitude to the subject, shown by word choice.
:::

:::example Describing a rhetorical situation
A letter to the editor of a town newspaper, written by a nurse at the town’s walk-in clinic the week after the council voted to close it, urges readers to come to a public meeting on Thursday and demand that the clinic stay open. Describe its rhetorical situation.
@step **Speaker:** a nurse who works at the clinic, who knows its value first-hand.
@step **Occasion:** the council’s vote to close the clinic.
@step **Audience:** the readers of the town newspaper, the people who could fill the meeting.
@step **Purpose:** to persuade readers to come to the meeting and fight to keep the clinic open.
@answer **Tone:** urgent and concerned. Each element helps explain the others: a nurse writing straight after the vote, to the whole town, has every reason to sound urgent.
:::

:::mistake Audience is not “anyone who reads it”
Almost any text could be read by anyone. The audience is the group the writer is *trying to reach*, and the text’s details show who that is. A graduation speech is for the graduates and their families, even if the local newspaper prints it.
:::

## Rhetorical choices

Once you know the situation, ask why the writer makes each choice. A strong answer names the choice, then explains its **effect** on the audience and how that serves the **purpose**.

| Choice | What it usually does |
|---|---|
| **Anecdote** (a short true story) | makes a problem real and personal before the argument begins |
| **Concession** (“It is true that …”) | treats the other side fairly, which builds the writer’s credibility |
| **Shift in register** (formal to plain, or the reverse) | jolts readers and makes a point stand out |
| **Direct address** (“you”) | makes each reader or listener feel personally involved |
| **Rhetorical question** | leads the audience to reach the answer for themselves |
| **Short sentence after long ones** | lands a point with sudden, heavy emphasis |
| **Repetition** | stresses an idea and makes it memorable |

Thoreau opens a key passage of “Civil Disobedience” with two questions:

> Must the citizen ever for a moment, or in the least degree, resign his conscience to the legislator? Why has every man a conscience, then? I think that we should be men first, and subjects afterward. It is not desirable to cultivate a respect for the law, so much as for the right.
>
> — Henry David Thoreau, “Civil Disobedience” (1849)

The questions expect the answer “no”. A reader who answers them has agreed with Thoreau before he states his claim.

:::example Explaining a rhetorical choice
In her book of 1792, Mary Wollstonecraft writes:

> My own sex, I hope, will excuse me, if I treat them like rational creatures, instead of flattering their *fascinating* graces, and viewing them as if they were in a state of perpetual childhood, unable to stand alone.
>
> — Mary Wollstonecraft, *A Vindication of the Rights of Woman* (1792)

Why does she address “my own sex” directly?
@step **Name the choice:** direct address to her women readers.
@step **Effect:** she speaks to them as equals, and promises to treat them “like rational creatures”.
@step **Purpose:** her book argues that women should be educated as rational beings. The way she addresses them shows the respect she is arguing for.
@answer To speak to women readers as rational adults, and so practise the respect her argument demands.
:::

:::tip Effect, then purpose
“It is a rhetorical question” names a choice but explains nothing. Always go on: *it makes the reader …*, *which helps the writer …*.
:::

:::summary
- The **rhetorical situation** is the speaker, occasion, audience, purpose, subject and tone of a text (SOAPSTone). Use the details of the text and its context to identify each.
- The **audience** is the group the writer is trying to reach, not everyone who might read it.
- To analyse a **rhetorical choice**, name it, explain its effect on the audience, and connect that effect to the writer’s purpose.
- Common choices: anecdote, concession, shift in register, direct address, rhetorical question, short sentence for emphasis, repetition.
:::
