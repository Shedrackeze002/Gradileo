“Ever since the new mayor took office, it has rained every weekend.” True, perhaps. “So the mayor must be bringing the bad weather.” Not true at all. The first sentence is a fact; the second is a conclusion it cannot support. In Grade 10 you learned the most common fallacies. This lesson adds five subtler ones, and then asks a harder question: when an argument *does* offer evidence, is the evidence any good?

## Five subtler fallacies

:::definition Five fallacies
- **Post hoc** (false cause) assumes that because one thing happened after another, the first caused the second: *I wore my green socks on the day I passed my driving test, so the socks helped me pass.*
- **Circular reasoning** uses its conclusion as its own evidence: *This website is trustworthy, because it says on its home page that it is trustworthy.*
- A **red herring** changes the subject to something irrelevant, drawing attention from the real issue: *Why worry about pollution from the factory when there are so many potholes in our roads?*
- A **false analogy** compares two things that are not alike in the way that matters: *Students should use notes in exams; after all, surgeons look at X-rays while they operate.*
- An **appeal to tradition** claims that something is right because it has always been done that way: *The club has always been for boys only, so girls should not be allowed to join.*
:::

The surgeon analogy fails because an exam tests what the student knows, while an operation is not a test of the surgeon’s memory at all. A good analogy holds in exactly the way the argument needs.

:::example Diagnosing a fallacy
> Asked why the new stadium went over budget, the councillor replied: “Our fans are the most loyal in the country, and they deserve a great place to watch.”

Which fallacy is this?
@step Find the real question: why the stadium cost more than planned.
@step Check whether the answer addresses it. The fans’ loyalty and what they deserve say nothing about the budget.
@step The reply moves attention away from the overspending to a feeling everyone shares.
@answer A **red herring**: the councillor changes the subject instead of answering it.
:::

:::mistake Telling close fallacies apart
- **Post hoc or hasty generalization?** Post hoc is about *cause* (“A happened, then B, so A caused B”). A hasty generalization is about *how many* (“two rude people, so everyone is rude”).
- **Appeal to tradition or bandwagon?** Tradition says “we have always done it”. Bandwagon says “everyone is doing it now”.
- **Red herring or straw man?** A straw man distorts the other side’s argument and attacks the distortion. A red herring ignores the argument and changes the subject.
:::

## Judging evidence

An argument with evidence is not automatically a strong one. Ask three questions of any evidence:

1. **Is it relevant?** Does it actually bear on the claim? A playground’s design award says nothing about whether it is safe.
2. **Is it sufficient?** Are there enough cases? Two plants, three students or one patient cannot support a general claim, however fairly they were chosen.
3. **Is it representative?** Are the cases typical of the whole group the claim is about? A survey of 2,000 members of a cycling club cannot tell you what the whole city thinks about bike lanes, however large it is.

:::definition Correlation and causation
A **correlation** means two things rise and fall together. **Causation** means one makes the other happen. Correlation alone does not prove causation, because a third factor may cause both, or the link may be chance. Ice-cream sales and sunburn rise together, but ice cream does not cause sunburn: hot, sunny weather causes both.
:::

:::

:::example Weighing a piece of evidence
Claim: *Most students at our school are happy with the early start to the day.*
Evidence: *In a survey at the school’s breakfast club, which opens at 7:00 a.m., 85% of the 200 students asked said they were happy with the early start.*

What is wrong with the evidence?
@step Relevant? Yes: it asks students about the start time.
@step Sufficient? 200 students is a reasonable number.
@step Representative? No. Students who choose to come to a 7:00 a.m. breakfast club are early risers, not typical of the whole school.
@answer The sample does not represent the group the claim is about, so it cannot show what most students think.
:::

Strong evidence passes all three tests. A trial in which 20,000 volunteers were randomly given a vaccine or a dummy injection is relevant, large and fairly chosen. Choosing who gets the vaccine at random also rules out a hidden third factor, which is why such a trial can show causation and not just correlation.

:::summary
- **Post hoc** confuses sequence with cause; **circular reasoning** assumes its conclusion; a **red herring** changes the subject; a **false analogy** compares things unlike in the way that matters; an **appeal to tradition** relies on “we have always done it”.
- Judge evidence by asking whether it is **relevant**, **sufficient** and **representative**.
- **Correlation** is not **causation**: two things can rise together because a third thing causes both.
:::
