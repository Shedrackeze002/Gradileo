This lesson quotes Jonathan Swift’s “A Modest Proposal” and Mark Twain’s “The War Prayer”, two satires that describe cruelty and war in deliberately shocking terms in order to condemn them.
:::

In 1729, with Ireland in the grip of famine, a pamphlet appeared that calmly proposed a solution to Irish poverty:

> I have been assured by a very knowing American of my acquaintance in London, that a young healthy child well nursed, is, at a year old, a most delicious nourishing and wholesome food, whether stewed, roasted, baked, or boiled …
>
> — Jonathan Swift, “A Modest Proposal” (1729)

Swift did not want anyone to eat children. He wanted readers to be so horrified by his narrator’s cool reasoning that they would see how coldly the poor were already being treated. That is **satire**: humour, irony and exaggeration used as weapons of criticism. In this lesson you will find the **target** of a satire and name the **tools** it uses.

## What satire is

:::definition Satire
**Satire** uses humour, irony, exaggeration or mock seriousness to expose and criticize the foolishness or wrongdoing of a person, group, institution or idea: its **target**. Its aim is not only to amuse but to make readers judge the target, and ideally to change it.
:::

The target is not always what the words seem to be about. Swift’s pamphlet *seems* to be about cooking children; its target is the landlords and rulers who let Ireland’s poor starve:

> I grant this food will be somewhat dear, and therefore very proper for landlords, who, as they have already devoured most of the parents, seem to have the best title to the children.
>
> — Jonathan Swift, “A Modest Proposal” (1729)

To say that landlords have “devoured most of the parents” is to accuse them of already consuming the poor through rents and greed.

:::example Finding the target
Mark Twain’s “The War Prayer” begins as a nation goes to war:

> It was a time of great and exalting excitement. The country was up in arms, the war was on, in every breast burned the holy fire of patriotism; the drums were beating, the bands playing, the toy pistols popping, the bunched firecrackers hissing and spluttering …
>
> — Mark Twain, “The War Prayer” (1916)

A congregation prays for victory, and a stranger then speaks aloud the unspoken half of their prayer. What is the target?
@step Ask whom readers are meant to judge. Not the enemy, and not the soldiers.
@step The festival mood (drums, bands, firecrackers) and the prayer for victory belong to the cheering crowd at home.
@step The stranger’s speech shows that a prayer for victory is also a prayer for the enemy’s suffering.
@answer The target is the excited, unthinking patriotism that prays for victory without thinking about what victory costs others.
:::

## The tools of satire

:::definition Five tools
- **Verbal irony** says the opposite of what the writer means, often as mock praise: *What a brilliant idea to hold the outdoor festival in the rainy season.*
- **Exaggeration** stretches a fault until it becomes absurd: *The rules are so strict that students need a permission slip to blink.*
- **Understatement** describes something serious as if it were minor: *The flood that swept away half the village was “a moderate inconvenience to residents”.*
- **Parody** imitates the style of a recognizable kind of text (a fairy tale, a recipe, a nature documentary) in order to mock its subject.
- **Mock seriousness** treats something trivial or absurd in a grave, formal, solemn manner: *After eighteen months of careful study, the board has decided that the lunch queue will form on the left.*
:::

Twain turns the congregation’s prayer inside out by spelling out what victory means:

> O Lord our God, help us to tear their soldiers to bloody shreds with our shells; help us to cover their smiling fields with the pale forms of their patriot dead …
>
> — Mark Twain, “The War Prayer” (1916)

:::tip The tools overlap
Satirists combine their tools. Understatement is often ironic, and Swift’s calm voice is at once mock-serious and ironic. When a question asks which tool a passage **mainly** uses, look for the one doing most of the work: a stretched fault (exaggeration), a shrunken disaster (understatement), an imitated form (parody), or solemn treatment of something silly (mock seriousness).
:::

Stephen Leacock satirized the health fads of his day with a straight face:

> Jiggins is dead. He was, of course, a pioneer, but the fact that he dumb-belled himself to death at an early age does not prevent a whole generation of young men from following in his path.
>
> — Stephen Leacock, “How to Live to Be 200” (1910)

The flat, matter-of-fact report of a death caused by too much exercise is understatement, and its target is the craze for extreme health habits.

:::mistake Reading satire literally
The most common mistake is to take a satirist at their word: to think that Swift wants children eaten, or that Leacock admires Jiggins. If a text praises something absurd, or proposes something monstrous in a calm voice, ask what the writer really wants you to think.
:::

:::summary
- **Satire** uses humour, irony and exaggeration to criticize a **target**: a person, group, institution or idea. The target may not be what the words seem to be about.
- Its main **tools** are verbal irony, exaggeration, understatement, parody and mock seriousness. They often overlap; name the one doing most of the work.
- Never read satire literally. Ask whom the writer wants you to judge, and why.
:::
