Many Grade 11 essays draw on outside sources: a study, a report, an expert’s article. Using sources well is a skill of honesty as much as of writing. You must show which ideas are yours and which are borrowed, report each source accurately, and then do something a list of quotations cannot do: connect the sources into an argument of your own. In this lesson the sources are invented for practice; the skills are the ones you will use with real ones.

## Quote, paraphrase or summarize

:::definition Three ways to use a source
- A **quotation** uses the source’s exact words, inside quotation marks.
- A **paraphrase** restates *all* of a passage’s ideas in your own words and sentence structure, at about the same length.
- A **summary** gives only the *main idea* of a passage, in your own words, and is much shorter.

All three need **attribution**: you must say where the idea comes from.
:::

Quote when the exact wording matters (a striking phrase, a definition, words you will analyse). Paraphrase when the ideas matter but the wording does not. Summarize when you need only the gist.

## Paraphrasing without plagiarism

Here is a passage from an invented source, a report by the Lakeside Youth Health Centre:

> Teenagers who sleep fewer than eight hours on school nights are more likely to report trouble concentrating in morning lessons. The effect is strongest after nights when they used a phone or computer in bed.

An **acceptable paraphrase** changes the words *and* the structure, keeps the meaning, and gives credit:

> The Lakeside Youth Health Centre found that students who get less than eight hours of sleep before a school day often say they struggle to focus in their first classes, especially if they were on a screen in bed the night before.

:::definition Patchwriting
**Patchwriting** keeps the source’s sentence structure and swaps in a few synonyms. Even with the source named, it is plagiarism, because the writing is still mostly the source’s.
:::

> *Patchwriting:* Teens who sleep less than eight hours on school nights are more likely to report difficulty concentrating in morning classes, and the effect is strongest after nights when they used a phone or computer in bed, says the Lakeside Youth Health Centre.

:::example Paraphrasing in four steps
Paraphrase the Lakeside passage above.
@step Read the passage until you understand it, then cover it up.
@step Write the ideas from memory, in your own sentence structure.
@step Uncover the source and compare: change any phrase that is still too close, and check that the meaning has not shifted.
@step Add the attribution.
@answer *The Lakeside Youth Health Centre found that students who get less than eight hours of sleep before a school day often say they struggle to focus in their first classes, especially if they were on a screen in bed the night before.*
:::

:::mistake Four ways a paraphrase goes wrong
- **Copying**: the source’s exact words without quotation marks.
- **Patchwriting**: the source’s structure with a few synonyms swapped in.
- **No credit**: your own words, but no mention of where the idea came from.
- **A changed meaning**: “using a phone in bed is the main reason teenagers fall asleep in class” is a claim the source never made.
:::

:::note Citation styles
Schools and subjects use different citation styles for naming sources in a list or in brackets. Follow the style your teacher asks for. Whatever the style, the rule is the same: every borrowed idea and every borrowed phrase is credited.
:::

## Synthesis

When you use several sources, it is tempting to report them one after another: *Source A says… Source B says…* That is a list, not an argument.

:::definition Synthesis
**Synthesis** connects several sources into one point of your own. It shows how the sources relate: whether they support each other, explain each other, or complicate each other, and it represents each one accurately.
:::

Suppose you have two invented findings:

- **Source A**, a sleep researcher: Teenagers’ body clocks shift later in adolescence, so many cannot fall asleep before eleven at night.
- **Source B**, a district report: When two schools moved their start time from 8:00 to 9:00, attendance at first-period classes rose by six per cent.

> *A list:* Source A says teenagers cannot fall asleep before eleven. Source B says attendance rose when two schools started at 9:00.
>
> *Synthesis:* Because teenagers’ body clocks make early sleep difficult (Source A), it is not surprising that a later start brought more students to first-period classes (Source B).

The synthesis does what the list cannot: it explains *why* the two findings belong together.

:::check e11-synthesis 1
:::

:::mistake Making sources say more than they do
“Both sources prove that a later start makes every student sleep more and get better marks” goes far beyond the findings: neither source measured marks, and neither found an effect on *every* student. Synthesis connects sources; it never stretches them.
:::

:::check e11-synthesis 2
:::

When one source **qualifies** the others (it limits, complicates or explains an exception), a strong synthesis says so instead of smoothing it over: *Online courses lose more students than classroom courses, but regular contact with a tutor seems to close that gap.*

:::

:::summary
- Quote for exact wording, paraphrase for all the ideas in your own words, and summarize for the main idea only; credit every source.
- An acceptable paraphrase changes both the words and the structure and keeps the meaning; copying, patchwriting, missing credit and a changed meaning are all failures.
- Synthesis connects sources into one point of your own and represents each accurately; listing sources is not synthesis.
- When a source qualifies the others, a good synthesis says so.
:::
