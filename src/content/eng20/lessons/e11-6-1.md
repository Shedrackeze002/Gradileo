In Grade 10 you learned that an analytical essay asks *how* a text works, not *what happens* in it. In Grade 11 the bar rises. A strong literary analysis names the writer’s choices precisely, explains how those choices work on a reader, and says why that matters: what the text reveals about people or the world. In this lesson you will write a thesis that does all of that, and commentary that goes deeper than paraphrase or labelling devices.

## The analytical thesis

:::definition Analytical thesis
An **analytical thesis** is an arguable claim about *how* a text creates meaning and *why* it matters. It names a **choice** the writer makes (an image, a narrator, a structure, an irony) and the **meaning** that choice creates.
:::

A useful pattern is: *[Writer] uses [a specific choice] to [create an effect], showing / suggesting [why it matters].* You will not always use those words, but a strong thesis contains all three parts.

| Thesis | What is wrong |
|---|---|
| Freedom and marriage in “The Story of an Hour” | A topic, not a claim. |
| Mrs. Mallard hears that her husband has died, feels free, and dies when he walks in alive. | A plot summary. |
| “The Story of an Hour” is a powerful and moving story. | Praise with nothing specific to prove. |
| “The Story of an Hour” shows that marriage can take away a woman’s freedom. | A meaning with no *how*: the essay would drift into a discussion of marriage instead of the story. |
| Kate Chopin uses imagery, irony and symbolism in “The Story of an Hour”. | A list of devices with no *why*: so what do they show? |

:::example Building an analytical thesis
Strengthen this thesis: *“The Story of an Hour” shows that marriage can take away a woman’s freedom.*
@step Ask *how* the story shows this. The turning point comes at the window, where Mrs. Mallard looks out at spring after hearing of her husband’s death.
@step Name the choice precisely: the spring scene outside the window, seen in her first hour as a widow.
@step Join the choice to the meaning, and say what it reveals about her marriage.
@answer *Through the spring scene outside Mrs. Mallard’s window, Kate Chopin turns an hour of mourning into an awakening, suggesting that the marriage she mourns had quietly confined her.*
:::

:::

:::tip Ask “How?” and “So what?”
Read your thesis and ask *How does the writer do that?* If it has no answer, add the technique. Then ask *So what?* If it has no answer, add what the technique shows. A thesis that survives both questions is ready.

## Commentary that goes deep

Evidence proves nothing on its own; your **commentary** (the analysis after a quotation) does the proving. Commentary can sit on four levels, from shallow to deep:

1. **Paraphrase**: repeats the quotation in other words. *This shows that she can see the trees moving outside.*
2. **Device-spotting**: names a technique and stops. *This is an example of imagery.*
3. **Word-level effect**: explains how particular words work. *The word “aquiver” makes the trees tremble with barely contained energy.*
4. **Effect linked to the point**: explains how the words work *and* ties that effect back to the paragraph’s claim.

Only the fourth level proves your point. Here is a model body paragraph on “The Story of an Hour”: the point in sentence 1, the evidence in sentence 2, and commentary at the deepest level in sentences 3 and 4.

> (1) Chopin uses the view from the window to show Mrs. Mallard’s grief giving way to a sense of new life. (2) Looking out, she sees “the tops of trees that were all aquiver with the new spring life” and notices “the delicious breath of rain.” (3) The word “aquiver” makes the trees tremble with barely contained energy, and “delicious” turns the rain into something to savour, not mourn. (4) Because this is what she sees in her first hour as a widow, the scene hints that a new life is stirring inside her before she can admit it to herself.

:::check e11-analysis-depth 1
:::

:::mistake Naming the device and stopping
“This is a metaphor” or “Chopin uses imagery” earns little credit on its own: every reader can spot a device. The marks are in what comes next: *which words, what effect, and why it matters to your claim.*
:::

:::mistake Claiming more than the evidence shows
Deep commentary stays honest. “This proves that Mrs. Mallard was glad her husband died from the very first moment” goes beyond the text: the story says she wept at once. Claim only what the words support, and use words such as *suggests* and *hints* when the text implies rather than states.
:::

:::

## Close reading: where to look

When you are not sure what to say about a quotation, look at it through one of these lenses:

- **Diction**: Which single word carries the most weight? What are its connotations?
- **Imagery**: Which senses does it appeal to, and what feeling does the image create?
- **Sound and rhythm**: Is there repetition, a sudden short sentence, a list that builds?
- **Contrast**: What two things are set side by side, and what does the gap between them show?
- **Position**: Where does this moment fall in the story, and why does that matter?

:::example From quotation to commentary
Write deep commentary on this evidence from “To Build a Fire”: the man was *“quick and alert in the things of life, but only in the things, and not in the significances.”*
@step Find the key words: “the things” and “the significances”, set against each other.
@step Explain how they work: the man notices facts, such as the temperature, but never asks what they mean for him.
@step Tie it to the point: this is exactly why he ignores a danger that the reader can already see.
@answer *The contrast between “the things” and “the significances” shows that the man notices facts without asking what they mean for him, which is exactly why he ignores a danger the reader can already see.*
:::

:::check e11-analysis-depth 3
:::

:::note How much to quote
Short quotations work best: a phrase or a single sentence, worked into your own sentence. A long quotation leaves less room for commentary, and commentary is where your marks are.
:::

:::summary
- An analytical thesis names a specific choice the writer makes and what that choice shows; a topic, a summary, praise, a meaning with no *how* and a list of devices with no *why* all fall short.
- Test a thesis by asking “How?” and “So what?”.
- Commentary goes deepest when it explains how particular words work *and* ties that effect to the paragraph’s point.
- Paraphrase, device-spotting and overreaching claims do not prove a point.
:::
