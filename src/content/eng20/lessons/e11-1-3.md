Every Sunday, Miss Brill sits in the public gardens and watches the crowd. Katherine Mansfield tells her story in the third person, yet listen to this:

> Oh, how fascinating it was! How she enjoyed it! How she loved sitting here, watching it all! It was like a play. It was exactly like a play.
>
> — Katherine Mansfield, “Miss Brill” (1920)

The narrator is not the one who is fascinated; Miss Brill is. Her breathless exclamations have slipped into the narration. Who tells a story, and in whose words, decides what we see and what we believe. In this lesson you will analyze **narrative voice** and judge how far a narrator can be **trusted**.

## Narrative voice

:::definition Narrative voice
**Point of view** is the position a story is told from: first person (“I”), or third person, limited to one character or all-knowing (omniscient). **Narrative voice** is the personality we hear in the telling: its vocabulary, its attitude and its rhythm.
:::

A third-person narrator can tell us a character’s thoughts in several ways. Compare these, all about the same moment:

| Method | Example |
|---|---|
| **Direct thought** | “I’m late again,” Ravi thought. |
| **Reported thought** | Ravi realized that he was late again. |
| **Free indirect style** | Late again! What would Ms. Grant say this time? |
| **Plain narration** | Ravi ran up the stairs. |

:::definition Free indirect style
**Free indirect style** (or free indirect discourse) blends a character’s thoughts into the narration. It stays in the third person and past tense, but it uses the character’s own words, feelings and judgements, with no quotation marks and no tag such as “she thought”.
:::

Free indirect style lets the reader stand very close to a character while the narrator stays quietly in charge. That double view makes irony possible: Miss Brill thinks the park is “exactly like a play”, and we will soon see that she is part of the show in a way she does not expect.

:::check e11-narrative-voice 1
:::

:::mistake Third person is not always the narrator’s opinion
A sentence in the third person can still carry a character’s view. If a sentence judges, exclaims or asks a question in a way that sounds like a character, ask whose words they are. A judgement in free indirect style belongs to the character, not to the author.
:::

## Voices inside the telling

A first-person narrator often tells the story years later. In “Araby”, an adult looks back on his boyhood crush. The boy’s grand romantic feelings and the man’s wry distance sit in the same sentence:

> These noises converged in a single sensation of life for me: I imagined that I bore my chalice safely through a throng of foes.
>
> — James Joyce, “Araby” (1914)

The boy pictured himself as a knight on a holy quest through the busy market. The man telling the story knows he only “imagined” it.

:::example Two voices in a frame story
In “The Celebrated Jumping Frog of Calaveras County”, an educated narrator describes old Simon Wheeler, who then tells a long tale about a gambler named Jim Smiley. Compare their voices:

> To me, the spectacle of a man drifting serenely along through such a queer yarn without ever smiling, was exquisitely absurd.
>
> — Mark Twain, “The Celebrated Jumping Frog of Calaveras County” (1865)

> why, if there was two birds setting on a fence, he would bet you which one would fly first;
>
> — Mark Twain, “The Celebrated Jumping Frog of Calaveras County” (1865)

How do the two voices differ, and what is the effect?
@step Describe the narrator’s voice: long, formal words (“spectacle”, “serenely”, “exquisitely absurd”) and an amused, superior attitude.
@step Describe Wheeler’s voice: everyday speech and dialect (“there was two birds”, “setting” for sitting) and a perfectly serious attitude.
@step Ask what the contrast does: the stiff narrator and the rambling storyteller bounce off each other.
@answer The narrator is formal and superior, and Wheeler is folksy and solemn. Setting the two voices side by side is the joke: the reader enjoys Wheeler’s tale and the narrator’s stuffy impatience with it.
:::

Narrators can also change how they speak. At the climax of “A White Heron”, the narrator suddenly speaks straight to Sylvia, in the present tense, as if standing beside her.

## Reliable and unreliable narrators

:::definition Unreliable narrator
An **unreliable narrator** is one whose account the reader cannot fully trust. The narrator may be naive (a child who misunderstands), biased (holding a grudge), self-deceiving (whose actions contradict their claims) or mentally unwell. Most narrators are **reliable**: they report events plainly and fairly.
:::

“The Yellow Wallpaper” is written as the secret journal of a woman ordered to rest by her husband, John, a doctor. At first we trust her account of the facts:

> I don’t like our room a bit. I wanted one downstairs that opened on the piazza and had roses all over the window, and such pretty old-fashioned chintz hangings! but John would not hear of it.
>
> — Charlotte Perkins Gilman, “The Yellow Wallpaper” (1892)

We may already read her situation differently from her, though. She calls John “very careful and loving”, while we notice that he decides everything for her. As the weeks pass, the pattern in the paper becomes a shape, then a woman behind bars, and finally the narrator believes she has come out of the wallpaper herself. Her facts and her perceptions drift apart, and the reader has to read past her words.

:::check e11-reliability 1
:::

:::example Spotting a narrator who contradicts themselves
> I am the calmest person in my family. When my brother borrowed my bike without asking, I didn’t say a word. I just let the air out of his tires and hid his phone.

Should the reader trust what this narrator says about themselves?
@step Find the claim: the narrator says they are “the calmest person” in the family.
@step Find the actions: they let the air out of the tires and hide the phone.
@answer No. The narrator’s actions contradict their claim, so the reader trusts what they do over what they say about themselves.
:::

:::tip The gap is the point
Unreliable narration works through a gap between what the narrator says and what the reader understands. Find the gap, then ask what causes it: innocence, bias, self-deception or illness.
:::

:::summary
- Narrative voice is the personality in the telling. A story may hold several voices, like the narrator and Wheeler in “The Celebrated Jumping Frog”.
- Free indirect style blends a character’s thoughts into third-person narration with no tag; it brings the reader close and makes irony possible.
- A narrator looking back, as in “Araby”, can show both the younger self’s feelings and the older self’s judgement.
- An unreliable narrator may be naive, biased, self-deceiving or unwell. Look for the gap between what they say and what the reader understands.
:::
