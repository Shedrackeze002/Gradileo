At the start of “The Bride Comes to Yellow Sky”, a newly married couple ride west across Texas in a luxurious railway carriage. Stephen Crane could have written “the train went fast”. Instead:

> The great Pullman was whirling onward with such dignity of motion that a glance from the window seemed simply to prove that the plains of Texas were pouring eastward.
>
> — Stephen Crane, “The Bride Comes to Yellow Sky” (1898)

“Dignity of motion” is a grand phrase for a train, and the joke that the train sits still while Texas pours past makes us smile at the couple’s awe. Every word is a choice, and so is every sentence shape. In this lesson you will explain how **diction** creates **tone**, and how **syntax** creates effects.

## Diction

:::definition Diction
**Diction** is a writer’s choice of words. Every word has a **denotation** (its dictionary meaning) and often a **connotation** (the feelings and associations it carries). Diction can also be formal or informal, plain or elaborate, concrete or abstract.
:::

| Positive connotation | Neutral | Negative connotation |
|---|---|---|
| slender | thin | scrawny |
| thrifty | careful with money | stingy |
| confident | sure of herself | arrogant |
| curious | interested | nosy |

The words in each row point to the same thing, but each one makes the reader feel differently about it.

## Tone

:::definition Tone
**Tone** is the attitude of the writer or narrator towards the subject: admiring, bitter, playful, reverent, sarcastic, nostalgic, clinical, ironic and so on. Tone is built mostly from diction: the words that judge, exaggerate, soften or understate.
:::

:::example Finding the tone
“Araby” opens on the dead-end street where the boy lives:

> North Richmond Street, being blind, was a quiet street except at the hour when the Christian Brothers’ School set the boys free. An uninhabited house of two storeys stood at the blind end, detached from its neighbours in a square ground. The other houses of the street, conscious of decent lives within them, gazed at one another with brown imperturbable faces.
>
> — James Joyce, “Araby” (1914)

What is the tone of the last sentence, and which words create it?
@step Find the words that carry an attitude: the houses are “conscious of decent lives within them”, and their faces are “brown” and “imperturbable” (calm and unbothered).
@step Ask what the attitude is: the houses are made to look smug, proud of being respectable, and dull.
@answer The tone is quietly mocking. Joyce’s diction turns the houses into self-satisfied neighbours who stare at each other with dull, unbothered brown faces: a street where nothing exciting happens.
:::

:::

:::mistake Tone is not mood
**Tone** is the writer’s or narrator’s attitude; **mood** is the feeling the reader gets. They often match, but not always: a narrator can describe a frightening scene in a calm, flat tone. Also choose precise tone words. “Sad” and “happy” are rarely enough; try “bitter”, “wistful”, “reverent” or “mocking”.
:::

## Syntax

:::definition Syntax
**Syntax** is the way words are arranged into sentences: their length, their order, and the patterns they make. Writers shape sentences to control pace, build suspense and stress ideas.
:::

| Technique | What it looks like | A common effect |
|---|---|---|
| **Short sentences** | The door opened. No one was there. | Speed, shock or emphasis |
| **Periodic sentence** | The main clause is held back to the end. | Suspense, then a strong finish |
| **Sentence fragments** | Silence. A step. Another. | Snapshots; tension or emotion |
| **Anaphora** | The same words begin several clauses. | Builds feeling and stresses an idea |
| **Polysyndeton** | Many “and”s join a list. | Piles things up; feels endless or breathless |
| **A run of questions** | Questions follow one another. | Doubt, panic or confusion |
| **Inversion** | The usual word order is reversed. | Puts weight on the word held back |

:::example How syntax shapes a moment
Near the end of “The Garden Party”, Laura goes into the cottage where the young carter lies dead:

> There lay a young man, fast asleep—sleeping so soundly, so deeply, that he was far, far away from them both. Oh, so remote, so peaceful. He was dreaming.
>
> — Katherine Mansfield, “The Garden Party” (1922)

How does the sentence structure shape Laura’s experience of this moment?
@step Describe the syntax: an inverted opening (“There lay a young man”), repetition (“so soundly, so deeply”, “far, far away”), a fragment (“Oh, so remote, so peaceful.”), then a very short sentence.
@step Ask what each feature does: the repetitions slow the pace to a hush, and the fragment sounds like Laura’s own wondering thought.
@answer The slow, repeating rhythm and the quiet fragment create a sense of calm and awe. Laura sees death not as horror but as deep, distant peace, and the short final sentence, “He was dreaming.”, ends the moment in stillness.
:::

:::tip Technique, example, effect
Naming a technique is only the first step. A strong answer names the technique, quotes the example, and explains the effect on the reader. “The fragments isolate each sound, so the tension rises” says much more than “there are fragments”.

:::summary
- Diction is word choice. Connotation, the feeling a word carries, does much of the work.
- Tone is the writer’s or narrator’s attitude, built mostly from diction. Mood is the reader’s feeling. Use precise tone words.
- Syntax is sentence structure: length, order and pattern. Short sentences, periodic sentences, fragments, anaphora, polysyndeton, questions and inversion each create effects.
- Always connect a technique to its effect: technique, example, effect.
:::
