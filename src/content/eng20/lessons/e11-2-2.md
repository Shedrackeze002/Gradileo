In the summer of 1919, mobs attacked Black neighbourhoods in cities across the United States. Claude McKay, a Jamaican-born poet living in New York, answered with a poem of fourteen lines:

> If we must die, let it not be like hogs\
> Hunted and penned in an inglorious spot,\
> While round us bark the mad and hungry dogs,\
> Making their mock at our accursèd lot.
>
> — Claude McKay, “If We Must Die” (1919)

McKay chose one of the oldest and strictest forms in English poetry, the **sonnet**, for one of the angriest subjects. In this lesson you will learn the two main kinds of sonnet, find the **volta** where a sonnet turns, and hear how poets bend the steady beat of the line to make it speak.

## Fourteen lines, two shapes

:::definition Sonnet
A **sonnet** is a poem of fourteen lines with a set rhyme scheme. In English, sonnets are usually written in **iambic pentameter**: five iambs (da-DUM) to a line, as in Milton’s line “they AL | so SERVE | who ON | ly STAND | and WAIT”.
:::

The two main kinds divide those fourteen lines differently.

| | Petrarchan (Italian) | Shakespearean (English) |
|---|---|---|
| Parts | an **octave** (8 lines) and a **sestet** (6 lines) | three **quatrains** (4 lines each) and a **couplet** (2 lines) |
| Rhyme scheme | ABBAABBA, then CDECDE or CDCDCD | ABAB CDCD EFEF GG |
| Where it turns | usually at line 9, where the sestet begins | often at the couplet, sometimes at line 9 |

Francesco Petrarca (Petrarch) made the first form famous in fourteenth-century Italy. When English poets of the 1500s borrowed it, they found that English has far fewer rhyming words than Italian, so they loosened the pattern to seven rhyme sounds instead of four or five. Shakespeare’s sonnets made that English form famous.

To find the rhyme scheme, write the last word of each line and give the same letter to the lines that rhyme. McKay’s poem rhymes *hogs, spot, dogs, lot*: ABAB. The next quatrain rhymes *die, shed, defy, dead*: CDCD. Three quatrains and a closing couplet make it Shakespearean.

## The volta

:::definition Volta
The **volta** (Italian for “turn”) is the point where a sonnet’s thought or feeling shifts: a problem meets its answer, a complaint becomes a wish, a description becomes a plea. Words such as *but*, *yet*, *then* and *now* often mark it.
:::

John Milton went blind in his forties. In a Petrarchan sonnet he asks how he can serve God now. Here is the moment it turns:

> “Doth God exact day-labor, light denied?”\
> I fondly ask. But Patience, to prevent\
> That murmur, soon replies, “God doth not need\
> Either man’s work or his own gifts.
>
> — John Milton, “When I consider how my light is spent” (1673)

The octave is a worried question; at “But Patience” the sestet’s answer begins, half a line early, as if Patience cannot wait to interrupt.

:::example Finding the turn in a Petrarchan sonnet
Gerard Manley Hopkins wrote this octave and sestet. Where does the poem turn, and what changes?
> Generations have trod, have trod, have trod;\
> And all is seared with trade; bleared, smeared with toil;\
> And wears man’s smudge and shares man’s smell: the soil\
> Is bare now, nor can foot feel, being shod.
>
> And for all this, nature is never spent;\
> There lives the dearest freshness deep down things;
>
> — Gerard Manley Hopkins, “God’s Grandeur” (1918)
@step Count the lines. These are lines 5 to 10, so the stanza break comes after line 8: the end of the octave.
@step Look for a signal word at line 9: “And for all this” answers everything said before it.
@step Compare the moods. The octave mourns an earth “seared with trade”; the sestet insists that “the dearest freshness” still lives “deep down things”.
@answer The poem turns at line 9, from grief at how people have scarred the earth to hope that nature is always renewed.
:::

In a Shakespearean sonnet the couplet often delivers the final turn, or clinches the argument. McKay ends with a promise:

> Like men we’ll face the murderous, cowardly pack,\
> Pressed to the wall, dying, but fighting back!
>
> — Claude McKay, “If We Must Die” (1919)

## Bending the beat

A sonnet that kept a perfect da-DUM through all fourteen lines would sound like a metronome. Poets keep the pattern but vary it, and the variations draw attention to the words they touch.

:::definition Variations in metre
- A **trochee** (DUM-da) reverses the iamb. A **trochaic inversion** puts one at the start of a line, so the first word lands hard.
- A **spondee** (DUM-DUM) puts two stresses side by side and slows the line.
- A **caesura** is a pause inside a line, usually at a full stop, semicolon or comma.
- An **end-stopped** line ends with a pause; in **enjambment** the sentence runs on past the line break.
:::

:::example Hearing a trochaic inversion
Wordsworth opens a sonnet about people who have lost touch with nature. Scan the second line.
> The world is too much with us; late and soon,\
> Getting and spending, we lay waste our powers:
>
> — William Wordsworth, “The world is too much with us” (1807)
@step Say the line aloud and listen for the stressed syllables: GET-ting and SPEND-ing.
@step An iambic line should begin da-DUM. This one begins DUM-da, “GET-ting”: a trochee.
@step Ask what the stress does. It lands on the very activity the poem condemns, so the busy, grasping life of “Getting and spending” hits the reader first.
@answer The line opens with a trochaic inversion, “Getting”, which stresses the restless buying and selling Wordsworth blames.
:::

Enjambment and caesura work together. Milton runs one sentence over the end of a line, then stops it short:

> And that one talent which is death to hide\
> Lodged with me useless, though my soul more bent
>
> — John Milton, “When I consider how my light is spent” (1673)

The sentence will not wait at “hide”; it tumbles into the next line, where “Lodged” takes a trochaic stress and the comma after “useless” makes a caesura. The gift is stuck, and the line stalls with it.

:::

:::mistake Inventing variations
Do not hunt for a trochee in every line. Read the line naturally first, and mark a variation only where your voice clearly stresses a syllable the pattern would leave light. Most lines in a good sonnet are regular, which is exactly why the variations stand out.
:::

:::summary
- A sonnet has fourteen lines, usually in iambic pentameter.
- A Petrarchan sonnet has an octave (ABBAABBA) and a sestet, and usually turns at line 9. A Shakespearean sonnet has three quatrains (ABAB CDCD EFEF) and a couplet (GG), which often clinches or twists the argument.
- The volta is the turn in thought or feeling; look for *but*, *yet*, *then* or *now*, or a change of mood.
- Poets vary the metre for emphasis: a trochee (often at the start of a line), a spondee, a caesura inside the line, and enjambment across the line break.
:::
