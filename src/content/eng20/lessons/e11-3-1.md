A storm, a lonely place, and three witches who will meet again “when the battle’s lost and won”. Before they vanish, they chant together:

> Fair is foul, and foul is fair:\
> Hover through the fog and filthy air.
>
> — The Witches, *Macbeth*, Act 1, Scene 1

William Shakespeare wrote *Macbeth* in about 1606, and it is the shortest of his tragedies. A Scottish general, Macbeth, meets three witches who tell him he will be king; urged on by his wife, he murders King Duncan, takes the crown, and rules by fear until his enemies close in. The Witches’ riddle sets the tone: in this play nothing is quite what it seems. In this lesson you will learn to paraphrase Shakespeare’s language and to follow the patterns of images that run through the play.

## Verse, prose and the Witches’ chant

Most of *Macbeth* is written in **blank verse**: unrhymed lines of about ten syllables with five beats, in a da-DUM rhythm. Macbeth’s very first line in the play is a good example, and it echoes the Witches before he has even met them:

> So foul and fair a day I have not seen.
>
> — Macbeth, *Macbeth*, Act 1, Scene 3

Read it aloud and you hear the beats: so FOUL and FAIR a DAY i HAVE not SEEN. Nobles speak in verse. The drunken Porter speaks in **prose**, ordinary sentences with no set rhythm, and so does Lady Macbeth when she walks in her sleep in Act 5: the queen who once spoke in commanding verse has lost control of her mind and her words. The Witches are different again. They chant in short, rhyming lines with four strong beats, like a spell:

> Double, double, toil and trouble;\
> Fire, burn; and cauldron, bubble.
>
> — The Witches, *Macbeth*, Act 4, Scene 1

:::tip Read to the punctuation, not to the end of the line
Shakespeare’s sentences often run over several lines. Read on until you reach a full stop, a question mark or a semicolon, and only then ask what the sentence means.
:::

## Paraphrasing Macbeth

:::definition Paraphrase
A **paraphrase** puts a passage into your own plain words, keeping every idea and image and adding nothing of your own. It is about as long as the original; a summary is much shorter.
:::

A few words come up again and again in *Macbeth*, and some have changed their meaning since 1606.

| Word | Meaning | Word | Meaning |
|---|---|---|---|
| **thane** | a Scottish lord | **issue** | children |
| **ere** | before | **surcease** | an ending; death |
| **durst** | dared | **physic** | medicine |
| **chuck** | dear (a pet name) | **incarnadine** | to stain red |
| **sere** | dry, withered | **palter** | to play tricks, deal falsely |

:::example Paraphrasing a hard passage
In Act 1, Scene 3, Macbeth has just learned that he is Thane of Cawdor, as the Witches foretold. Already he is thinking about how the rest of their prophecy might come true. Paraphrase his words: “Present fears / Are less than horrible imaginings. / My thought, whose murder yet is but fantastical, / Shakes so my single state of man / That function is smother’d in surmise, / And nothing is but what is not.”
@step Read to the full stops. There are two sentences, and the first is short: “Present fears / Are less than horrible imaginings.”
@step Replace the hard words. “Present fears” are real dangers in front of him; “fantastical” means imagined; his “single state of man” is his whole self; “function” is his power to act; “surmise” is imagining.
@step Put every idea into plain modern English, in the same order.
@answer Real dangers frighten me less than the horrible things I imagine. The murder I am thinking of is still only a fantasy, yet it shakes my whole being so badly that I cannot act, and nothing seems real to me except what has not happened yet.
:::

:::mistake Do not explain instead of paraphrasing
A paraphrase keeps the images. When Macbeth says, “Stars, hide your fires!”, the plain version is “Stars, stop shining”, not “It is getting dark”: he is asking the night to hide his wishes. Save your interpretation for the next sentence, after the paraphrase.
:::

:::definition Equivocation
**Equivocation** is saying something with two meanings, so that the listener believes the false one while the speaker can claim to have told the truth. The drunken Porter jokes about an “equivocator” who could “swear in both the scales against either scale”, and many scholars think his joke points at a famous treason trial of 1606, in which a priest had defended equivocating under oath. Watch for double meanings: the Witches’ promises will turn out to be equivocations.
:::

## Imagery and motif

:::definition Imagery and motif
**Imagery** is language that appeals to the senses: what we see, hear, smell, touch or taste. A **motif** is an image or idea that returns again and again in a work, gathering meaning each time.
:::

*Macbeth* is built from a handful of motifs. Learn to spot them, and ask each time what has changed.

| Pattern | What it comes to mean | A line that shows it |
|---|---|---|
| Blood | courage in battle at first, then murder and guilt | “It will have blood, they say, blood will have blood.” (Macbeth, Act 3, Scene 4) |
| Darkness and night | night hides evil deeds, and the murderers call on it | “Stars, hide your fires!” (Macbeth, Act 1, Scene 4) |
| Sleep and sleeplessness | the innocent rest that the murderers destroy | “You lack the season of all natures, sleep.” (Lady Macbeth, Act 3, Scene 4) |
| Clothing | titles as clothes that never fit Macbeth | “why do you dress me / In borrow’d robes?” (Macbeth, Act 1, Scene 3) |
| Sickness | evil as a disease of the mind and of Scotland | “Canst thou not minister to a mind diseas’d” (Macbeth, Act 5, Scene 3) |
| Disorder in nature | the murder of a king turns nature upside down | “A falcon, towering in her pride of place, / Was by a mousing owl hawk’d at and kill’d.” (Old Man, Act 2, Scene 4) |

The blood motif changes the most. In Act 1, a wounded captain praises Macbeth in battle, and the blood is a mark of honour:

> For brave Macbeth (well he deserves that name),\
> Disdaining Fortune, with his brandish’d steel,\
> Which smok’d with bloody execution,
>
> — Captain, *Macbeth*, Act 1, Scene 2

Minutes after he has killed Duncan, Macbeth stares at his hands, and the blood has become guilt so great that an ocean could not wash it off:

> Will all great Neptune’s ocean wash this blood\
> Clean from my hand? No, this my hand will rather\
> The multitudinous seas incarnadine,\
> Making the green one red.
>
> — Macbeth, *Macbeth*, Act 2, Scene 2

Lady Macbeth comes back from smearing the sleeping servants with Duncan’s blood and tells him, “A little water clears us of this deed.” By Act 5 she is walking in her sleep, rubbing her hands and crying, “all the perfumes of Arabia will not sweeten this little hand.” The image returns with its meaning reversed: the guilt she dismissed has broken her.

:::example Tracing a motif
Banquo says of Macbeth’s new title that “New honours come upon him, / Like our strange garments”. In Act 5, Angus says Macbeth feels his title “Hang loose about him, like a giant’s robe / Upon a dwarfish thief.” What does the clothing motif suggest about Macbeth?
@step Name the image: in both passages a title is pictured as clothing.
@step Compare the two uses. In Act 1 the new clothes are only strange; they will fit “with the aid of use”. In Act 5 the robe is far too big, and the wearer is a thief.
@answer The clothing motif shows that the crown never fits Macbeth: at first his honours feel new and strange, but by the end he is a small man in a stolen robe, a king in name who has no right to the title.
:::

:::summary
- Most of the play is blank verse, unrhymed lines with five beats; the Porter and the sleepwalking Lady Macbeth speak prose, and the Witches chant in short rhymes.
- To paraphrase, read to the punctuation, replace the old words, and keep every idea and image in plain modern English.
- Equivocation is speaking with two meanings so that the listener believes the false one.
- A motif is a recurring image. Blood moves from honour to guilt; darkness hides crime; sleep is destroyed; clothes that do not fit show a stolen title; sickness and disorder show a land poisoned by a murder.
:::
