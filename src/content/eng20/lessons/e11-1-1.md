Mark Twain’s “The Celebrated Jumping Frog of Calaveras County” is not really told by Mark Twain’s narrator at all. He visits a mining camp, asks an old man a simple question, and then steps aside:

> He never smiled, he never frowned, he never changed his voice from the gentle-flowing key to which he tuned the initial sentence, he never betrayed the slightest suspicion of enthusiasm;
>
> — Mark Twain, “The Celebrated Jumping Frog of Calaveras County” (1865)

Most of the sketch is old Simon Wheeler’s rambling tale, set inside the narrator’s story like a picture inside a frame. How a writer arranges events in time, and how fast or slowly the story moves through them, shapes everything the reader feels. In this lesson you will name the main kinds of **structure** and explain their effects, and see how writers control **pacing**.

## Five structures

:::definition Structure
A story’s **structure** is the way its events are arranged and ordered. The order in which things happen and the order in which the reader learns about them are not always the same.
:::

| Structure | How it works | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Chronological** | Events are told in the order they happen. | “Araby”: a boy’s crush, his promise, his long wait and his night at the bazaar, in order. |
| **Non-linear** | The story jumps back and forth in time, often with flashbacks. | “Désirée’s Baby” opens with a visit, then goes back eighteen years to how Désirée was found as a toddler. |
| **Frame narrative** | A story is told inside another story. | In “The Celebrated Jumping Frog”, Wheeler’s tale sits inside the narrator’s visit. |
| **Circular** | The ending returns to the opening scene or image. | A story that begins and ends at the same bus stop, years apart. |
| **Parallel** | Two storylines happening at the same time are told in turn. | “The Bride Comes to Yellow Sky” cuts between a couple on a train and a gunman in town. |

:::check e11-structure 1
:::

Stephen Crane opens the second section of “The Bride Comes to Yellow Sky” by leaving the newly married marshal on his train and jumping to the town he is heading for:

> The California Express on the Southron Railway was due at Yellow Sky in twenty-one minutes.
>
> — Stephen Crane, “The Bride Comes to Yellow Sky” (1898)

The precise “twenty-one minutes” tells us the two storylines are running at the same time, and that they are about to meet.

:::mistake Labels that overlap
Some labels can describe the same story. A circular story is often told in order, and the story inside a frame usually is too. When a question asks for the structure, choose the feature that makes the story distinctive: a frame, a return to the start, two storylines, or jumps in time. Call a story chronological only when none of those apply.
:::

## Why structure matters

A structure is a choice, and every choice has an effect. A frame can set two voices side by side; a flashback can plant a question; parallel storylines can build suspense; a circular ending invites the reader to compare the beginning with the end.

:::example The effect of a parallel structure
Why does Crane cut back and forth between the marshal on the train and the drunken gunman, Scratchy Wilson, roaming the town?
@step Name the structure: two storylines, happening at the same time, told in turn. That is a parallel structure.
@step Ask what the reader knows. Each time we return to Scratchy, we know something the marshal does not: danger is waiting at the end of his journey, and he has no gun.
@step Ask what that knowledge does to the reader: every minute of the train ride now feels tense.
@answer The parallel structure lets the reader see the clash coming before the characters do, which builds suspense about the moment the two storylines meet.
:::

## Pacing: scene and summary

:::definition Scene and summary
A **scene** shows events moment by moment, as they happen, often with dialogue and close detail. A **summary** covers a long stretch of time in a few words. **Pacing** is the speed at which a story moves through time, and writers control it by choosing between the two.
:::

A writer can also skip time altogether (“Three years later…”) or stop the clock to describe a place or a thought. Slowing down tells the reader that a moment matters; speeding up tells the reader to move on.

Hawthorne spends almost all of “Young Goodman Brown” on a single night in the forest, told scene by scene. Then he covers the rest of Brown’s life in one sentence:

> And when he had lived long, and was borne to his grave a hoary corpse, followed by Faith, an aged woman, and children and grandchildren, a goodly procession, besides neighbors not a few, they carved no hopeful verse upon his tombstone, for his dying hour was gloom.
>
> — Nathaniel Hawthorne, “Young Goodman Brown” (1835)

Decades pass in a breath because, for Brown, nothing after that night mattered except its gloom. The pacing itself makes the point.

:::tip Compare clock time with page time
To judge pacing, ask two questions: how much time passes in the story, and how many words does the writer spend on it? Many words for a few seconds is a scene slowed right down; a few words for many years is summary.
:::

:::example Slowing down a moment
A student’s story says: *Amara opened the letter from the university.* It is the turning point of the story. How could the writer slow it down?
@step Break the action into its smallest steps: picking up the envelope, tearing the flap, unfolding the page.
@step Add what Amara senses and thinks at each step: the stiff paper, a sound outside, her held breath.
@step Hold back the result until the last possible moment.
@answer *Amara picked up the envelope and turned it over twice. She slid a finger under the flap; the paper tore with a sound like a whisper. Outside, a car door slammed. She unfolded the page, and her eyes went straight to the first word: “Congratulations.”* Each detail stretches the moment, so the reader waits with her.
:::

:::summary
- A story’s structure can be chronological, non-linear, a frame narrative, circular or parallel. Choose the label for the feature that makes the story distinctive.
- Every structure has an effect: a frame sets voices side by side, a flashback plants a question, parallel storylines build suspense, and a circular ending invites comparison.
- A scene shows events moment by moment; a summary covers a long time in a few words. Writers slow down the moments that matter most.
- To judge pacing, compare how much time passes with how many words it gets.
:::
