This lesson discusses the endings of “The Garden Party”, “Miss Brill”, “Araby” and “Young Goodman Brown”.

In Katherine Mansfield’s “The Garden Party”, Laura learns on the morning of her family’s party that a young carter from the cottages below has been killed. She begs her family to call the party off. Then her mother gives her a beautiful new hat, and Laura sees herself in the mirror:

> I’ll remember it again after the party’s over, she decided. And somehow that seemed quite the best plan....
>
> — Katherine Mansfield, “The Garden Party” (1922)

Laura never says “I want to enjoy myself more than I want to do what is right”. The “somehow” and the trailing dots say it for her. In this lesson you will work out **why** characters act as they do, and trace **how** and **why** they change.

## Motive: why characters act

:::definition Motive
A character’s **motive** is the reason behind an action: a want, a fear, a belief or a duty. Writers rarely state motives outright, so the reader infers them from what the character says, does and thinks, and from the timing of the action.
:::

Motives are often **mixed**. In “A White Heron”, nine-year-old Sylvia likes the young hunter who is staying with her grandmother, and he has offered ten dollars to anyone who can show him the heron’s nest. Yet from the start something holds her back:

> Sylvia would have liked him vastly better without his gun; she could not understand why he killed the very birds he seemed to like so much.
>
> — Sarah Orne Jewett, “A White Heron” (1886)

Her friendship with him pulls one way; her love of the birds pulls the other. When the story reaches her final choice, both motives are in play, which is why the choice feels hard.

:::mistake What a character says is not always the motive
Characters fool themselves. Goodman Brown promises to be good “after this one night”, and Hawthorne calls it an “excellent resolve”. But the promise is what lets him hurry on with his “evil purpose” with a clear conscience. Test a stated reason against what the character actually does.
:::

:::example Inferring a motive
Jack Potter, the town marshal of Yellow Sky, is riding home with the bride he married in San Antonio. He has told no one in town. Crane writes:

> He had committed an extraordinary crime. Face to face with this girl in San Antonio, and spurred by his sharp impulse, he had gone headlong over all the social hedges.
>
> — Stephen Crane, “The Bride Comes to Yellow Sky” (1898)

Why does Potter plan to slip home from the station unseen?
@step Notice the exaggeration: marrying is not a crime. Calling it an “extraordinary crime” shows how guilty Potter feels.
@step Find the cause: he married on a “sharp impulse”, far from home, without telling the friends who expect him to.
@step Connect the feeling to the action: a man who feels he has broken an unwritten rule wants to avoid facing the people he broke it with.
@answer Potter is driven by guilt and embarrassment: he fears how the town will react to a marriage he kept from them, so he hopes to reach home before anyone sees.
:::

## Change: how characters develop

:::definition Dynamic and static characters
A **dynamic** character changes in an important way: in what they believe, understand or do. A **static** character stays the same. The **catalyst** is the event that sets a change in motion.
:::

To show a change clearly, writers often repeat a moment so we can see the character behave differently the second time. Every Sunday, Miss Brill ends her afternoon in the park the same way:

> On her way home she usually bought a slice of honey-cake at the baker’s. It was her Sunday treat.
>
> — Katherine Mansfield, “Miss Brill” (1920)

But on the Sunday she overhears a young couple mocking her and her fur, the routine breaks:

> But to-day she passed the baker’s by, climbed the stairs, went into the little dark room—her room like a cupboard—and sat down on the red eiderdown.
>
> — Katherine Mansfield, “Miss Brill” (1920)

Mansfield never says “Miss Brill was hurt”. The skipped treat, the “little dark room” and the silence show that the cruel remark (the catalyst) has broken her happy picture of herself.

:::tip Compare the start with the end
To describe a change, find a moment at the end that mirrors one at the start: a greeting, a habit, a place. Then ask what the character does differently, and what has happened in between to cause it.
:::

:::definition Epiphany
An **epiphany** is a sudden moment of understanding, when a character sees themselves or their world in a new light. The word was made famous by James Joyce, whose stories often end on one.
:::

At the end of “Araby”, the boy reaches the bazaar just as it is closing, with almost nothing left to buy for the girl he adores:

> Gazing up into the darkness I saw myself as a creature driven and derided by vanity; and my eyes burned with anguish and anger.
>
> — James Joyce, “Araby” (1914)

He suddenly sees his grand romance as foolish self-importance. Not every change is for the better: Goodman Brown comes home from the forest unable to trust anyone, and stays that way until he dies.

:::summary
- A motive is the reason behind an action. Infer it from words, actions, thoughts and timing, and expect motives to be mixed.
- Test what a character says against what they do: characters often fool themselves.
- A dynamic character changes; a static one does not. The catalyst is the event that causes the change.
- Look for a moment at the end that mirrors one at the start, and compare how the character behaves.
- An epiphany is a sudden moment of understanding, and it is often where a story ends.
:::
