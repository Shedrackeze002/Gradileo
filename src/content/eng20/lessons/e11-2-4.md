Two young English poets, both soldiers in the First World War, wrote about dying for their country. Rupert Brooke wrote in 1914, before he had seen battle:

> If I should die, think only this of me:\
> That there’s some corner of a foreign field\
> That is for ever England.
>
> — Rupert Brooke, “The Soldier” (1914)

Wilfred Owen wrote in 1917, after months in the trenches, and ended his poem with a Latin line from the Roman poet Horace, “It is sweet and fitting to die for one’s country”:

> My friend, you would not tell with such high zest\
> To children ardent for some desperate glory,\
> The old Lie: Dulce et decorum est\
> Pro patria mori.
>
> — Wilfred Owen, “Dulce et Decorum Est” (1920)

Same war, same subject, opposite answers. Reading two poems side by side sharpens both: each one shows what the other chose to say, and what it chose to leave out. In this lesson you will learn a method for **comparing poems** and for writing about the comparison.

## Start with what they share

:::definition Comparing poems
To **compare** two poems is to explain how they are alike and how they differ, and why the differences matter. Begin with what they share, usually the **subject**, and then look at how each poet treats it.
:::

A shared subject is the ground the comparison stands on. Brooke and Owen both write about a soldier’s death. Emily Dickinson and Thomas Hardy both write about a small bird that stands for hope. Once the common ground is clear, the differences become meaningful instead of random.

## Points of comparison

Work through the same questions for both poems, and note the answers side by side.

| Point | Questions to ask |
|---|---|
| Speaker and situation | Who is speaking, and in what circumstances? |
| Attitude and tone | How does the speaker feel about the subject: proud, bitter, hopeful, doubtful? |
| Imagery | Which images does each poem choose, and what do they make us feel? |
| Form and structure | Sonnet, free verse, stanzas, rhyme; where does each poem turn? |
| Ending | Where does each poem leave the reader? |
| Context | When was each written, and does that help explain the difference? |

Brooke’s imagery is gentle and green: a body “Washed by the rivers, blest by suns of home”. Owen’s is brutal: blood that comes “gargling from the froth-corrupted lungs”. Context helps explain the gap. Brooke wrote before he had seen the fighting, and died of an infection on his way to battle in 1915; Owen wrote after he had seen gas attacks with his own eyes.

:::example Comparing two birds of hope
Here is the end of each poem. Compare the two speakers’ attitudes to hope.
> I’ve heard it in the chillest land,\
> And on the strangest sea;\
> Yet, never, in extremity,\
> It asked a crumb of me.
>
> — Emily Dickinson, “Hope is the thing with feathers” (1891)

> That I could think there trembled through\
> His happy good-night air\
> Some blessed Hope, whereof he knew\
> And I was unaware.
>
> — Thomas Hardy, “The Darkling Thrush” (1900)
@step Name the shared subject: in both poems, a small bird stands for hope.
@step Describe Dickinson’s speaker. She has heard hope singing in the worst conditions, and it never asked anything in return: she is certain of it.
@step Describe Hardy’s speaker. On a bleak winter evening he hears an old thrush sing with joy, but he can only guess at the hope it knows; he himself is “unaware”.
@answer Both poems use a singing bird for hope, but Dickinson’s speaker feels hope within her, steady and generous, while Hardy’s speaker hears it from outside and cannot share it.
:::

## Writing the comparison

There are two common ways to organize a comparison.

- **Point by point**: take one point at a time (attitude, then imagery, then ending) and discuss both poems under each. This keeps the comparison running through every paragraph.
- **Poem by poem**: discuss all of the first poem, then all of the second, and finish by drawing them together. This is simpler but risks becoming two separate essays.

Comparative words hold the two poems together in a single sentence: *both*, *similarly*, *likewise* for likenesses; *whereas*, *while*, *unlike*, *in contrast* for differences.

:::tip A comparison sentence
Name both poems, the point, and the evidence from each: “**Whereas** Brooke imagines his body as ‘a richer dust’ in English soil, Owen forces the reader to watch a gassed man’s ‘white eyes writhing in his face’, **so** the glory Brooke takes for granted becomes, for Owen, ‘The old Lie’.”
:::

:::mistake Two summaries side by side
A paragraph on one poem followed by a paragraph on the other is not yet a comparison. Every paragraph should connect the poems: say how they are alike or different on that point, quote both, and explain why the difference matters.
:::

:::summary
- Begin a comparison with what the two poems share, usually the subject.
- Compare them point by point: speaker and situation, attitude and tone, imagery, form and structure, ending, and context.
- Support each point with evidence from both poems, and explain why the difference matters.
- Use comparative words (*both*, *whereas*, *while*, *unlike*) to keep the two poems together in each sentence.
:::
