A first draft gets the ideas down; revision makes them land. Two essays can make the same argument, and one will still read as confident and precise while the other plods, hedges and wanders off in tone. The difference is style: how sentences begin and end, how long they run, which words they choose. In this lesson you will revise for rhythm, emphasis, precision and a consistent tone.

## Varying your sentences

Sentences that all begin the same way and all run the same length lull a reader to sleep:

> Della counted her money three times. Della found only one dollar and eighty-seven cents. Della sat down on the couch and cried. Della then decided to sell her hair.

Revision varies the **openings** (start with a phrase or a dependent clause, not always the subject) and **combines** closely related ideas:

> Three times Della counted her money, and each time she found only one dollar and eighty-seven cents. After sitting down on the couch to cry, she decided to sell her hair.

:::definition Sentence variety
**Sentence variety** means mixing sentence openings (subject, phrase, dependent clause), sentence types (simple, compound, complex) and sentence lengths, so that the rhythm supports the meaning.
:::

:::mistake Combining into a run-on
Joining sentences is not the same as stringing them together with commas. *Della counted her money three times she found only one dollar…, Della sat down…* is a run-on with comma splices. Combine with conjunctions, phrases and dependent clauses, and punctuate them correctly.
:::

### Short sentences for emphasis

A short sentence after longer ones lands hard. Save it for the moment you most want the reader to feel:

> After walking along the frozen trail for hours, the man stopped to build a fire under a spruce tree. Snow fell from the branches above. In a moment, the fire was out.

:::check e11-sentence-variety 2
:::

## End focus

Readers give the most weight to what comes **last** in a sentence. Put background and known information first, and the idea you want to stress at the end.

:::example Moving the key idea to the end
A writer wants to stress that no one listened to the engineers. Revise: *No one listened to the engineers, who warned the council about the bridge for months.*
@step Find the idea to stress: *no one listened*.
@step Move the background (the months of warnings) to the start.
@step End with the key idea.
@answer *The engineers warned the council about the bridge for months, but no one listened.*
:::

:::

## Precision

Vague words (*stuff*, *things*, *a lot*, *good*, *bad*, *interesting*, *does something*) tell the reader nothing. Replace them with exactly what you mean.

> *Vague:* The story has a lot of good stuff about things like fate.
>
> *Precise:* The story uses three wishes on a magic paw to ask whether people can control their fate.

:::check e11-revision 1
:::

## Hedges and intensifiers

**Hedges** (*I think*, *maybe*, *sort of*, *it could be argued that*) weaken a claim you mean to make. **Empty intensifiers** (*really*, *very*, *extremely*, *totally*) try to add force but usually signal that the word they modify is too weak. Cut both, and choose a stronger word if you need one.

> *Wordy:* I think that maybe the ending of the story is sort of really very surprising.
>
> *Revised:* The ending of the story is surprising.

:::tip Hedge once, on purpose
Some qualification is honest: *suggests*, *probably* and *in most cases* limit a claim to what the evidence shows. The problem is piling hedges onto a claim you are actually sure of. Qualify where the evidence requires it; otherwise, state the point plainly.
:::

:::

## A consistent tone

A literary essay uses a **formal register**: complete sentences, precise vocabulary, no slang, no chatty asides to the reader. One informal sentence in a formal paragraph jars, and it can undermine an otherwise strong point.

> **(1)** Jack London presents the man as practical but unimaginative. **(2)** He doesn’t get that it’s super cold, which is a big oops. **(3)** This failure of imagination proves fatal.

Revising sentence 2 means changing the register *and* keeping the meaning:

> He notices the extreme cold but never considers what it could do to him.

:::mistake Fixing the tone but changing the point
“He is terrified of the cold from the start” is formal, but it says the opposite of what the paragraph argues. A revision for tone must still say the same thing about the text.
:::

:::check e11-revision 3
:::

:::summary
- Vary sentence openings and lengths; combine related ideas with phrases and clauses, never with comma splices.
- A short sentence after longer ones creates emphasis; save it for the key moment.
- Readers stress what comes last: end a sentence with the idea you most want to stress.
- Replace vague words with precise ones, cut needless hedges and empty intensifiers, and keep a consistent formal tone without changing the meaning.
:::
