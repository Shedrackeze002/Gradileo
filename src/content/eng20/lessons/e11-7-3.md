Compare two sentences about the same event. *The striker scored the winning goal.* *The winning goal was scored by the striker.* The facts are the same, but the first puts the person in the spotlight and the second puts the goal there. In this lesson you will learn to choose between the **active** and **passive** voice on purpose, and to use the **subjunctive mood**, the verb forms formal writing uses for wishes, imagined situations and demands.

## Active and passive voice

:::definition Active and passive voice
In the **active voice**, the subject does the action: *The volunteers cleaned the beach.*
In the **passive voice**, the subject receives the action: *The beach was cleaned by the volunteers.* The doer, if it is named at all, comes after *by*.
:::

A passive verb always has two parts: a form of *be* (*is*, *was*, *were*, *has been*, *will be*) and a **past participle** (*cleaned*, *chosen*, *built*, *written*).

- *The new stamps **were designed** by a local artist.* (passive)
- *The winning design **has been chosen**.* (passive, with no doer named)
- *The new footbridge **will be built** next year.* (passive)

:::mistake “Was” does not always mean passive
*A local artist **was designing** the new stamps* has a form of *be*, but it is followed by an *-ing* word, not a past participle. The artist is doing the action, so the sentence is active.
:::

:::example Rewriting a passive sentence in the active voice
Rewrite in the active voice: *The mural in the school hall was painted by Leila.*
@step Find the doer, after *by*: *Leila*.
@step Make the doer the subject: *Leila …*
@step Keep the same tense: *was painted* is past, so the active verb is *painted*.
@answer *Leila painted the mural in the school hall.*
:::

### When the passive is the better choice

Most writing is clearer and more direct in the active voice, so use it by default. The passive is the better choice when:

- **the doer is unknown or does not matter**: *My bicycle was taken from outside the library.*
- **the method matters more than the person**, as in a scientific report: *The solution was heated to 80 °C and stirred for five minutes.*
- **you want to save the doer for the end**, for emphasis: *The final was won by a fifteen-year-old who had never played in a stadium before.*

:::tip Read the passive critically
The passive can also hide who is responsible. When a company writes *Mistakes were made, and some orders were lost*, a careful reader asks: who made the mistakes?

## The subjunctive mood

:::definition Mood
A verb’s **mood** shows how the speaker presents an action. The **indicative** states facts and asks questions (*She is here*). The **imperative** gives commands (*Be here at six*). The **subjunctive** presents something wished for, imagined or demanded rather than real (*I wish she were here*; *The coach insists that she be here at six*).
:::

### “Were” for wishes and situations that are not real

After *if*, *wish* and *as if*, when the situation is imagined rather than real, formal writing uses **were** for every subject:

- *If I **were** taller, I could reach the top shelf.*
- *I wish my grandmother **were** here to see this.*
- *Daniel acts as if he **were** in charge of the whole team.*

:::note Speech and formal writing
In everyday speech, many people say *If I was taller* or *I wish she was here*, and listeners understand. In formal writing, such as essays, reports and applications, use *were*.
:::

### The base form after demands and recommendations

After words of demanding, requiring or recommending (*insist*, *suggest*, *recommend*, *require*, *demand*, *it is essential that*, *it is important that*), the subjunctive uses the **base form** of the verb, with no *-s* and no change for the past:

- *The coach insisted that Nia **be** on time for every practice.*
- *The doctor recommended that Omar **drink** more water.*
- *It is vital that the gate **not be** left open.*

:::note Another correct form
British English often uses *should* here (*The doctor recommended that Omar should drink more water*), which is also correct. The subjunctive without *should* is correct everywhere.
:::

### Conditions that did not happen

For an imagined past, put *had* and a past participle in the *if* part, and *would have* in the other part.

:::mistake “If I would have known”
*If I would have known about the party, I would have come* puts *would have* in both parts. Write *If I **had known** about the party, I would have come.* The same goes for wishes about the past: *I wish I **had bought** the tickets*, not *I wish I would have bought them*.
:::

:::summary
- In the active voice the subject does the action; in the passive voice (a form of *be* + a past participle) the subject receives it.
- Use the active voice by default. Choose the passive when the doer is unknown or unimportant, in a scientific method, or to save the doer for emphasis, and notice when it hides who is responsible.
- In formal writing, wishes and imagined situations take *were*: *If I were*, *I wish she were*.
- After words of demanding or recommending, use the base form: *that he be*, *that she apply*, *that the gate not be left open*.
- For an imagined past, use *had* + past participle in the *if* part: *If I had known, I would have come.*
:::
