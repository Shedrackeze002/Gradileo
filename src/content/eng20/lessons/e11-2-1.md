In 1611, before a long journey abroad, John Donne is said to have written a poem for his wife. Instead of comparing their love to a rose or a star, he compared the two of them to a pair of compasses, the tool for drawing circles:

> If they be two, they are two so\
> As stiffe twin compasses are two,\
> Thy soule the fixt foot, makes no show\
> To move, but doth, if the’other doe.
>
> — John Donne, “A Valediction: Forbidding Mourning” (1633)

A drawing tool is a strange thing to find in a love poem, and that is the point. In this lesson you will follow comparisons that run through a whole poem, from extended metaphors to Donne’s startling **conceits**, and learn to recognize and explain **allusions**: references that borrow meaning from stories everyone is expected to know.

:::note Old spelling
Donne’s and George Herbert’s poems are quoted with the spelling of their first printing in 1633: *soule* for soul, *doe* for do. Read them aloud and the words come clear.
:::

## Extended metaphor

:::definition Extended metaphor
An **extended metaphor** carries one comparison through several lines or a whole poem. The subject (the thing really being described) is compared to something else, and detail after detail is drawn from that something else.
:::

To follow one, name the **subject**, which is often in the title, then list the words that all belong to the other thing. In a poem called “Grief” that speaks of something that “comes in twice a day and covers everything”, then “pulls back” so that “the sand is smooth again”, every detail belongs to the sea’s tide: grief arrives, withdraws and returns, again and again.

Emily Dickinson turns a book into transport:

> There is no frigate like a book\
> To take us lands away,\
> Nor any coursers like a page\
> Of prancing poetry.
>
> — Emily Dickinson, “There is no frigate like a book” (1896)

The frigate (a fast ship), the coursers (swift horses) and, later, a “chariot” are all ways of travelling. A book carries the reader as far as any of them, and, as the last four lines add, it costs nothing, so even “the poorest” can make the journey.

## The metaphysical conceit

:::definition Conceit
A **conceit** is an extended metaphor that compares two strikingly unlike things and works out the comparison in surprising, often witty, detail. The seventeenth-century poets who loved them, John Donne and George Herbert among them, are called the **metaphysical poets**.
:::

A conceit asks the reader to think. Why should two lovers be like a pair of compasses? Donne answers stanza by stanza.

:::example Following Donne’s compasses
In “A Valediction: Forbidding Mourning”, a man about to travel tells the woman he loves not to grieve. Work out what the compasses stand for, and what the last line means.

> Such wilt thou be to mee, who must\
> Like th’other foot, obliquely runne;\
> Thy firmnes makes my circle just,\
> And makes me end, where I begunne.
>
> — John Donne, “A Valediction: Forbidding Mourning” (1633)

@step Name the two parts of the tool: a pair of compasses has a fixed foot, which stays at the centre, and a moving foot, which draws the circle.
@step Match them to the lovers. She is “the fixt foot”, who stays at home; he is “th’other foot”, who must travel (“obliquely runne”).
@step Read the last line literally, then for the lovers. A compass draws a perfect (“just”) circle only if the centre foot holds firm, and the moving foot ends where it started.
@answer Her constancy keeps him true while he is away and brings him home again: he will “end, where I begunne”, back with her.
:::

The surprise is what makes the comparison memorable. Herbert does something similar in “The Pulley”: God pours every blessing on humankind except rest, so that restlessness and weariness will work like a pulley, lifting people back to God.

:::

:::mistake Stopping at the label
Saying “this is a conceit” is only the start. A good answer names both halves of the comparison (lovers and compasses), matches the details (the fixed foot is the woman who stays home) and says what the comparison suggests (her faithfulness will bring him back). Each step needs evidence from the poem.
:::

## Allusion

:::definition Allusion
An **allusion** is a brief reference to a well-known person, place, event or text. It borrows the meaning of that story without retelling it. When someone calls a weakness an “Achilles’ heel”, the whole story of the Greek warrior who could be wounded only in his heel comes with the phrase.
:::

Most allusions come from four sources:

| Kind | Where it comes from | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Biblical | the Bible | a **Good Samaritan** (a stranger who helps someone in need) |
| Classical | ancient Greece and Rome: myths, legends and writers | a **Herculean** task (one needing enormous strength) |
| Historical | real people and events | **met his Waterloo** (suffered a final defeat, like Napoleon in 1815) |
| Literary | later works of literature | a **Scrooge** (a miser, from Dickens’s “A Christmas Carol”) |

Some allusions could belong to two groups: the Trojan horse comes from Greek legend and also from Homer’s and Virgil’s poems. What matters most is knowing the story behind the reference.

:::tip When you do not know the story
Allusions assume shared knowledge, and nobody knows every story. If a name or event is unfamiliar, look it up. Then ask the two questions that matter: *What is this story famous for?* and *Why does the writer want that meaning here?*
:::

## Allusion in poems

Poets use allusion to say a great deal in a few words. William Wordsworth, unhappy with the England of 1802, calls on a poet who had died more than a century before:

> Milton! thou should’st be living at this hour:\
> England hath need of thee: she is a fen\
> Of stagnant waters:
>
> — William Wordsworth, “London, 1802” (1807)

John Milton was famous for his moral seriousness and his defence of liberty. By wishing him alive, Wordsworth measures the present against him and finds it lacking: a stagnant marsh of “selfish men”.

Sometimes a poet **twists** an allusion, and the twist carries the meaning.

:::example A twisted allusion
In the Bible, God tells Abraham to sacrifice his son Isaac, then sends an angel to stop him and a ram to sacrifice instead. Wilfred Owen, a soldier in the First World War, retells the story. What does his ending suggest?

> When lo! an angel called him out of heaven,\
> Saying, Lay not thy hand upon the lad,\
> Neither do anything to him. Behold,\
> A ram caught in a thicket by its horns;\
> Offer the Ram of Pride instead of him.\
> But the old man would not so, but slew his son. . . .
>
> — Wilfred Owen, “Parable of the Old Men and the Young” (1920)

@step Identify the allusion: the biblical story of Abraham and Isaac.
@step Notice the changes. Earlier in the poem, Abram builds “parapets and trenches”, the defences of the First World War. The ram becomes “the Ram of Pride”, and the old man refuses to sacrifice it.
@step Ask what the change means. In the Bible the son is spared; here the old man kills him rather than give up his pride.
@answer Owen suggests that the older generation who led the war sacrificed their sons rather than sacrifice their own pride.
:::

:::

:::mistake Naming without explaining
“Owen alludes to the Bible” earns little on its own. Explain what the original story means and what the poem does with it: does it borrow that meaning, as Wordsworth borrows Milton’s greatness, or reverse it, as Owen reverses the rescue of Isaac?
:::

:::summary
- An extended metaphor carries one comparison through a poem: name the subject, then list the details drawn from the thing it is compared to.
- A conceit is a surprising, intellectual extended metaphor, like Donne’s lovers as a pair of compasses. Match each detail of the comparison to the subject, then say what it suggests.
- An allusion is a brief reference to a well-known person, place, event or text: biblical, classical, historical or literary.
- To analyse an allusion, ask what the original story is famous for and how the writer uses it: to borrow its meaning, or to twist it.
:::
