Two of Poe’s narrators confess to murder, but one is undone by guilt and the other calmly boasts. Put the stories side by side and each one becomes clearer: Montresor’s calm is more chilling *because* we have heard the Tell-Tale narrator’s panic. That is the point of a comparative essay. It is not two essays stapled together; it is one argument about what we see when two texts are read together.

## The comparative thesis

:::definition Comparative thesis
A **comparative thesis** connects two texts through a shared subject, states how they are alike or different in their treatment of it, and says **why the comparison matters**: what it reveals that neither text shows as clearly on its own.
:::

A helpful pattern is: *Both [A] and [B] [share something], but [A does this] while [B does that], so [what the contrast reveals].*

Weak comparative theses fail in predictable ways:

| Weak thesis | What is wrong |
|---|---|
| “The Tell-Tale Heart” and “The Cask of Amontillado” have many similarities and differences. | True of any two texts: it says nothing. |
| “The Tell-Tale Heart” is about a murder. “The Cask of Amontillado” is about revenge. | Two separate claims, never connected. |
| In “The Tell-Tale Heart”, Poe’s frantic narrator shows how guilt destroys the mind. | It discusses only one text. |
| The narrator of “The Tell-Tale Heart” feels guilty, but Montresor does not. | A real difference, but *so what?* |

:::example Building a comparative thesis
Write a comparative thesis on the narrators of “The Tell-Tale Heart” and “The Cask of Amontillado”.
@step Find what they share: both are murderers who tell their own stories.
@step Find the key difference: one narrator is destroyed by guilt; the other is untouched by it.
@step Say what the contrast reveals: Montresor’s calm becomes even more chilling beside the other narrator’s panic.
@answer *Both of Poe’s murderers tell their own stories, but the narrator of “The Tell-Tale Heart” is destroyed by guilt while Montresor is untouched by it, a contrast that makes Montresor’s calm the more chilling of the two.*
:::

## Block and point-by-point structure

There are two standard ways to organize a comparison.

:::definition Block and point-by-point
In **block** (subject-by-subject) structure, you discuss all of one text, then all of the other, linking the second back to the first. In **point-by-point** structure, each body section takes one point of comparison and discusses both texts under it.
:::

Here are both outlines for the essay on Poe’s narrators.

**Block**

1. Introduction and comparative thesis
2. “The Tell-Tale Heart”: the narrator’s voice; guilt; the ending
3. “The Cask of Amontillado”: the narrator’s voice; guilt; the ending, with links back to “The Tell-Tale Heart”
4. Conclusion: what the comparison shows

**Point by point**

1. Introduction and comparative thesis
2. The narrator’s voice: “The Tell-Tale Heart”, then “The Cask of Amontillado”
3. Guilt: “The Tell-Tale Heart”, then “The Cask of Amontillado”
4. The ending: “The Tell-Tale Heart”, then “The Cask of Amontillado”
5. Conclusion: what the comparison shows

Block structure suits short texts, short essays and tasks that ask you to discuss each text in turn. Point-by-point structure keeps the comparison in view all the way through, which suits longer essays with several points.

:::check e11-comparison-structure 1
:::

:::mistake Two essays stapled together
The commonest weakness of a block essay is that the second half never mentions the first, so the comparison appears only in the conclusion. In the second block, discuss the same points in the same order, and keep linking back: *Unlike the Tell-Tale narrator, Montresor…*
:::

## Writing a comparative paragraph

Transitions do much of the work of comparison. Use *similarly*, *likewise* and *both* for likenesses, and *whereas*, *by contrast*, *unlike* and *while* for differences. Here is a model point-by-point paragraph on the narrators’ voices.

> Both narrators speak directly to the reader, but in opposite tones. The narrator of “The Tell-Tale Heart” protests too much: he admits he is “very, very dreadfully nervous” and then demands, “How, then, am I mad?” The broken rhythm and the anxious question make his voice sound like the madness he denies. Montresor, by contrast, is unhurried and precise, explaining that he continued “to smile in his face” while planning the murder. Where the first narrator’s panic invites the reader to doubt him, Montresor’s composure invites something worse: the realization that he means every word.

:::example Choosing a structure
A student must compare the ways two war poems present soldiers, under three points: the speaking voice, the images of death and what each poem asks of the reader. The essay will be long, and the student wants every paragraph to compare. Which structure suits it?
@step The essay is long and has three points of comparison.
@step The student wants the comparison to run through every paragraph, not wait until the end.
@answer Point by point: each body paragraph takes one point and discusses both poems under it.
:::

:::check e11-comparison-structure 3
:::

:::summary
- A comparative thesis connects two texts, states how they differ or agree, and says why the comparison matters.
- “They are alike and different”, two separate claims, a thesis about one text, and a difference with no *so what* all fall short.
- Block structure discusses one text, then the other; point-by-point structure takes one point at a time across both texts.
- In a block essay, link the second text back to the first; in either structure, use transitions that compare.
:::
