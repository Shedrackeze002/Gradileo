You have probably never seen the word *chronometer*, but you can work it out. *Chron* means time, as in *chronological*, and a *meter* measures, as in *thermometer*: a chronometer is an instrument that measures time very precisely. A large share of English words, especially the words of school subjects, come from Latin and Greek, and a few dozen roots open up thousands of them. In this lesson you will use word parts to work out unfamiliar words, and then turn to the other side of vocabulary: choosing the precise word instead of a vague, worn-out or evasive one.

## Building words from parts

:::definition Prefix, root and suffix
A **root** carries a word’s core meaning. A **prefix** is added before it and changes the meaning (*in-* not, *re-* back or again, *trans-* across). A **suffix** is added after it and often changes the part of speech (*-able* can be, *-logy* study of, *-ist* a person who).
:::

| Root | Meaning | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| aud | hear | audience, audible, audition |
| chron | time | chronic, chronological, synchronize |
| dict | say, speak | predict, contradict, dictate |
| ject | throw | eject, reject, project |
| path | feeling, suffering | sympathy, empathy, apathy |
| port | carry | portable, transport, export |
| rupt | break, burst | erupt, interrupt, rupture |
| spect | look | inspect, spectator, perspective |
| tract | pull, drag | attract, extract, distract |
| vert | turn | reverse, divert, introvert |

Prefixes change a root’s meaning in predictable ways.

| Prefix | Meaning | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| in-, im- | not | inaudible, immortal, incredible |
| inter- | between | interrupt, intervene, intercept |
| trans- | across | transport, transmit, transcript |
| circum- | around | circumference, circumnavigate |
| sub- | under | subterranean, submarine |
| poly- | many | polygon, polyglot |

:::example Working out a word from its parts
What does *inaudible* mean in *Kenji spoke so softly that his answer was almost inaudible*?
@step Split the word: *in-* + *aud* + *-ible*.
@step Give each part its meaning: *in-* means not, *aud* means hear, *-ible* means can be.
@step Put them together: not able to be heard.
@step Check that it fits the sentence: he spoke softly, so his answer could hardly be heard.
@answer *Inaudible* means impossible to hear.
:::

Words that share a root often differ only in their prefix, so the prefix decides the meaning: *attract* (pull toward), *extract* (pull out) and *distract* (pull away) all come from *tract*.

:::mistake Look-alike roots
The same letters can come from different roots. *Ped* means foot in *pedestrian* and *pedal* (from Latin), but the *ped* in *pediatrician* comes from a Greek word for child. Always check your guess against the sentence.
:::

## Precise diction

**Diction** is word choice. Precise diction says exactly what you mean, in words your reader will understand. Four kinds of word get in the way.

:::definition Four enemies of precision
- A **vague word** says too little: *good*, *nice*, *bad*, *thing*, *stuff*, *a lot*.
- **Jargon** is the technical or insider language of a field, which outsiders may not understand: *leverage our core competencies*, *acute cephalalgia*.
- A **euphemism** is a soft or polite expression that avoids a harsh truth: *passed away* for *died*, *let go* for *dismissed*.
- A **cliché** is an expression so overused it has lost its force: *at the end of the day*, *think outside the box*, *in the nick of time*.
:::

:::example Revising for precision
Revise: *At the end of the day, the trip was really good.*
@step *At the end of the day* is a cliché: cut it.
@step *Really good* is vague: what exactly made the trip worth taking?
@step Replace it with a specific detail the reader can picture.
@answer *On the trip, we held a fossil that is two hundred million years old.*
:::

:::note It depends on the reader
Jargon is the right choice for experts: a doctor writing to another doctor should use medical terms. A euphemism can be kind in a sympathy card. The problem comes when the words do not suit the reader, or hide a truth the reader needs, as in a news report.
:::

:::tip “Very” is a warning sign
When you write *very* or *really*, look for one precise word or a specific detail instead: not *very tired* but *exhausted*, not *really good* but *the twist ending surprised everyone*.

:::summary
- Split an unfamiliar word into prefix, root and suffix, put their meanings together, and check the result against the sentence.
- Words that share a root differ by their prefix: *attract*, *extract* and *distract*.
- Replace vague words with specific details, and cut clichés.
- Use jargon only for readers who know it, and avoid euphemisms where the reader needs the plain truth.
:::
