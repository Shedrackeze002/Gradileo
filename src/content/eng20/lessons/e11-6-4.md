An argument is not a list of opinions said loudly. It is a chain: a claim, reasons for it, evidence for the reasons, and reasoning that shows why the evidence proves the claim. A strong argument also faces the other side honestly, admitting what is fair before answering it. In this lesson you will build each link of that chain, concede and rebut, and claim exactly as much as your evidence supports.

## The parts of an argument

:::definition The parts of an argument
- **Claim**: the position the argument sets out to prove.
- **Reason**: why the claim is true.
- **Evidence**: specific facts, findings or examples that support the reason.
- **Warrant** (reasoning): the explanation of *why* the evidence proves the claim.
- **Counterargument**: a fair statement of the opposing view.
- **Rebuttal**: the answer to the counterargument.
:::

Here is a model argument paragraph, with its sentences numbered.

> **(1)** Our school should start classes at 9:00 instead of 8:00. **(2)** A later start would let students get the sleep they need to learn. **(3)** When two schools in our district made this change, attendance at first-period classes rose by six per cent. **(4)** If more students arrive for the first lesson when school starts later, then a later start is helping students take part in learning, not just letting them sleep in. **(5)** Some parents worry that a later start would make it harder for them to get to work on time. **(6)** However, the school could open its library at 8:00 for supervised reading, so students could still be dropped off early.

Sentence 1 is the claim, 2 a reason, 3 the evidence, 4 the warrant, 5 the counterargument and 6 the rebuttal.

### The missing link: the warrant

Evidence does not speak for itself. A reader may accept that attendance rose and still ask: *so what?* The warrant answers that question by making the link explicit. It does not add new facts, and it does not simply repeat the claim.

:::example Finding the warrant
Claim: *Phones should be kept in lockers during lessons.* Evidence: *In a classroom study, students whose phones lay silent on their desks remembered less of a lesson than students whose phones were in their bags.* Write the warrant.
@step Ask what the evidence shows: even a silent phone in sight lowers how much students remember.
@step Ask how that leads to the claim: if a visible phone harms learning, the fix is to put phones out of sight.
@answer *If even a silent phone in sight lowers what students remember, then keeping phones out of sight altogether is the surest way to protect learning.*
:::

## Conceding and rebutting

A **concession** admits that the other side has a point. It is a sign of strength, not weakness: it shows the reader you have thought about the issue fairly, and it sets up your rebuttal. Concessions are often signalled by *admittedly*, *granted*, *certainly*, *it is true that* or *some may argue that*.

The strongest move is **concede, then turn**: grant what is fair, then explain why your claim still holds.

> *Admittedly, protected lanes would take some road space away from cars, but in a nearby city that built them, car journeys slowed by less than a minute while cycling injuries fell by almost half.*

:::check e11-concession 1
:::

:::mistake Three weak answers to a counterargument
- **Giving up**: “It is true that a later start would make some parents’ mornings harder, so the school should probably keep its 8:00 start.” The concession swallows the argument.
- **Ignoring**: repeating your own claim as if the objection had never been raised.
- **Attacking**: “Parents who complain only care about themselves.” Insulting the other side answers nothing, and it costs you the reader’s trust.
:::

:::check e11-concession 2
:::

:::example Concede, then turn
An essay argues that students should work no more than ten hours a week during the school year. Answer this counterargument: *Some students need the money to help their families.*
@step Concede what is fair: some students really do need the income.
@step Turn back to the claim with a reason: the harm to marks is real, and there are other ways to meet the need.
@step Keep your position: the limit still stands, with support for those who need it.
@answer *Some students do need to earn money to help their families, but grants and flexible timetables could support them without asking them to sacrifice their marks.*
:::

## Qualifying a claim

A **qualifier** limits a claim to what the evidence can support: *most*, *many*, *often*, *usually*, *probably*, *likely*, *in most cases*.

| Claim | Problem |
|---|---|
| A later start would improve the life of every single student. | Overstated: one study cannot prove anything about *every* student. |
| An early start never helps any student learn. | Overstated: *never* is almost impossible to prove. |
| It is possible that a later start might perhaps help some students a little, in some cases. | Over-hedged: it claims almost nothing. |
| For most students, a later start would probably mean more sleep and better attendance. | Well qualified. |

:::tip Count your absolutes
Before you hand in an argument, circle every *all*, *every*, *always*, *never* and *no one*. For each, ask whether your evidence really proves it. If not, qualify it. Then check the other way: if one sentence contains *maybe*, *perhaps* and *possibly* together, cut them down to one.
:::

:::

:::summary
- An argument links a claim, reasons, evidence and a warrant that explains why the evidence proves the claim, and it answers the other side with a counterargument and rebuttal.
- A warrant makes the link explicit; it neither repeats the claim nor adds new facts.
- Concede what is fair, then turn back to your claim with a reason; never give up your position, ignore the objection or attack the people who hold it.
- Qualify claims to fit the evidence: avoid absolutes, and avoid piling up hedges.
:::
