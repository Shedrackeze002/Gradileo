A wealthy duke draws back a curtain to show a visitor a painting of his late wife:

> That’s my last Duchess painted on the wall,\
> Looking as if she were alive. I call\
> That piece a wonder, now: Frà Pandolf’s hands\
> Worked busily a day, and there she stands.\
> Will’t please you sit and look at her?
>
> — Robert Browning, “My Last Duchess” (1842)

He sounds polite, proud and in control. By the end of the poem we suspect he is something far worse, and he never notices that he has told us. In this lesson you will read poems spoken by characters, the **dramatic monologue**, and look at the gaps between what is said and what is true that create **irony** in poetry.

## The speaker is not the poet

:::definition Speaker
The **speaker** of a poem is the voice that says it. Sometimes the speaker is close to the poet; often it is an invented character, or **persona**, with a personality, a situation and opinions the poet may not share.
:::

When you write about a poem, say “the speaker” rather than the poet’s name unless you know the poem is personal. Robert Browning was not a murderous duke, and Thomas Hardy, whose speakers include a soldier and a dead woman, was neither.

## The dramatic monologue

:::definition Dramatic monologue
A **dramatic monologue** is a poem spoken by one character to a silent listener, the **auditor**, at a particular moment. As the speaker talks, the poem reveals their character, often including things the speaker does not intend to reveal.
:::

To read one, ask three questions.

| Question | What to look for |
|---|---|
| Who is speaking? | name, rank, job, situation, and how the speaker talks |
| To whom, and where? | words addressed to the listener (“you”, “sir”), questions, replies to things we never hear |
| What does the speaker reveal without meaning to? | the gap between how the speaker wants to sound and what the details show |

The listener never speaks, but we can track them. When the Duke says “Will’t please you sit”, someone is standing beside him. Near the end we learn who: the agent of a Count, sent to arrange the Duke’s marriage to the Count’s daughter.

:::example Reading between the Duke’s lines
The Duke is explaining that his late wife smiled too freely, at everyone. What does he reveal?

> Oh, sir, she smiled, no doubt,\
> Whene’er I passed her; but who passed without\
> Much the same smile? This grew; I gave commands;\
> Then all smiles stopped together. There she stands\
> As if alive.
>
> — Robert Browning, “My Last Duchess” (1842)

@step Identify speaker and listener: the Duke, speaking to “sir”, the agent arranging his next marriage.
@step Notice the calm tone: short, casual phrases (“This grew; I gave commands”) for something that ended a life.
@step Read the gap. He “gave commands”, then “all smiles stopped together”, and now she stands only “As if alive”: he most likely had her killed, for smiling at other people.
@answer The Duke reveals, without seeming to notice, a jealous and cruel man who had his wife killed, and he tells it to the man whose master’s daughter will be his next wife.
:::

Alfred, Lord Tennyson gives the Greek hero Ulysses (Odysseus) a voice in old age, home at last in Ithaca and bored:

> It little profits that an idle king,\
> By this still hearth, among these barren crags,\
> Match’d with an aged wife, I mete and dole\
> Unequal laws unto a savage race,\
> That hoard, and sleep, and feed, and know not me.
>
> — Alfred, Lord Tennyson, “Ulysses” (1842)

Many readers admire Ulysses’ courage; his last line, “To strive, to seek, to find, and not to yield”, is carved on walls and quoted at graduations. Yet the same speech shows a king who scorns his people as a “savage race”, gives his wife two words, and hands his kingdom to his son with faint praise: “He works his work, I mine.” A good reading holds both.

:::

:::mistake Taking the speaker at their word
The Duke says he would not “stoop” to tell his wife what displeased her, and wants to sound dignified. A careful reader asks what he leaves out. In a dramatic monologue, the speaker’s own words are the evidence against them.
:::

## Irony in poems

:::definition Irony
**Irony** is a gap between what is said or expected and what is actually true.
- **Verbal irony**: someone says the opposite of what they mean.
- **Situational irony**: events turn out the opposite of what we expect.
- **Dramatic irony**: the reader knows something a character does not.
:::

Poets use irony to make readers feel a truth instead of being told it. In Hardy’s “The Man He Killed”, a soldier tries to explain why he shot a man in battle:

> “I shot him dead because—\
> Because he was my foe,\
> Just so: my foe of course he was;\
> That’s clear enough; although
>
> — Thomas Hardy, “The Man He Killed” (1902)

The dashes and repetition give him away. He keeps insisting the reason is “clear enough”, and the more he insists, the less clear it sounds. In peacetime, he says, he would have bought this man a drink.

:::example Situational irony in “Richard Cory”
The townspeople have described Richard Cory: a gentleman, “richer than a king”, admired and envied by all. What makes the last stanza ironic?

> So on we worked, and waited for the light,\
> And went without the meat, and cursed the bread;\
> And Richard Cory, one calm summer night,\
> Went home and put a bullet through his head.
>
> — Edwin Arlington Robinson, “Richard Cory” (1897)

@step State what we expect. Everything so far suggests that Cory, with wealth, manners and admiration, is happy, and that the poor townspeople should envy him.
@step State what happens. The man who seemed to have “everything” takes his own life, while the struggling townspeople go on living.
@answer The irony is situational: the outcome is the opposite of what the poem led us to expect. It suggests that wealth and appearances do not reveal, or guarantee, happiness.
:::

:::check e11-poetic-irony 2
:::

Stephen Crane builds a whole poem on verbal irony. Three of its stanzas speak to someone left behind, a young woman, a child and a mother, describe how a soldier died, and end with the same comfort: “Do not weep. / War is kind.” The more often the phrase returns, the more bitter it sounds.

:::

:::summary
- The speaker of a poem is not necessarily the poet; often it is an invented persona.
- A dramatic monologue is spoken by one character to a silent listener. Ask who is speaking, to whom and where, and what the speaker reveals without meaning to.
- Irony is a gap between what is said or expected and what is true: verbal (saying the opposite of what is meant), situational (events reverse expectations) and dramatic (the reader knows more than a character).
- Explain irony by naming the gap and saying what it shows: about the speaker, or about the subject, such as war or wealth.
:::
