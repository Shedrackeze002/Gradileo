One Sunday morning in a New England village, the well-liked minister, Mr. Hooper, walks into church looking exactly as usual, except for one thing:

> Swathed about his forehead, and hanging down over his face, so low as to be shaken by his breath, Mr. Hooper had on a black veil.
>
> — Nathaniel Hawthorne, “The Minister’s Black Veil” (1836)

He never explains it, and he never takes it off. His congregation spends the rest of the story asking what it means, and so do we. In this lesson you will interpret **symbols** and trace how their meaning develops, and read whole stories as **allegories**.

## Symbols

:::definition Symbol
A **symbol** is an object, person, place or action that is real in the story and also stands for something beyond itself, usually an idea or a feeling. Some symbols are **conventional** (a dove for peace, winter for old age); others are **contextual**, and get their meaning only from the story.
:::

The black veil is a piece of real crape: it flutters with Mr. Hooper’s breath and darkens everything he looks at. But it soon means much more. Hawthorne lets its meaning grow over the whole story, until the minister lies dying:

> All through life that piece of crape had hung between him and the world: it had separated him from cheerful brotherhood and woman’s love, and kept him in that saddest of all prisons, his own heart;
>
> — Nathaniel Hawthorne, “The Minister’s Black Veil” (1836)

The veil began as a mystery. By the end it is a barrier and a prison, and in his last speech Mr. Hooper says he sees a black veil “on every visage”: on every face. It now stands for the secret sin he believes every person hides.

:::tip How to read a symbol
Look for an object the story keeps returning to or lingers over. Note what is happening to the characters each time it appears, and pay special attention to the ending. Then check that your reading fits every appearance, not just one.
:::

## Motifs

:::definition Motif
A **motif** is an image, object or idea that recurs through a story. Each return adds meaning, and a motif often supports the story’s theme.
:::

In “Young Goodman Brown”, the pink ribbons on Faith’s cap return at each turning point. As Brown leaves, “the wind” plays with them at the door. Deep in the forest, one comes fluttering down from the dark sky:

> But something fluttered lightly down through the air and caught on the branch of a tree. The young man seized it, and beheld a pink ribbon. “My Faith is gone!” cried he, after one stupefied moment. “There is no good on earth; and sin is but a name. Come, devil; for to thee is this world given.”
>
> — Nathaniel Hawthorne, “Young Goodman Brown” (1835)

The next morning Faith runs to meet him, still wearing her pink ribbons. The ribbons stand for her innocence, and their journey through the story raises the story’s great question: was she ever really in the forest at all?

:::

:::mistake Not everything is a symbol
An object is not a symbol just because it is mentioned. A cup of tea is usually just tea. Treat something as a symbol when the story gives it weight through repetition, emphasis or a key position, and support your reading with evidence from the text.
:::

## Allegory

:::definition Allegory
An **allegory** is a story in which characters, places and events stand for ideas, often moral, religious or political ones. The story works on two levels at once: the literal events, and the lesson they stand for. A **parable** is a short allegory that teaches a moral lesson.
:::

The difference is one of scale. A symbol is one element of a story; in an allegory, the whole story forms a system of meanings. In John Bunyan’s *The Pilgrim’s Progress* (1678), a man named Christian travels through places called the Slough of Despond and Vanity Fair: the names announce what they stand for.

Hawthorne’s allegories are less direct, but they give clear signals. Goodman Brown leaves the village (daylight, church, order) and his wife, Faith, “as the wife was aptly named”, to walk into the dark forest. There he meets a traveller who looks like an older Brown, and who carries a strange staff:

> But the only thing about him that could be fixed upon as remarkable was his staff, which bore the likeness of a great black snake, so curiously wrought that it might almost be seen to twist and wriggle itself like a living serpent.
>
> — Nathaniel Hawthorne, “Young Goodman Brown” (1835)

The snake points back to the serpent in the Garden of Eden: the traveller is the devil. At the allegorical level, Brown’s night walk is every person’s encounter with temptation and doubt.

:::example Reading an allegory
> A woman named Patience planted an orchard, though she knew she would be old before it bore fruit. Her neighbour Haste laughed and planted flowers that bloomed in a week and were dead within a month. Years later, when hard times came to the village, everyone ate from the orchard of Patience.

What does the story teach, and how do you know it is an allegory?
@step Look at the names: Patience and Haste are qualities, not ordinary names. That signals an allegory.
@step Match the events to the ideas: Patience waits and builds something lasting; Haste wants quick results, and they are soon gone.
@answer The story teaches that patient, long-term effort lasts while hasty shortcuts fail. The characters’ names and the neat moral pattern of the events show that the story is an allegory.
:::

:::tip Keep both levels in view
An allegory still works as a story. A strong reading explains the idea each element stands for and shows how the literal events (what happens to Brown, or to the ships, or to the orchard) carry that idea.

:::summary
- A symbol is real in the story and also stands for an idea. Its meaning can develop, as the black veil grows from a mystery into a prison.
- To interpret a symbol, look at every appearance, what is happening to the characters, and the ending.
- A motif is a recurring image, like Faith’s pink ribbons, and it often supports the theme.
- An allegory is a whole story whose characters, places and events stand for ideas; a parable is a short allegory that teaches a moral.
- Names, settings and patterns of events are the clearest signals of an allegory.
:::
