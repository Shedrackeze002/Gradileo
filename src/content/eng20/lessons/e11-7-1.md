Read these sentences aloud: *The storm hit the coast. The power went out. The town was dark for two days. Volunteers handed out candles.* Every sentence is correct, but together they sound like a list of facts read by a robot, and they never say how the facts are connected. Now try: *When the storm knocked out the power, the town was dark for two days, so volunteers handed out candles.* The ideas are the same, but the reader now knows what caused what. In this lesson you will learn the three kinds of dependent clause and use them, with phrases, to combine sentences so they show how ideas relate.

## Clauses

:::definition Independent and dependent clauses
A **clause** is a group of words with a subject and a verb. An **independent clause** makes sense on its own as a sentence. A **dependent clause** cannot stand alone; it does a job inside a sentence, the job of a noun, an adjective or an adverb.
:::

Every dependent clause is one of three kinds, named after the part of speech whose job it does.

### Noun clauses

:::definition Noun clause
A **noun clause** does the job of a noun: it can be the subject of the sentence, the object of a verb, the object of a preposition, or the part after *is* that says what the subject is. Noun clauses often begin with *that*, *what*, *whether*, *if*, *who*, *whoever*, *how*, *why*, *where* or *when*.
:::

- Subject: ***What Ravi said at the meeting** surprised everyone.*
- Object of a verb: *Hana admitted **that she had forgotten the tickets**.*
- Object of a preposition: *We argued about **how the money should be spent**.*
- After *is*: *The problem is **that the shop closes at five**.*

:::tip The “something” test
Replace the clause with the word *something*. If the sentence still makes sense (*Something surprised everyone*; *Hana admitted something*), the clause is a noun clause.
:::

### Adjective (relative) clauses

:::definition Adjective clause
An **adjective clause**, also called a **relative clause**, describes a noun. It usually comes straight after that noun and begins with *who*, *whom*, *whose*, *which*, *that*, *where* or *when*: *The woman **who lives next door** is a pilot.*
:::

Use *who* and *whom* for people, and *which* for things. *That* can be used for either in a clause that identifies which one is meant.

### Adverb clauses

:::definition Adverb clause
An **adverb clause** tells when, where, why, under what condition or in spite of what the main action happens. It begins with a word such as *when*, *until*, *after*, *because*, *since*, *if*, *unless*, *although* or *even though*.
:::

- Time: ***When the bell rang**, the students rushed outside.*
- Reason: *The match was cancelled **because the pitch was flooded**.*
- Condition: ***If the ferry is running**, we will cross tomorrow.*
- Contrast: ***Although the road was icy**, the ambulance arrived on time.*

When an adverb clause comes first, a comma follows it.

:::

:::mistake Judging a clause by its first word
The same word can open different kinds of clause, so the first word never decides. Compare:

- *No one knows **when the storm will reach the coast**.* (a noun clause: the object of *knows*)
- *I remember the day **when we moved to the city**.* (an adjective clause: it describes *day*)
- *Call your grandmother **when you get home**.* (an adverb clause: it tells when to call)

Ask what job the clause does in its sentence.
:::

## Combining sentences

Clauses and phrases let you join short sentences so that the result shows how the ideas relate. Five tools do most of the work.

:::definition Five ways to combine
1. **Coordination**: a comma and *and*, *but*, *so* or *yet* joins two independent clauses of equal weight.
2. **Subordination**: *because*, *although*, *when* or *if* turns one idea into an adverb clause and shows cause, contrast, time or condition.
3. **A relative clause** adds a detail about a noun: *My aunt, **who lives in Mombasa**, is a marine biologist.*
4. **An appositive** renames a noun in a few words: *Lagos, **the largest city in Nigeria**, is home to millions of people.*
5. **A participial phrase** describes the subject: ***Opening the envelope**, Diego found two tickets.*
:::

The most important choice is the connecting word. *Because* and *so* show a cause; *although* and *but* show a contrast. Choose the wrong one and the sentence says something false.

:::example Combining three sentences
Combine: *Mei was exhausted after the long shift. She fell asleep on the bus. She missed her stop.*
@step Decide how the ideas relate: the tiredness explains the sleep, and the sleep explains the missed stop.
@step Turn the first sentence into a participial phrase that describes Mei: *Exhausted after the long shift*.
@step Join the last two actions with one subject and two verbs: *fell asleep on the bus and missed her stop*.
@step Check that the subject straight after the comma is the person who was exhausted: *Mei*.
@answer *Exhausted after the long shift, Mei fell asleep on the bus and missed her stop.*
:::

:::check e11-sentence-combining 1
:::

:::mistake Four ways a combination goes wrong
- **A comma splice**: *Mei was exhausted, she fell asleep on the bus.* Two independent clauses need more than a comma.
- **The wrong relation**: *Although Mei was exhausted, she fell asleep.* Tiredness causes sleep; it does not contrast with it.
- **A dangling modifier**: *Exhausted after the long shift, the bus carried Mei past her stop.* The bus was not exhausted.
- **A clause on the wrong noun**: *The mayor who cleaned the beach thanked the volunteers* says the mayor did the cleaning. Put the relative clause straight after the noun it describes: *The mayor thanked the volunteers who cleaned the beach.*
:::

:::summary
- A noun clause does the job of a noun (subject, object, or after *is*); an adjective (relative) clause describes a noun; an adverb clause tells when, why, under what condition or in spite of what.
- The job a clause does, not its first word, decides its kind.
- Combine sentences with coordination, subordination, relative clauses, appositives and participial phrases.
- The connecting word must show the true relation: cause, contrast, time or condition.
- Check every combination for comma splices, fragments, dangling modifiers and clauses attached to the wrong noun.
:::
