In a phone advert, a grandmother holds the phone upside down until her grandson rescues her. It is meant to be a gentle joke. But if every advert shows older people baffled by technology, viewers absorb a picture of a whole group that is simply untrue. Media do not only report the world; they **represent** it, and what they show again and again shapes what people expect. In this lesson you will look at how media represent people, and at how digital media decide what you see.

## Representation and stereotypes

:::definition Representation and stereotype
**Representation** is the way media show people, groups, places and ideas. A **stereotype** is an oversimplified idea that all the members of a group are the same: that teenagers are lazy, that older people cannot cope with technology, that engineering is for boys.
:::

Stereotypes do harm even when they are meant as jokes. Repeated often enough, they shape how people see others, and how people see themselves: a girl who never sees a girl with an engineering kit may decide engineering is not for her.

| Portrayal | Stereotype it relies on |
|---|---|
| Every teenager in a comedy is slumped on a sofa, ignoring adults. | Teenagers are lazy and rude. |
| Only mothers clean the house in a cleaning-product advert. | Housework is women’s work. |
| The only scientist in a film is an awkward man who cannot hold a conversation. | Scientists are awkward and isolated. |
| The only country character in a cartoon is slow-witted and easily fooled. | Country people are less clever than city people. |

### Whose voice is heard?

Representation is also about **who gets to speak**. A report on a plan to close a youth centre that quotes the mayor, a councillor and a local business owner leaves out the people most affected: the young people who use the centre. Readers then hear only the views of those with power over the decision.

:::example Finding the missing voice
> A report on a strike by bus drivers quotes the bus company’s chief executive, a city councillor and two passengers.

@step Check the list of people quoted: the company, a councillor and passengers.
@answer **The bus drivers.** Without them, readers never hear why the strike is happening, only how it affects others.

## Digital media: what decides what you see?

Online, much of what you see is chosen *for* you, and some of it is not what it seems.

:::definition Five terms for digital media
- A **recommendation algorithm** chooses what to show you next from what you have already watched, clicked or bought.
- An **echo chamber** is a group or space where people hear only views that match their own, because other views are pushed out.
- **Sponsored content** is paid promotion made to look like ordinary posts or articles. Honest sponsored content says clearly that it is paid.
- **Clickbait** is a headline or title that exaggerates or teases to get clicks, then fails to deliver.
- **Misinformation** is false information that spreads, often because it is shocking and people share it without checking.
:::

Algorithms and echo chambers can work together: a feed that shows you only what you already like, and a group that removes anyone who disagrees, both leave you hearing one side.

:::mistake Disclosure hidden away is not disclosure
An influencer who says “my partnerships are listed in a link on my profile” has not really told viewers that this post is paid. A clear disclosure appears in the post itself, at the start, in plain words: *Ad: Brightsmile paid me to try their toothpaste.*

### Checking a viral claim

When a shocking post goes viral, the instinct is to study the post itself: to reread it, check its likes, scan the comments. Professional fact-checkers do the opposite. They read **laterally**: they leave the post and open new tabs to see what trusted news sources, official websites and fact-checking organizations say about the claim, and who is behind the post.

:::tip Before you share
Ask three quick questions: *Who made this? What do other reliable sources say? Is the photo or quotation really from where it claims to be?* A popular post is not the same as a true one.
:::

:::summary
- **Representation** is how media show people. A **stereotype** reduces a whole group to one trait, and repeated stereotypes shape how people see others and themselves.
- Ask **whose voice is heard**: the people most affected by a story are often the ones left out.
- Online, **algorithms** and **echo chambers** narrow what you see; **sponsored content** should say clearly that it is paid; **clickbait** teases; **misinformation** spreads because it shocks.
- Check a viral claim by **reading laterally**: leave the post and see what reliable sources say.
:::
