A documentary feels like a window onto reality: real places, real people, real events. But every documentary is made of choices: whom to interview, which footage to use, what the narrator says, which music plays, and which shot follows which. Those choices can inform viewers, and they can also persuade them. In this lesson you will learn the main techniques of documentary film and the effect each has on an audience.

## The techniques of documentary

:::definition Six techniques
- An **expert interview** lets someone with special knowledge or first-hand experience speak to the camera, lending the film authority.
- **Voice-over narration** is an unseen voice that explains the images and tells viewers how to understand them.
- **Archival footage** is real film or photographs from the past.
- A **reconstruction** (re-enactment) uses actors to show an event that no camera recorded.
- **Music** added to the soundtrack tells viewers how to feel about what they see.
- **Editing** places shots side by side, so that viewers connect them. Placing two contrasting shots together is called **juxtaposition**.
:::

| In the film | Technique |
|---|---|
| A professor of ocean science explains how plastic breaks down in sea water. | expert interview |
| An unseen voice says, over an empty factory, “Here, a whole town once made its living.” | voice-over narration |
| Grainy black-and-white film shows the town’s first ship being launched in 1920. | archival footage |
| Actors in period costume show a secret meeting that no one filmed. | reconstruction |
| Low, slow strings play over an empty playground at dusk. | music |
| A shot of a smiling company boss is followed by a shot of the polluted river beside his factory. | editing (juxtaposition) |

## How the techniques persuade

Each technique shapes what viewers believe or feel, often without their noticing.

- An **expert** makes a claim sound settled. Ask whose expertise it is, and whether other experts would disagree.
- **Narration** interprets the images before viewers can. The same shots of an empty factory could be narrated as a tragedy or as progress.
- **Archival footage** feels like proof, because it is real. But someone chose which footage to show.
- A **reconstruction** makes an unseen event vivid, but its details (what people said, how they looked) are the film-makers’ choices.
- **Music** sets the mood. Threatening music under one side’s spokesperson and hopeful music under the other’s takes sides without a word.
- **Editing** suggests links. Two shots placed together invite viewers to connect them, even when the film never says they are connected.

:::example Explaining the effect of editing
> Shots of children walking miles to fetch water are placed straight after shots of a lawn being watered in the rain.

What effect does this have on viewers?
@step Name the technique: two contrasting shots placed side by side, which is editing (juxtaposition).
@step Ask what viewers do with the two shots: they compare them.
@step The comparison sets scarcity against waste.
@answer It makes viewers compare the two and feel the unfairness, though the film never states it.
:::

:::mistake Real footage is not the whole truth
Footage can be completely genuine and still mislead, through what comes before and after it, what the narrator says over it, or what was left out. Judge a documentary by its choices, not only by whether its pictures are real.
:::

## Point of view and balance

Every documentary has a **point of view**, even when no one in it says “I think”. You can find it by looking at the pattern of choices across the whole film:

- **Who speaks, and for how long?** A film about a new dam that interviews five opponents and one engineer, briefly, leans one way.
- **Who has the last word?** The final interview or line of narration is the one viewers remember.
- **How is each side filmed?** One side may be shot in warm daylight in their homes, the other in a dark office from a low angle.
- **What does the music do?** Music that changes with the speaker tells viewers whom to trust.

A documentary with a clear point of view is not necessarily dishonest; many of the best ones argue openly. The problem comes when a film presents itself as neutral while its choices quietly take a side.

:::example Reading a documentary’s point of view
A documentary about a plan to build a wind farm on a hill opens with archival footage of the hill in spring, under soft piano music. A local farmer, filmed in her kitchen, says the view has been in her family for a century. The energy company’s spokesperson appears once, for twenty seconds, filmed through a glass office wall. The film ends on the farmer, silent, looking at the hill.

What point of view does the film take, and how?
@step **Who speaks:** the farmer speaks at length; the company only briefly.
@step **How they are filmed:** the farmer warmly, at home; the spokesperson behind glass, at a distance.
@step **Music and footage:** gentle piano and old film make the hill something precious and fragile.
@step **The last word:** the film ends on the farmer.
@answer It opposes the wind farm. Without ever stating an opinion, its choice of speakers, filming, music and ending all lead viewers to side with the farmer.
:::

:::summary
- Documentaries are built from **expert interviews**, **voice-over narration**, **archival footage**, **reconstructions**, **music** and **editing**.
- Each technique can inform and persuade: experts lend authority, narration interprets, archival footage feels like proof, reconstruction imagines, music sets the mood, and editing suggests links.
- Real footage can still mislead. Judge a documentary by the choices behind it.
:::
