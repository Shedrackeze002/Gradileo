:::note Spoiler
This lesson explains how the Witches’ prophecies come true at the end of *Macbeth*, including how Macbeth dies.
:::

Macbeth and Banquo are crossing a heath on their way home from battle when three strange figures block their path. One after another, they greet Macbeth:

> **First Witch.** All hail, Macbeth! hail to thee, Thane of Glamis!
>
> **Second Witch.** All hail, Macbeth! hail to thee, Thane of Cawdor!
>
> **Third Witch.** All hail, Macbeth! that shalt be king hereafter!
>
> — *Macbeth*, Act 1, Scene 3

Macbeth is already Thane of Glamis. He does not know that the King has just given him Cawdor’s title too, and the third greeting promises the crown. Shakespeare’s first audiences took witches seriously: King James, who became Shakespeare’s patron in 1603, had written a book about witchcraft, and Banquo was believed to be one of his ancestors. In this lesson you will see how Shakespeare uses the supernatural and other **dramatic devices** to let the audience into Macbeth’s mind, and to let us know more than the characters do.

## Who is listening?

:::definition Kinds of speech in a play
- **Dialogue**: a conversation between two or more characters.
- **Monologue**: a long speech by one character to other characters, who listen.
- **Soliloquy**: a speech in which a character alone on stage speaks their thoughts aloud, so that only the audience hears.
- **Aside**: a short remark to the audience that the other characters on stage do not hear.
:::

Macbeth’s soliloquies show his mind falling apart. Alone, on his way to Duncan’s room, he sees a dagger floating in the air:

> Is this a dagger which I see before me,\
> The handle toward my hand? Come, let me clutch thee:—\
> I have thee not, and yet I see thee still.\
> Art thou not, fatal vision, sensible\
> To feeling as to sight? or art thou but\
> A dagger of the mind, a false creation,\
> Proceeding from the heat-oppressed brain?
>
> — Macbeth, *Macbeth*, Act 2, Scene 1

His asides show what he hides from the people around him. Moments after he hears that he is Thane of Cawdor, with Banquo and two noblemen beside him, he says to himself:

> [*Aside.*] If chance will have me king, why, chance may crown me\
> Without my stir.
>
> — Macbeth, *Macbeth*, Act 1, Scene 3

## Prophecy, apparitions and equivocation

:::definition Prophecy, apparition and equivocation
- A **prophecy** is a prediction of what will happen, often from a supernatural source.
- An **apparition** is a ghostly figure or vision.
- **Equivocation** is speaking in words with two meanings, so as to mislead without telling a plain lie.
:::

In Act 4, Macbeth goes back to the Witches, who raise three apparitions. An armed head warns him to beware Macduff. A bloody child promises something that sounds like safety:

> Be bloody, bold, and resolute. Laugh to scorn\
> The power of man, for none of woman born\
> Shall harm Macbeth.
>
> — Second Apparition, *Macbeth*, Act 4, Scene 1

Then a crowned child with a tree in his hand promises that Macbeth will never be defeated until Birnam Wood comes to Dunsinane hill. A forest cannot walk, so Macbeth feels safe. Both promises are **equivocations**. In Act 5, Malcolm’s soldiers each cut a branch from Birnam Wood to hide their numbers, so the wood seems to move. And Macduff was not “born” in the usual way: he was “from his mother’s womb / Untimely ripp’d”, delivered early by surgery. Too late, Macbeth sees the trick:

> And be these juggling fiends no more believ’d,\
> That palter with us in a double sense;\
> That keep the word of promise to our ear,\
> And break it to our hope!
>
> — Macbeth, *Macbeth*, Act 5, Scene 8

:::mistake Prophecy or foreshadowing?
A **prophecy** is spoken by a character, usually a supernatural one, and it says outright what will happen. **Foreshadowing** is the playwright’s hint: a detail whose meaning we only see later. Duncan’s complete trust in the first Thane of Cawdor, a traitor, foreshadows his trust in the next Thane of Cawdor, Macbeth.
:::

## Comic relief and stage directions

**Comic relief** is a funny scene, speech or character that eases the tension of a serious play. Right after the murder, while the knocking at the gate grows louder, a drunken Porter stumbles to answer it, pretending he is the gatekeeper of hell. The audience laughs, but the joke has a dark edge: Macbeth’s castle has just become a kind of hell.

**Stage directions** are the instructions in the script that tell the actors what to do. In *Macbeth* they often carry the supernatural: *[Thunder. An Apparition of an armed Head rises.]*, or *The Ghost of Banquo rises, and sits in Macbeth’s place.* The repeated *[Knocking within.]* after the murder is only a sound, but it seems to the Macbeths like the world demanding to be let in.

## Dramatic irony

:::definition Dramatic irony
**Dramatic irony** happens when the audience knows something important that a character on stage does not.
:::

In Act 1, Duncan has just had the traitor Cawdor executed, and he regrets how completely he trusted him:

> There’s no art\
> To find the mind’s construction in the face:\
> He was a gentleman on whom I built\
> An absolute trust.
>
> — Duncan, *Macbeth*, Act 1, Scene 4

At that very moment Macbeth walks in, and Duncan greets him warmly. We have just heard Macbeth thinking about murder; Duncan is about to trust the wrong man a second time. When the King arrives at Macbeth’s castle and praises its “pleasant seat”, the irony is sharper still: we know Lady Macbeth plans to have him killed there that night.

:::example Explaining dramatic irony
At the banquet in Act 3, Scene 4, Macbeth raises his cup: “I drink to the general joy o’ th’ whole table, / And to our dear friend Banquo, whom we miss: / Would he were here.” Explain the dramatic irony and its effect.
@step Ask what the audience knows: Macbeth has had Banquo murdered that night, and earlier in this scene a murderer came to tell him it was done.
@step Ask what the lords believe: they hear a king wishing his absent friend were at the feast.
@step Ask what the gap does: Macbeth’s friendly words are a lie, and the moment he speaks them, Banquo’s ghost appears again.
@answer The audience knows that Banquo is dead at Macbeth’s orders; the lords do not. The irony makes the toast chilling, and when the ghost appears, it seems to answer Macbeth’s false wish, which shows that his guilt will not stay hidden.
:::

:::tip Three kinds of irony
**Dramatic irony** is about what the audience knows that a character does not. **Verbal irony** is saying the opposite of what you mean, as when Lennox calls Macbeth’s killing of the grooms “nobly done”. **Situational irony** is an outcome that is the opposite of what was expected: Macbeth kills to become secure, and is never secure again.
:::

:::summary
- A soliloquy is spoken alone; an aside is a quick remark the other characters do not hear; a monologue is a long speech to others; dialogue is conversation.
- The Witches’ prophecies and apparitions equivocate: Birnam Wood does come to Dunsinane, and Macduff was not born in the usual way.
- Comic relief, such as the Porter’s scene, eases tension; stage directions bring on ghosts, apparitions and the knocking at the gate.
- Dramatic irony, as when Duncan trusts Macbeth, lets the audience see what the characters cannot, and creates dread and suspense.
:::
