Commas and periods can carry most sentences, but some ideas need a stronger signal. *Kwame opened the door and found the room empty* is a plain report. *Kwame opened the door—and found the room completely empty* makes the reader pause before the surprise. In this lesson you will use the marks that give writers that control: dashes, colons and parentheses, and the square brackets and ellipses used inside quotations.

## Dashes

:::definition The dash
A **dash** (—) marks a strong break in a sentence. A **pair of dashes** sets off an interruption or extra detail in the middle of a sentence, like brackets. A **single dash** creates a pause before a sudden turn, or gives emphasis to what comes at the end.
:::

- A pair: *The old bridge—built more than a century ago—still carries traffic every day.*
- A single dash: *After years of practice, Ravi finally had what he wanted—a place in the orchestra.*

With a pair, the sentence must still read correctly if you lift out the dashes and everything between them. An interruption that opens with a dash must close with a dash, not a comma.

:::example Punctuating an interruption
Punctuate with dashes: *My grandmother a champion swimmer in her youth still swims every morning.*
@step Find the main sentence: *My grandmother still swims every morning.*
@step Find the words that interrupt it: *a champion swimmer in her youth*.
@step Put a dash before the interruption and another after it, with no commas.
@step Check: lift out the dashes and the words between them, and the sentence still reads correctly.
@answer *My grandmother—a champion swimmer in her youth—still swims every morning.*
:::

:::note A matter of style
Publishers write dashes in different ways: some use a long dash with no spaces (—), others a shorter dash with a space on each side ( – ). Both are correct. This course uses the long dash with no spaces; choose one style and keep it throughout a piece of writing.
:::

## Colons

:::definition The colon
A **colon** (:) comes after a **complete clause** and points forward to what follows: a list, an explanation, or the thing the clause has promised.
:::

- A list: *The kit contains everything a climber needs: a rope, a harness, a helmet and gloves.*
- An explanation: *The reason for the delay was simple: the pilot was ill.*
- The thing promised: *Leila had trained for one goal: a place in the national team.*

:::mistake A colon after an incomplete clause
A colon never comes straight after a verb or a preposition, because what comes before it is not yet complete. *The recipe needs: cumin, turmeric and chilli* and *Leila had trained for: one goal* are both wrong. Either leave the colon out or complete the clause: *The recipe needs three spices: cumin, turmeric and chilli.*
:::

## Parentheses

:::definition Parentheses
**Parentheses** ( ) enclose extra information that the sentence could do without: *The museum (which reopened last spring) has a new hall of dinosaur fossils.*
:::

When the words in parentheses are part of a sentence, the sentence’s own punctuation goes **outside** the closing parenthesis: *We met at the old station (the one near the river).* Do not put a comma before an opening parenthesis.

:::tip Dashes, commas or parentheses?
All three can set off extra information, but they sound different. **Dashes** make the extra words stand out. **Commas** are neutral. **Parentheses** quietly play the words down, like an aside.
:::

## Marks inside quotations

When you quote a source, the words inside the quotation marks must be exactly the speaker’s or writer’s. Two marks let you shorten or clarify a quotation honestly.

:::definition Square brackets and the ellipsis
**Square brackets** [ ] show words that **you** have added to or changed in a quotation, often to make clear who *he*, *she*, *they* or *it* means: “[The two climbers] finally reached the summit at noon.”

An **ellipsis** (…) shows where you have **left words out** of a quotation.
:::

:::example Shortening a quotation
The original sentence: “The bridge will close on Monday, for safety reasons, and will reopen when the repairs are complete.” Quote it without the words *for safety reasons*.
@step Find the words to remove: *, for safety reasons,* (with their commas).
@step Put an ellipsis exactly where they were.
@step Read the result: it must still say what the original said.
@answer “The bridge will close on Monday … and will reopen when the repairs are complete.”
:::

:::mistake An ellipsis that changes the meaning
Leaving words out must never change what was said. From “I did not think, at first, that the plan would work, but the results have proved me wrong,” the quotation “I … think … that the plan would work” cuts *did not* and reverses the speaker’s meaning. That is misquoting, however carefully the ellipses are placed.
:::

In dialogue, an ellipsis can also show a voice trailing off: “I was going to tell you,” Grace said quietly, “but then …”

:::summary
- A pair of dashes sets off an interruption; a single dash creates a pause before a sudden turn or emphasis. Close an interruption the way you opened it.
- A colon follows a complete clause and introduces a list, an explanation or what the clause has promised; never put one straight after a verb or preposition.
- Parentheses enclose extra information; the sentence must work without it, and its own punctuation goes outside the closing parenthesis.
- In a quotation, square brackets mark words you added or changed, and an ellipsis marks words you left out, without ever changing the meaning.
:::
