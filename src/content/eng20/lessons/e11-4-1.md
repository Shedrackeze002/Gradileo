In 1625, Francis Bacon began an essay with a sentence that still sounds like a teacher’s advice:

> Some books are to be tasted, others to be swallowed, and some few to be chewed and digested …
>
> — Francis Bacon, “Of Studies” (1625)

The word *essay* comes from the French *essayer*, “to try”: an essay is an attempt to think something through on the page. Some essays explain, some argue, some remember and some mock. In this lesson you will learn to tell these kinds of essay apart and to find an essay’s **central idea**, whether the writer states it or leaves you to work it out.

## Four kinds of essay

:::definition Kinds of essay
- An **expository** essay explains or informs. It sets out facts, causes or processes without arguing for one side: *how a volcano forms*, *why the seasons change*.
- An **argumentative** essay takes a position and supports it with reasons and evidence, often answering the other side: *why every city should make its buses free*.
- A **personal** (or **reflective**) essay explores the writer’s own experience and what the writer has come to understand from it: *the summer I worked in my grandmother’s bakery*.
- A **satirical** essay uses irony, exaggeration or mock seriousness to make a target look ridiculous, in order to criticize it: *a solemn proposal to grade students on how quietly they breathe*.
:::

The quickest test is to ask what the writer is mainly *doing*. Explaining? Arguing? Remembering and reflecting? Or mocking?

Often the opening tells you. Compare four openings on the same subject, sleep:

| Opening | Kind |
|---|---|
| *During sleep, the brain passes through several stages in cycles of about ninety minutes.* | expository |
| *Secondary schools should start no earlier than nine o’clock, because teenagers need more sleep.* | argumentative |
| *For a whole term I slept four hours a night and called it ambition.* | personal |
| *Sleep is clearly a waste of a third of our lives; schools should award prizes for staying awake.* | satirical |

:::

:::tip Kinds overlap
Real essays mix their methods. A personal essay can argue a point, and a satire always argues one, indirectly. When you name the kind, name what the essay is **mainly** doing. Swift’s “A Modest Proposal” argues about Irish poverty, but it does so by mocking, so it is a satire.
:::

:::example Naming the kind of essay
Chesterton opens “A Piece of Chalk” (1909) this way:

> I remember one splendid morning, all blue and silver, in the summer holidays when I reluctantly tore myself away from the task of doing nothing in particular, and put on a hat of some sort and picked up a walking-stick, and put six very bright-coloured chalks in my pocket.
>
> — G. K. Chesterton, “A Piece of Chalk” (1909)

What kind of essay does it begin?
@step The writer speaks as “I” and remembers a moment from his own life.
@step He is not explaining a process, arguing for a position or mocking anyone.
@answer A **personal (reflective)** essay: it begins from a small memory that the essay will find meaning in.
:::

## The central idea

:::definition Central idea
The **central idea** of an essay or passage is the main point the whole of it makes. A **stated** central idea appears in a sentence of its own, often at the beginning or end. An **implied** central idea is never stated: the reader works it out from what all the details have in common.
:::

When the idea is stated, look for the one general sentence that the others support with specific examples:

> Traffic on the main road rarely falls silent before midnight. Delivery lorries begin unloading outside the shops at five in the morning. Residents near the airport hear a plane roughly every two minutes. Noise is one of the most overlooked costs of city life.

The first three sentences are details; the last is the claim they support.

:::check e11-central-idea 1
:::

Older essays often pack a central idea into a striking image. Thoreau writes:

> Our life is frittered away by detail. An honest man has hardly need to count more than his ten fingers, or in extreme cases he may add his ten toes, and lump the rest. Simplicity, simplicity, simplicity!
>
> — Henry David Thoreau, *Walden* (1854)

He does not really want readers to count on their toes. His point is that people waste their lives on unnecessary detail, and should simplify them.

:::mistake Four ways to misread a central idea
A statement of the central idea must cover the whole passage. Rule out choices that:
- **repeat one detail** (“People should count on their fingers”);
- are **too broad** to say anything (“Life is complicated”);
- take a **figure of speech literally**;
- say the **opposite** of the passage (“A busy life with many affairs is best”).
:::

## Implied ideas

When nothing is stated, gather the details and ask what they add up to:

> Every evening at six, my grandmother set the table for four, though only two of us lived in the house by then. She folded the napkins into the same neat triangles and set out the blue plates my mother had loved. She never explained, and I never asked.

No sentence says *she is remembering the family members who are gone*, but every detail (the empty places, the absent mother’s plates, the silence) points to it.

:::example Stating an implied central idea
> The new shopping centre has six cinemas, forty shops and a fountain that changes colour every minute. It has no benches. Security guards ask teenagers who sit on the steps to move along.

@step List what the centre offers: cinemas, shops, a fountain. All of them cost money or attract spending.
@step List what it lacks or forbids: benches, and teenagers simply sitting.
@step Put the two lists together as one general statement.
@answer The shopping centre welcomes people who spend money, but not people who simply want somewhere to be.
:::

:::check e11-central-idea 3
:::

:::summary
- An **expository** essay explains; an **argumentative** essay argues for a position; a **personal** essay reflects on the writer’s experience; a **satirical** essay mocks a target to criticize it. Name what the essay is mainly doing.
- The **central idea** is the main point of the whole passage. It may be **stated** in one general sentence or **implied** by the details.
- Test a statement of the central idea against every detail. Rule out details, statements too broad to mean anything, literal readings of figures of speech, and opposites.
:::
