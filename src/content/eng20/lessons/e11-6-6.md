In an exam, a literary essay has to be planned, written and checked against the clock. Most marks lost in timed writing are not lost for lack of ideas. They are lost because the essay answered half the question, ran out of time before the conclusion, or was handed in full of errors that one reread would have caught. In this lesson you will break a prompt into its tasks, plan quickly, manage your time and finish strongly.

## Reading the prompt

A literary essay prompt often contains more than one task. Find every key verb and what it applies to.

| Key verb | What it asks |
|---|---|
| **Analyse** | Examine how the writer’s choices create meaning or effects. |
| **Explain** | Make clear how or why something happens. |
| **Compare** / **Contrast** | Show likenesses / differences between texts, and what they reveal. |
| **Evaluate** | Judge how effective or successful something is, with reasons. |
| **Discuss** | Consider a question from more than one angle and reach a conclusion. |
| **To what extent** | Weigh the evidence for and against, and give a measured answer. |

:::example Breaking a prompt into tasks
Break down this prompt: *Analyse how Kate Chopin uses the scene outside Mrs. Mallard’s window to reveal her inner life in “The Story of an Hour”, and evaluate whether the final sentence is an effective ending.*
@step Underline the key verbs: *analyse* and *evaluate*.
@step Find what each applies to: *analyse* → the window scene and what it reveals; *evaluate* → the final sentence.
@step Notice the *and*: there are two tasks, and an essay that answers only one of them is incomplete.
@answer Task 1: analyse how the window scene reveals Mrs. Mallard’s inner life. Task 2: judge, with reasons, whether the final sentence is an effective ending.
:::

:::mistake Answering the question you hoped for
If you prepared an essay on imagery and the prompt asks you to *evaluate the ending*, a prepared imagery essay will not do. Use what you know, but shape it to the tasks in front of you.

## Planning in ten minutes

A plan does not need full sentences. In the first few minutes of a timed essay:

1. Jot down ideas and evidence: moments, quotations, images.
2. Look for what they have in common, and write a **thesis** that answers every task in the prompt.
3. Choose two or three body points, each with its evidence, and put them in order.

Here is a plan for the Chopin prompt:

- **Thesis:** Chopin’s spring imagery reveals a longing for freedom that Mrs. Mallard can barely admit, and the final sentence’s irony is an effective ending because it shows how completely that longing went unseen.
- **Body 1:** The window scene: “aquiver”, the “delicious breath of rain” and new life after death.
- **Body 2:** Her inner change: from grief to “free”, and her prayer that life might be long.
- **Body 3 (evaluate):** The doctors’ “joy that kills”: irony, and why it works.
- **Conclusion:** Return to the thesis: what the reader understands that the characters never do.

## Managing the clock

Spend roughly the first sixth of the time reading and planning, most of it writing, and the last few minutes checking.

| Total time | Read and plan | Write | Check |
|---|---|---|---|
| 45 minutes | 5 | 35 | 5 |
| 60 minutes | 10 | 45 | 5 |
| 90 minutes | 15 | 65 | 10 |

:::tip Never write a rough draft to copy out
In an exam there is no time to write an essay twice. Your plan is your rough draft; the essay you write from it is your final copy.
:::

### When time runs short

A complete essay scores better than a longer one that stops in the middle. If you have ten minutes left and one point to go, write a brief version of it and a short conclusion that returns to your thesis. If you have five minutes and no conclusion, write two or three sentences that answer the prompt. And if you notice late that your body paragraphs prove a slightly different point from your thesis, revise the thesis: one sentence brings the whole essay into line.

## Finishing strongly

Save the last minutes to reread. Check that:

- every paragraph answers the prompt, not just the topic;
- the thesis matches what the body paragraphs prove;
- quotations are accurate and worked into your sentences;
- spelling, punctuation and the names of characters and authors are correct.

:::summary
- Find every key verb in a prompt and what it applies to; a prompt with *and* usually sets more than one task.
- Plan in the first few minutes: ideas and evidence, a thesis that answers every task, and two or three ordered points.
- Divide the time: about a sixth to read and plan, most to write, a few minutes to check; never copy out a draft.
- When time runs short, finish the argument briefly; a complete essay beats an unfinished one.
:::
