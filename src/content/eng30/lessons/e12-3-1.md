William Shakespeare wrote *Hamlet* in about 1600, and it is the longest of his plays. Prince Hamlet’s father, the King of Denmark, has died suddenly, and within a month Hamlet’s mother, Gertrude, has married the dead King’s brother, Claudius, who now wears the crown. Then a ghost in the shape of the dead King appears and tells Hamlet that he was murdered. The play opens with a question, and it never stops asking them: who is there, what is real, and what should a man do? In this lesson you will learn to paraphrase the language of *Hamlet* and to trace the arguments of its soliloquies.

## Verse, prose and wordplay

Most of *Hamlet* is written in **blank verse**: unrhymed lines of about ten syllables with five beats, in a da-DUM rhythm. The King speaks it at court, and Hamlet speaks it when he is alone with his thoughts. But when Hamlet plays the madman he often slips into **prose**, ordinary sentences with no set rhythm, and his answers turn into riddles:

> **Polonius.** What do you read, my lord?
>
> **Hamlet.** Words, words, words.
>
> — *Hamlet*, Act 2, Scene 2

Hamlet is also the great punster of the play. His very first line, spoken aside so that only the audience hears it, is a play on words:

> **Claudius.** But now, my cousin Hamlet, and my son—
>
> **Hamlet.** [*Aside.*] A little more than kin, and less than kind.
>
> — *Hamlet*, Act 1, Scene 2

Claudius is now “more than kin” to Hamlet, his uncle and his stepfather at once, yet “less than kind”: not truly of the same kind or family, and not kindly either. In nine words Hamlet tells us how he feels about the new King.

:::tip Read to the punctuation, not to the end of the line
Shakespeare’s sentences often run over several lines. Read on until you reach a full stop, a question mark or a semicolon, and only then ask what the sentence means.
:::

## Paraphrasing Hamlet

:::definition Paraphrase
A **paraphrase** puts a passage into your own plain words, keeping every idea and image and adding nothing of your own. It is about as long as the original; a summary is much shorter.
:::

A few words in *Hamlet* have changed their meaning, or have fallen out of use, since Shakespeare’s day:

| Word | In the play | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| antic | “To put an antic disposition on” (Act 1, Scene 5) | strange and mad-seeming |
| arras | “Behind the arras hearing something stir” (Act 4, Scene 1) | a hanging tapestry |
| bodkin | “With a bare bodkin” (Act 3, Scene 1) | a dagger |
| bourn | “from whose bourn / No traveller returns” (Act 3, Scene 1) | a boundary or border |
| contumely | “the proud man’s contumely” (Act 3, Scene 1) | insulting contempt |
| quietus | “his quietus make” (Act 3, Scene 1) | release from life; death |
| unbated | “A sword unbated” (Act 4, Scene 7) | not blunted; with a sharp point |

:::mistake Words that look familiar
Some words look modern but meant something else. The **coil** in “this mortal coil” is not a coil of rope but turmoil, the fuss and trouble of life. A **rub** is not a massage but an obstacle, like a bump that turns a ball aside in the game of bowls. And a **quietus** is not peace and quiet: it was the settling of a debt, so to make one’s quietus is to end one’s life.
:::

:::example Paraphrasing a bitter joke
Horatio says he came to Elsinore for the old King’s funeral, and Hamlet suggests it was really for his mother’s wedding. Paraphrase Hamlet’s answer: “Thrift, thrift, Horatio! The funeral bak’d meats / Did coldly furnish forth the marriage tables.”
@step Find the situation: the wedding followed the funeral so quickly that the two feasts almost ran together.
@step Unpack the words: the “bak’d meats” are the pies and pastries made for the funeral, and “coldly furnish forth” means they were served up again, cold.
@step Keep the tone: Hamlet praises “thrift”, saving money, but he does not mean the praise.
@answer “It was to save money, Horatio! The pies baked for the funeral were served cold at the wedding feast.” Hamlet pretends to admire the economy, but he means that the marriage was disgracefully quick.
:::

## The soliloquies

:::definition Soliloquy
A **soliloquy** is a speech in which a character alone on stage speaks their thoughts aloud. No other character hears it, so it takes the audience inside the speaker’s mind.
:::

Hamlet hides his thoughts from almost everyone at court, so his soliloquies are where we learn what he really thinks. Each one is an argument with himself, and each one moves.

| The soliloquy begins | Where | What it reveals |
|---|---|---|
| “O that this too too solid flesh would melt” | Act 1, Scene 2 | grief and disgust at his mother’s hasty marriage; he wishes he could die, but must stay silent |
| “O what a rogue and peasant slave am I!” | Act 2, Scene 2 | shame that an actor can weep over a story while he does nothing; then a plan to test the King with a play |
| “To be, or not to be, that is the question” | Act 3, Scene 1 | whether it is nobler to endure life’s troubles or to end them, and why fear of death makes us endure |
| “Now might I do it pat, now he is praying” | Act 3, Scene 3 | he could kill the King, but holds back so that Claudius will not go to heaven |
| “How all occasions do inform against me” | Act 4, Scene 4 | an army marching to war shames him into resolving, “My thoughts be bloody or be nothing worth” |

The most famous of them opens with a question that is bigger than revenge:

> To be, or not to be, that is the question:\
> Whether ’tis nobler in the mind to suffer\
> The slings and arrows of outrageous fortune,\
> Or to take arms against a sea of troubles,\
> And by opposing end them?
>
> — Hamlet, *Hamlet*, Act 3, Scene 1

Hamlet weighs life against death. Death seems like sleep, an easy escape, but then he turns: “To sleep, perchance to dream—ay, there’s the rub”. No one knows what dreams may come in death, “The undiscover’d country, from whose bourn / No traveller returns”, so we bear the troubles we know rather than fly to ones we do not. His conclusion reaches beyond himself: “Thus conscience does make cowards of us all”. Here **conscience** means thinking and awareness as much as a sense of right and wrong, so the speech also explains why a thinking man may find it hard to act.

:::tip Follow the turns
Mark every place where the thought changes direction: a question, a “but”, an exclamation. In the second soliloquy the turn comes at “About, my brain!”, where Hamlet stops cursing himself and starts to plan.
:::

:::example Tracing the argument of a soliloquy
Trace how Hamlet’s thought moves in his first soliloquy (Act 1, Scene 2), spoken just after the court leaves the stage.
@step The start: he wishes his “too too solid flesh would melt”, and that God had not forbidden “self-slaughter”. Grief has made him long for death.
@step The middle: the world seems “an unweeded garden”, and he turns to the cause, his mother’s marriage “within a month” to a man as far below his father as a satyr is below the sun god Hyperion.
@step The end: “It is not, nor it cannot come to good. / But break, my heart, for I must hold my tongue.”
@answer The soliloquy moves from a wish to die, to disgust with the whole world, to its cause, his mother’s hasty remarriage, and it ends in enforced silence. It reveals the grief and anger that Hamlet hides at court, and it does so before he has even met the Ghost.

:::summary
- *Hamlet* is mostly in blank verse; Hamlet often slips into prose and riddles when he plays the madman, and he loves puns, as in “A little more than kin, and less than kind”.
- To paraphrase, read to the punctuation, put every idea into plain words, and keep the tone. Watch for words whose meaning has changed, such as coil, rub and quietus.
- A soliloquy is a speech spoken alone, so it shows a character’s private thoughts. Hamlet’s soliloquies move from grief, to self-reproach, to doubt, to resolve.
- Trace a soliloquy by finding its turns, the places where the argument changes direction.
:::
