Frederick Douglass, introducing the day he fought back against Edward Covey, a farmer notorious for breaking the spirit of enslaved people, wrote a sentence that turns on itself:

> You have seen how a man was made a slave; you shall see how a slave was made a man.
>
> — Frederick Douglass, *Narrative of the Life of Frederick Douglass* (1845)

“Man … slave” comes back as “slave … man”. The reversal is the whole story of his life in a dozen words, and it is hard to forget once you have read it. Patterns like this are **rhetorical figures**: ways of arranging or bending words that writers have used for more than two thousand years. In this lesson you will recognize nine of them, chiasmus, hyperbaton, zeugma, litotes, synecdoche, metonymy, apostrophe, paradox and oxymoron, and explain the effect each one has.

## Figures of order

:::definition Chiasmus and hyperbaton
**Chiasmus** is two phrases in which the key words of the first come back in reverse order in the second: *man … slave; slave … man*.

**Hyperbaton** is words placed out of their usual order for emphasis.
:::

Chiasmus makes an idea turn back on itself, so it feels complete and memorable. In the Gospel of Mark, the reversal shows that a rule exists to serve people:

> The sabbath was made for man, and not man for the sabbath
>
> — Mark 2:27, King James Bible (1611)

Hyperbaton throws weight on the words it moves and gives a line a lofty sound. Peter, speaking to a man begging at the temple gate, puts the riches first: *Silver and gold have I none*. The usual order, “I have no silver and gold”, would make their absence far less striking.

:::note Hyperbaton and inversion
In Lesson 7.1 you met inversion, a verb placed before its subject. Hyperbaton is the wider term for any words moved out of their usual order, so inversion is one kind of hyperbaton.
:::

## Figures of meaning: zeugma and litotes

:::definition Zeugma and litotes
**Zeugma** is one word, usually a verb, governing two others in two different senses at once: *Leila lost her keys and her temper.*

**Litotes** is understatement that affirms something by denying its opposite: *The exam was not easy* (it was hard).
:::

Zeugma surprises the reader and often deflates the grander of its two senses, so it is a favourite of comic and satirical writers.

:::example Explaining a zeugma
Explain the figure in Dickens’s description of a guest leaving a card party: she “went straight home, in a flood of tears and a sedan-chair.”
@step Find the word doing two jobs: “in” governs both “a flood of tears” and “a sedan-chair”.
@step The senses differ: she is in tears (an emotional state) and in a sedan-chair (a vehicle). That is zeugma.
@step Ask what the yoking does: setting real distress beside a mode of transport makes her grief absurd.
@answer The zeugma joins her tears and her sedan-chair with one “in”, so her distress becomes comic and the reader laughs at the drama of a card game.
:::

Litotes makes a point quietly, with modesty, irony or restrained force. When Paul calls his home town “no mean city”, he means that it is a great one, and the understatement sounds both proud and modest.

## Figures of substitution: synecdoche and metonymy

:::definition Synecdoche and metonymy
**Synecdoche** uses a part to stand for the whole, or the whole for a part: *all hands on deck* (sailors), *our daily bread* (all food).

**Metonymy** names a thing by something closely associated with it: *the crown* (the monarch), *the pen is mightier than the sword* (writing and war).
:::

Synecdoche fixes attention on the part that matters most: what a captain needs from a sailor is a pair of working hands. Metonymy turns a large or abstract idea into something concrete that the reader can picture.

:::mistake Part or association?
Ask whether the word names a **piece** of the thing. Hands are part of a sailor, so *hands* is synecdoche. A crown is not part of a monarch, only worn by one, so *the crown* is metonymy.
:::

## Address and contradiction

:::definition Apostrophe, paradox and oxymoron
**Apostrophe** is a direct address to someone absent or dead, or to a thing or an idea.

A **paradox** is a statement that seems to contradict itself yet expresses a truth.

An **oxymoron** is two contradictory words placed side by side.
:::

Apostrophe makes a speaker’s feeling urgent and intense by speaking straight to something that cannot answer. John Donne ends a sonnet by addressing death directly, and the line is also a paradox:

> And death shall be no more; death, thou shalt die.
>
> — John Donne, “Death, be not proud” (1633)

How can death die? The apparent contradiction makes the reader pause and work out the truth inside it: if the dead rise to eternal life, death itself has no more power. Hamlet uses a paradox in the same way when he tells his mother, “I must be cruel, only to be kind.”

An oxymoron presses the contradiction into two words, capturing a mixed or conflicted feeling:

> O heavy lightness! serious vanity!
>
> — William Shakespeare, *Romeo and Juliet* (1597)

:::tip Paradox or oxymoron?
An oxymoron is a pair of words (*sweet sorrow*); a paradox is a whole statement (*I must be cruel, only to be kind*). Ask how much of the passage carries the contradiction.
:::

:::summary
- Chiasmus reverses key words; hyperbaton moves words out of their usual order.
- Zeugma yokes two senses to one word; litotes affirms by denying the opposite.
- Synecdoche uses a part for the whole; metonymy uses something closely associated.
- Apostrophe speaks to the absent; a paradox is a statement that seems to contradict itself yet is true; an oxymoron joins two contradictory words.
- Always say what the figure does: makes an idea memorable, deflates, understates, makes it concrete, intensifies or makes the reader think.
:::
