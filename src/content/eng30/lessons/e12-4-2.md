In 1859 the philosopher John Stuart Mill made one of the boldest claims in the history of free speech:

> If all mankind minus one, were of one opinion, and only one person were of the contrary opinion, mankind would be no more justified in silencing that one person, than he, if he had the power, would be justified in silencing mankind.
>
> — John Stuart Mill, *On Liberty* (1859)

A claim this strong needs strong support, and *On Liberty* supplies it: evidence, principles, reasons for trusting those principles, and even the cases where the claim stops applying. A century later the philosopher Stephen Toulmin noticed that real arguments, in courtrooms, newspapers and essays like Mill’s, are built from the same six kinds of parts. In this lesson you will learn **Toulmin’s model** and use it to take an argument apart, find its hidden assumptions and judge how strong it is.

## The six parts of an argument

:::definition Toulmin’s model
- The **claim** is the position the arguer wants you to accept.
- The **data**, or **grounds**, are the facts and evidence offered in support of the claim.
- The **warrant** is the general principle that links the data to the claim: the reason the evidence counts.
- The **backing** is support for the warrant: research, law, experience or authority showing that the principle can be trusted.
- The **qualifier** is a word or phrase that limits how strongly the claim is made, such as *probably*, *in most cases* or *almost certainly*.
- The **rebuttal** names the conditions under which the claim would not hold: the exceptions.
:::

Here is an argument built from all six parts:

| Part | The argument |
|---|---|
| Data | Since the library began opening on Sunday afternoons last spring, it has had more visitors on Sundays than on any other day. |
| Warrant | A public service should be open when the public most wants to use it. |
| Backing | The city’s charter states that the library exists “to serve every resident”, and its surveys show that Sunday is when working families are free. |
| Qualifier | probably |
| Claim | The library should **probably** keep its Sunday hours. |
| Rebuttal | unless paying Sunday staff would force the library to cut its weekday hours. |

Read it as one chain: *the data, so (qualifier) the claim, since the warrant, because of the backing, unless the rebuttal.* The data answers “What have you got to go on?” and the warrant answers “How does that get you to your claim?”

## The warrant: the hidden link

Arguers often state their claim and their data and leave the warrant unsaid, because it seems obvious to them. But the warrant is where an argument is most often weak, so it is worth finding.

:::tip Finding an unstated warrant
Put the data and the claim side by side and ask: *What general principle would someone have to believe to move from this evidence to that conclusion?* The warrant is general (it would apply to other cases too); it does not just repeat the claim or add another fact.
:::

Mill states his warrant outright, in a single sentence:

> All silencing of discussion is an assumption of infallibility.
>
> — John Stuart Mill, *On Liberty* (1859)

To be **infallible** is to be incapable of being wrong. Mill’s data come from history: people as wise as Socrates were condemned for opinions that later ages honoured. His warrant explains why such cases matter: whoever silences an opinion acts as if they could not be mistaken, and no person or government is infallible. So no one has the right to silence an opinion.

:::example Finding the unstated warrant
An argument in the school newspaper says: “The vending machines in the cafeteria sell almost nothing but sugary drinks. The school should replace them with water fountains.” What is the unstated warrant?
@step Identify the data: the machines sell mostly sugary drinks.
@step Identify the claim: the school should replace them with water fountains.
@step Ask what principle links them. Merely selling sugary drinks is only a problem if the school has some duty about what its students drink.
@step Check that the principle is general and does not just repeat the claim or add a new fact.
@answer **A school should not make unhealthy choices the easiest ones for its students.** Anyone who accepts this principle will move from the data to the claim; anyone who rejects it will not be persuaded, however many sugary drinks are sold.
:::

## Backing, qualifiers and rebuttals

**Backing** supports the warrant, not the claim. If a reader asks, “Why should I accept your principle?”, the backing answers. For the library, the city charter and the surveys show why the library ought to serve working families.

:::mistake Data or backing?
Both are evidence, so they are easy to confuse. Ask what each piece of evidence supports. **Data** supports the *claim* directly (Sunday visitor numbers show that Sunday hours are wanted). **Backing** supports the *warrant* (the charter shows that the library’s job is to serve everyone).
:::

A **qualifier** makes a claim defensible. “Phones should always be banned in every class” can be destroyed by one good exception; “in most lessons, phones should stay in lockers” cannot. Words such as *always*, *never*, *all* and *every* are a sign that a claim may be stronger than its evidence.

A **rebuttal** shows that the arguer has thought about the exceptions. Mill, who defends free opinion more strongly than almost anyone, still names one:

> An opinion that corn-dealers are starvers of the poor, or that private property is robbery, ought to be unmolested when simply circulated through the press, but may justly incur punishment when delivered orally to an excited mob assembled before the house of a corn-dealer …
>
> — John Stuart Mill, *On Liberty* (1859)

A **corn-dealer** was a grain merchant. Printed in a newspaper, the opinion is free; shouted to an angry crowd outside the merchant’s house, it becomes a call to violence. Naming the exception does not weaken Mill’s argument. It makes the argument harder to attack, because he has already answered the most obvious objection.

:::tip Testing an argument with the model
Once you have laid an argument out, test each part in turn:
- Are the **data** accurate, relevant and enough?
- Would a fair-minded reader accept the **warrant**, and is there **backing** for it?
- Is the claim **qualified** as much as the evidence requires?
- Has the arguer faced the most obvious **rebuttal**, or ignored it?

An argument is only as strong as its weakest part, and the weakest part is often the one that was never written down.
:::

:::summary
- Toulmin’s model has six parts: the **claim**, the **data** (grounds) that support it, the **warrant** that links them, the **backing** that supports the warrant, the **qualifier** that limits the claim, and the **rebuttal** that names its exceptions.
- The warrant is often unstated. Find it by asking what general principle would carry someone from the data to the claim.
- Data support the claim; backing supports the warrant.
- A well-qualified claim with its exceptions named is harder to attack than an absolute one.
:::
