Every number in the advert was true, and the picture was still a lie. Arguments today lean on statistics: percentages, averages, graphs and studies. In Grade 11 you learned that correlation is not causation and that evidence must be relevant, sufficient and representative. In this lesson you will learn to spot the most common ways that true figures mislead, and to reason carefully about what causes what.

## How true figures mislead

:::definition Five ways figures mislead
- A **truncated axis** starts a graph’s vertical axis above zero, so a small difference looks dramatic.
- A **cherry-picked window** shows only the stretch of time that suits the claim and hides the longer trend.
- A **missing base** reports a relative change (“doubled”, “up 300%”) without the small numbers behind it.
- A **misleading average** gives a single mean that hides how spread out the figures are, or how a few extreme values drag it up or down.
- A **biased sample** is too small, or too narrow, to speak for the whole population the claim is about.
:::

Each has a fix. Start the axis at zero; show the whole period; give the actual numbers before and after; report the **median** (the middle value) and the range as well as the mean; and survey a large group chosen at random from the whole population.

A small firm that advertises a high “average salary” may be hiding a pattern like this one:

| Staff member | Salary (thousands) |
|---|---|
| Owner | 450 |
| Manager | 50 |
| Four assistants | 25 each |

The mean is 100 thousand, but five of the six people earn 50 thousand or less. The median, 25 thousand, describes a typical job far better.

## Relative and absolute change

A headline that says a medicine “raises the risk of headaches by 50%” sounds alarming. But a **relative change** compares the new figure with the old one, while an **absolute change** is the actual difference between them. If 2 people in every 1,000 have headaches without the medicine and 3 in every 1,000 have them with it, the relative rise is 50%, but the absolute rise is 1 person in 1,000.

:::example Turning a relative change into an absolute one
An advert says a new bicycle helmet makes serious head injuries in a crash “60% less likely”. In the trial, 5 in every 100 riders in crashes with an ordinary helmet were seriously injured, against 2 in every 100 with the new one. What does the change mean in absolute terms?
@step Write down the two rates: 5 in 100 before, and 2 in 100 after.
@step Subtract to find the absolute change: 5 − 2 = 3 fewer serious injuries in every 100 crashes.
@step Check the relative change: 3 is 60% of the original 5, so the advert’s figure is correct.
@answer The new helmet means **3 fewer serious injuries in every 100 crashes**. That is a real benefit, but the absolute figure lets a reader weigh it fairly.
:::

:::tip Ask “out of how many?”
Whenever you see a percentage change, ask for the numbers behind it. A 200% rise in burglaries may mean that a village went from one burglary to three.
:::

## Reasoning about causes

When two things go together, it is tempting to say that one causes the other. Before you accept such a claim, check for four common errors.

:::definition Four errors in causal reasoning
- **Reverse causation**: the arrow of cause may run the other way. Families with more fire extinguishers may have had more house fires because a fire led them to buy extinguishers, not the reverse.
- **A confounding factor**: a third thing may cause both. Towns with more dentists have more traffic accidents, because bigger towns have more dentists *and* more traffic.
- **Survivorship bias**: the evidence includes only the cases that survived or succeeded. The restaurants that have lasted fifty years all serve excellent food, but the poor ones closed long ago and are no longer counted.
- **Base-rate neglect**: a striking figure means little until you know how common the thing is overall. Most people injured in car crashes were wearing seat belts, simply because most people wear them.
:::

During the Second World War, analysts studied the bullet holes in bombers that returned from missions, which were mostly in the wings. The statistician Abraham Wald pointed out that the planes hit in other places, such as the engines, were the ones that never came back. The armour belonged where the returning planes had *no* holes. That is survivorship bias, and seeing past it saved lives.

:::mistake Calling every link a confounder
Before you name a third factor, ask whether the cause could simply run backwards. Patients who take the most pain tablets report the most pain *because* pain is what makes them take the tablets; no third factor is needed to explain the link.
:::

:::example Diagnosing a causal claim
> Students at schools with swimming pools get higher exam marks, so building a pool at every school would raise marks.

What is wrong with this reasoning?
@step Could the cause run backwards? High marks do not build swimming pools, so reverse causation is unlikely.
@step Could a third factor cause both? Schools with more money can build pools *and* pay for smaller classes, more books and more support.
@step Are failures missing, or is a base rate ignored? No: the comparison covers all schools.
@answer A **confounding factor**: the schools’ wealth probably explains both the pools and the higher marks, so building a pool would not by itself raise anyone’s marks.
:::

The strongest evidence of a cause comes from a **randomized controlled trial**. Chance, not choice, decides who gets the treatment, so the two groups differ only in the treatment, and a difference in their results can be traced to it. A study in which people *chose* the treatment, or one that compares only before and after with no other group, cannot rule out the other explanations.

:::summary
- True figures can mislead through a **truncated axis**, a **cherry-picked window**, a **missing base**, a **misleading average** or a **biased sample**. Each has a fix.
- A **relative change** (“50% more”) can hide a tiny **absolute change** (one more in 1,000). Always ask, “Out of how many?”
- Before accepting that one thing causes another, check for **reverse causation**, a **confounding factor**, **survivorship bias** and **base-rate neglect**.
- A **randomized controlled trial**, in which chance decides who gets the treatment, gives the strongest evidence of a cause.
:::
