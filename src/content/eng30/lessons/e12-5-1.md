:::note Content note
This unit quotes *Southern Horrors* (1892), Ida B. Wells’s investigation of lynching in the American South. The passages quoted are about how newspapers reported those crimes; the lessons do not describe the violence itself.
:::

In 1892 Ida B. Wells was a young journalist and part-owner of *Free Speech*, a Black newspaper in Memphis, Tennessee. After a mob murdered three Black shopkeepers in the city, she began to investigate how such killings were reported, and she found that the city’s leading papers printed rumours as facts. When she wrote about it, a mob destroyed her newspaper’s office, and she was warned that she would be killed if she returned. From exile she published *Southern Horrors*, and in it she asked a question that media students still ask: who owns the press, and whose interests does it serve?

> The *Daily Commercial* and *Evening Scimitar* of Memphis, Tenn., are owned by leading business men of that city …
>
> — Ida B. Wells, *Southern Horrors* (1892)

News, entertainment and social media feel like windows onto the world, but every one of them is made by an industry that must pay its bills. In this lesson you will learn how **funding**, **ownership** and **platforms** shape what media make, and what audiences never get to see.

## Who pays?

Every outlet is paid for by someone, and content tends to drift towards whatever keeps the money coming.

| Funding | Who pays | How it tends to shape content |
|---|---|---|
| **Advertising** | businesses buying the audience’s attention | content is built to draw the largest audience, or the audience advertisers want most; stories that offend advertisers may be avoided |
| **Subscription** | readers or viewers who pay a fee | content is shaped to keep the paying audience, so it serves their interests and may avoid what upsets them |
| **Public funding** | taxpayers or a licence fee, with a legal mission | content must serve the whole country, including regions, languages and groups that are not profitable |

Free is never free. If a news site charges you nothing, its income comes from advertisers, and what it sells them is you: your attention, your clicks and your data. That is why so many free sites are full of dramatic headlines that are designed to be clicked.

:::definition Target audience
The **target audience** of a media text is the group it is made for. A **mass audience** is as large and varied as possible; a **niche audience** is a smaller group with a shared interest, such as cyclists, investors or gamers.
:::

Advertisers pay more to reach some audiences than others, usually the ones with the most money to spend. So commercial media are often made for the audiences that are worth the most to advertisers, not for the ones that most need the information. A business section full of investment advice and a weekly page on the needs of low-paid workers may cost the same to produce, but only one of them attracts advertisers who pay well. Public funding exists partly to fill those gaps.

## Who owns?

:::definition Ownership and concentration
**Ownership** is who controls a media outlet. When owners have other businesses or political interests, those interests can shape what the outlet covers and how. **Concentration** of ownership means that a few companies or people own many outlets, so fewer voices decide what the public hears.
:::

Concentration matters most where a town has only one newspaper or one local station: if that outlet will not cover a story, the public may never hear it.

Influence of this kind is rarely an order from the owner. More often it works quietly: editors avoid stories that would embarrass the owner, reporters learn which topics win praise, and some subjects are simply never assigned. Wells noticed that the Memphis papers that spread false reports were owned by the city’s leading businessmen, and that the Black press, which was willing to investigate, was too poor to do so:

> The Afro-American papers are the only ones which will print the truth, and they lack means to employ agents and detectives to get at the facts. The race must rally a mighty host to the support of their journals, and thus enable them to do much in the way of investigation.
>
> — Ida B. Wells, *Southern Horrors* (1892)

Her answer was about money: if readers supported their own papers, those papers could afford to find the facts.

## Platforms and gatekeepers

:::definition Gatekeeping and platforms
A **gatekeeper** decides which stories pass through to the audience and which are left out. For most of the twentieth century the gatekeepers were editors and producers. Today they include **platforms**: the apps and websites whose design and ranking rules (their **algorithms**) decide what each user sees first, or at all.
:::

A platform shapes content through its format (one image, a thirty-second clip, a short post) and through what its ranking rules reward. If the rules reward watch time, creators learn to hook viewers in the first second. If they reward shares, content that provokes anger or delight spreads fastest.

Platforms also decide what is *not* seen. A story that the ranking rules push far down the feed may reach almost no one, however true and important it is, so a platform can act as a gatekeeper without deleting a single post. Wells understood why gatekeeping matters so much:

> The people must know before they can act, and there is no educator to compare with the press.
>
> — Ida B. Wells, *Southern Horrors* (1892)

Whoever controls what the public knows controls what the public is able to do.

:::example Explaining how an industry factor shapes content
A paid newsletter about farming, funded entirely by its readers’ yearly fees, loses dozens of subscribers every time it criticizes large farm businesses, many of whose owners subscribe. Over a year its articles grow more cautious. Explain what is happening.
@step Ask who pays: the readers, through subscriptions. There are no adverts and no owner with other businesses.
@step Ask what keeps the money coming: keeping those readers happy, and many of them own large farms.
@step Connect the money to the content: criticism costs subscribers, so the newsletter learns to avoid it.
@answer **Subscription** funding is shaping the content: to keep its paying readers, the newsletter softens stories that would upset them, so its other readers lose the criticism they once relied on.
:::

:::tip Follow the money
For any media text, ask four questions: Who paid for this? Who owns the outlet? Where does it appear, and what do that platform’s rules reward? Who is the audience, and who is left out?
:::

:::mistake Seeing a conspiracy everywhere
Most influence is structural, not a secret plot. A newsroom with less money does fewer investigations; a site paid by clicks drifts towards drama. Explain the pattern by the pressure that produces it, not by imagining that someone ordered it.
:::

:::summary
- Media content is shaped by who **pays** (advertising, subscription or public funding), who **owns** the outlet, and the **platform** where it appears.
- Advertising rewards large audiences, subscription rewards the paying audience, and public funding comes with a mission to serve everyone.
- **Gatekeepers**, once editors and now also platforms and their algorithms, decide what passes through to the public.
- Ida B. Wells showed how ownership and money shaped the press of her day, and argued that a well-funded independent press was the public’s best educator.
:::
