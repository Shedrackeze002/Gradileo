Wilfred Owen was killed in France on 4 November 1918, a week before the war ended. Among his papers his friends found a draft preface for a book of poems he never lived to publish. It says what the poems are for:

> The subject of it is War, and the pity of War.\
> The Poetry is in the pity.
>
> — Wilfred Owen, preface to *Poems* (1920)

Owen did not want his poems to celebrate battles or to console. He wanted them to make readers see what war does to people. In this lesson you will compare how poets of the American Civil War and the First World War **bear witness**, and how they use situation, form, imagery and irony to do it.

## Where the witness stands

:::definition Poetry of witness
A poem of **witness** records suffering that the poet has seen or lived through, so that it cannot be forgotten or explained away. The first question to ask of it is *where* it watches from, and what that place lets us see.
:::

War poems watch from very different places:

| Where the poem stands | Poem | What we are shown |
|---|---|---|
| The front line | Isaac Rosenberg, “Break of Day in the Trenches” | a rat crossing between the armies at dawn |
| The battlefield at night | Walt Whitman, “Vigil Strange I Kept on the Field One Night” | a soldier sitting by a dead young comrade |
| The grave | Thomas Hardy, “Channel Firing” | the dead woken by guns practising at sea |
| The railway station | Wilfred Owen, “The Send-off” | soldiers leaving quietly for the front |
| Home | Edward Thomas, “The Owl”; Amy Lowell, “Patterns” | comfort spoiled by thoughts of soldiers; a fiancée’s hidden grief |

A poem written far from the fighting can bear witness as powerfully as one written in a trench. Lowell’s speaker never sees Flanders; we learn of the war through a letter, a stiff gown and a garden.

:::example What the send-off leads to
Owen’s “The Send-off” describes soldiers marching to a country station and leaving “So secretly, like wrongs hushed-up”. Work out what its last stanza suggests.

> Shall they return to beatings of great bells\
> In wild trainloads?\
> A few, a few, too few for drums and yells,\
> May creep back, silent, to still village wells\
> Up half-known roads.
>
> — Wilfred Owen, “The Send-off” (1920)

@step Read the question: will the men come home to bells and cheering crowds, “In wild trainloads”?
@step Read the answer. Only “A few, a few, too few” will come back, and they will “creep back, silent”, not march home in triumph.
@step Connect it to the start. The men left secretly, with flowers that made them look like the dead; they return unnoticed, too few to celebrate.
@answer Most of the men will die. The few who survive will come home quietly, changed and uncelebrated, so the send-off’s flowers were really funeral flowers.
:::

## Irony and the elegy

:::definition Elegy
An **elegy** is a poem of mourning for the dead. Traditional elegies end in consolation; many war elegies refuse it, or end in questions instead.
:::

War poets often reach for **irony**, the gap between what is expected and what is. In Hardy’s “Channel Firing”, written in April 1914, the dead hear naval guns and sit up in their coffins, thinking it is the Day of Judgment. God tells them it is only gunnery practice, and that the world is as mad for war as ever. The joke is grim: the living are preparing a slaughter, and the dead are the ones who are calm.

:::tip Ask what the poem refuses
Irony in war poetry often works by refusing the expected comfort: the sun that cannot wake a dead man, the cheering homecoming that never happens, the poppy that survives while the soldiers do not. Ask what a reader of the time might have expected, then ask what the poem gives instead.
:::

## Form as witness: pararhyme and the long line

:::definition Pararhyme
**Pararhyme** pairs words whose consonants match but whose vowels differ, such as *years* and *yours*. Owen used it so that the ends of his lines echo but never quite chime.
:::

> “None,” said the other, “Save the undone years,\
> The hopelessness. Whatever hope is yours,\
> Was my life also; I went hunting wild\
> After the wildest beauty in the world,
>
> — Wilfred Owen, “Strange Meeting” (1920)

*Years* and *yours*, *wild* and *world*: each pair almost rhymes and then slides away. In a poem about two dead soldiers who almost recognize each other, the sound itself feels unresolved.

Whitman works at the other extreme. His long, unrhymed lines unroll slowly, so that in “Vigil Strange” the reader sits through the night with the speaker, line by line. The form is part of the witness: Owen’s clashing echoes unsettle us, while Whitman’s long lines make us wait and watch.

## Comparing two war poems

When two poems share a subject, the comparison lies in their differences: where each speaker stands, what each one sees, and how each one feels about it.

:::example A bird and a rat
Edward Thomas wrote “The Owl” in 1915, at home in England; Isaac Rosenberg wrote in the trenches. Compare how each poem uses a creature to bring the war home to its speaker.

> And salted was my food, and my repose,\
> Salted and sobered, too, by the bird’s voice\
> Speaking for all who lay under the stars,\
> Soldiers and poor, unable to rejoice.
>
> — Edward Thomas, “The Owl” (1917)

> It seems you inwardly grin as you pass\
> Strong eyes, fine limbs, haughty athletes\
> Less chanced than you for life,
>
> — Isaac Rosenberg, “Break of Day in the Trenches” (1916)

@step Compare the situations. Thomas is warm and fed at an inn; Rosenberg is in a trench at dawn.
@step Compare the creatures. An owl’s cry reaches Thomas from outside; a rat crosses Rosenberg’s hand and seems to grin at the soldiers.
@step Compare what each creature shows. The owl speaks “for all who lay under the stars”, so Thomas’s comfort is “Salted and sobered”; the rat has a better chance of living than the “haughty athletes” around it.
@step Compare the tones: Thomas is sober and guilty about his own safety, Rosenberg dry and ironic.
@answer Both poets use a creature to make the speaker see the soldiers’ fate. Thomas, safe at home, feels his comfort spoiled by the thought of men out in the cold; Rosenberg, at the front, wryly notes that a rat is safer than the young men dying around him.
:::

:::mistake Comparing poem by poem
Writing everything about one poem and then everything about the other gives you two short essays, not a comparison. Take one point at a time (where each poem watches from, its central image, its form, its tone) and say how the two poems are alike and how they differ on that point.
:::

:::summary
- A poem of witness records suffering so that it cannot be forgotten. Ask where it watches from: the front, the battlefield, the grave, the station or home.
- Many war elegies refuse consolation. Look for irony: the gap between what a reader expects and what the poem gives.
- Form is part of the witness: Owen’s pararhyme echoes without resolving, and Whitman’s long lines slow the reader down.
- To compare two war poems, go point by point: situation, imagery, form and tone.
:::
