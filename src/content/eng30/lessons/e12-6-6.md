A final examination essay draws on everything in this unit at once: reading a prompt closely, choosing a text and an approach, writing a controlling idea, then planning, writing and checking under time. The practice questions in this lesson imagine an examination with two writing tasks, a personal response and a critical essay. Your own examination may be set out differently, so always read its instructions first.

## Reading the prompt

Critical essay prompts are often written in one pattern: *Discuss the idea, developed in a literary text you have studied, about …* followed by a subject. Each part of that sentence is an instruction.

| Words in the prompt | What they ask of you |
|---|---|
| *Discuss the idea* | Make an argument about a theme, not a summary of events. |
| *developed in a literary text* | Show *how* the text builds that idea, through its choices, with evidence. |
| *you have studied* | Choose a text you know well enough to quote and discuss in detail. |
| *about …* (the subject) | Stay on this subject; it is what every paragraph must answer. |

:::example Unpacking a prompt
Unpack this prompt: *Discuss the idea, developed in a literary text you have studied, about the cost of ambition.*
@step Mark the key terms. The subject is *the cost of ambition*: not ambition in general, but what it costs.
@step Turn the prompt into a question your essay will answer: *What does this text suggest ambition costs a person, and how does it develop that idea?*
@step Note what the prompt does not ask for: a retelling, your own view of ambition apart from a text, or a list of techniques.
@answer *Argue, with evidence from one text, what that text suggests about what ambition costs, and show how the text develops that idea.*
:::

:::check e12-exam-prompt 1
:::

:::mistake Answering the topic instead of the prompt
A prompt about *the cost of keeping a secret* is not a prompt about secrets in general. An essay that explains why characters keep secrets, without saying what the secret costs, has drifted to the topic and left the prompt behind. Reread the subject phrase and check that your controlling idea answers it.
:::

## Choosing the text and the controlling idea

Choose the text whose central idea fits the subject most closely, and that you know well enough to quote from memory. A text you love but that only touches the subject will push you into retelling. Then write a **controlling idea**: your thesis for the examination essay, an arguable claim that answers the prompt in your own words.

| Controlling idea for *the cost of ambition* | Verdict |
|---|---|
| In *Hamlet*, Shakespeare develops an interesting idea about the cost of ambition. | Only repeats the prompt. |
| In *Hamlet*, Shakespeare shows a Danish court full of spies, flatterers and watchful guards. | Turns to setting instead of the subject. |
| In *Hamlet*, Claudius murders his brother, marries the queen and is killed by Hamlet at the end. | Summarizes the plot. |
| In *Hamlet*, Shakespeare suggests that ambition costs most after it succeeds: Claudius cannot truly repent, because he will not give up the crown and the queen he murdered for. | Answers the prompt with an arguable claim. |

:::check e12-exam-prompt 2
:::

A plan written in five minutes saves twenty minutes of wandering. Here is a quick plan for the ambition prompt:

- **Controlling idea:** In *Hamlet*, ambition costs most after it succeeds.
- **Paragraph 1:** Claudius at court in Act 1: the crown and the queen are his, and he seems secure.
- **Paragraph 2:** The prayer scene: he cannot repent while he keeps “My crown, mine own ambition, and my queen”.
- **Paragraph 3:** The final scene: the poisoned cup he prepared for Hamlet kills the queen he murdered to win.
- **Counter-reading:** Claudius is a capable king, so is ambition really his ruin? His skill only delays the cost.

:::tip Keep the prompt in sight
After each body paragraph, reread your controlling idea and the prompt’s subject phrase. If the paragraph you just wrote does not connect to both, add a sentence that makes the link before you move on.
:::

## Managing the time

Divide the time in proportion to the marks, and keep a few minutes at the start for reading and planning and at the end for checking.

:::example Budgeting the time
An examination gives **140 minutes** for two tasks: a personal response worth 25% of the marks and a critical essay worth 75%. Make a time plan.
@step Set aside time to read and plan at the start and to check at the end: about 10 minutes each, which leaves 120 minutes for writing.
@step Divide the writing time by the marks: 25% of 120 is 30 minutes, and 75% of 120 is 90 minutes.
@step Write the plan in order, and note the times on your paper so you can check your pace.
@answer *About 10 minutes reading and planning, 30 minutes on the personal response, 90 minutes on the critical essay and 10 minutes checking both.*
:::

:::check e12-exam-strategy 1
:::

Plans go wrong under pressure. When they do, choose the action that leaves you with complete answers at the end. Finish a task with a short, clear ending rather than polishing it while the other waits; if your text fits the prompt less well than you hoped, reframe your controlling idea around the part that does fit, rather than starting again; and with ten minutes left, write a brief final point and a conclusion that answers the prompt rather than a long new paragraph you cannot finish.

:::check e12-exam-strategy 2
:::

For the critical essay itself, a reliable order is: read the prompt and mark its key terms; choose the text; jot down the moments and quotations that bear on the prompt; shape a controlling idea from what those moments show; plan the order of your paragraphs; write; and reread against the prompt. Gathering evidence before you fix your claim means the claim is one your evidence can support.

:::mistake Polishing the first task
Students often spend too long on the first task because it is in front of them. A polished personal response and an unfinished critical essay earn fewer marks than two complete answers. Watch the clock at the time you planned to switch, and switch.
:::

:::check e12-exam-strategy 3
:::

:::summary
- Read the prompt as a set of instructions: argue about the idea, show how the text develops it, and stay on the subject.
- Choose a text that fits the subject and that you know well, and write a controlling idea that answers the prompt arguably.
- Divide the time by the marks, with minutes kept for reading at the start and checking at the end.
- When a plan goes wrong, choose the action that leaves you with complete answers.
:::
