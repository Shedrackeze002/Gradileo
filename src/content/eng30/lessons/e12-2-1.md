In the spring of 1819 a nightingale built its nest near the house in Hampstead, outside London, where John Keats was living. One morning, a friend later remembered, Keats carried a chair out to the grass under a plum tree, sat there for two or three hours, and came back with a few scraps of paper. They held a poem that begins:

> My heart aches, and a drowsy numbness pains\
> My sense, as though of hemlock I had drunk,
>
> — John Keats, “Ode to a Nightingale” (1820)

Keats does not simply describe the bird. He speaks to it, and the poem becomes less about a nightingale than about what its song makes him feel and think. In this lesson you will read the **ode**, the grandest kind of lyric the Romantic poets wrote, and follow how a **meditative lyric** develops its thought.

:::note Romantic spelling
Poems of this period often drop a letter and mark the gap: *fam’d* for famed, *cover’d* for covered. *Thou*, *thee* and *thy* are the old singular forms of *you* and *your*.
:::

## The ode and its address

:::definition Ode
An **ode** is a long, serious lyric poem, usually in elaborate stanzas, that speaks to a person, thing or idea and praises it or reflects on it. The Romantic poets wrote odes to a bird, a painted vase, a season and the wind.
:::

:::definition Apostrophe
**Apostrophe** is speaking directly to someone absent or something not human, as if it could hear and answer. Most odes are built on it.
:::

To read an ode, first find the **addressee**: look for *thou*, *thee* and *thy*, or for *you*. Then ask two questions. What does the speaker feel about the thing addressed, and what does the speaker want from it?

:::example Whom does Keats speak to, and what does he want?
In the third stanza of “Ode to a Nightingale”, Keats has just wished he could drink and slip away with the bird. Work out whom he addresses and what he wants.

> Fade far away, dissolve, and quite forget\
> What thou among the leaves hast never known,\
> The weariness, the fever, and the fret\
> Here, where men sit and hear each other groan;
>
> — John Keats, “Ode to a Nightingale” (1820)

@step Find the addressee. “Thou among the leaves” is the nightingale, hidden in the trees; the speaker is not talking to a friend or to the reader.
@step Find the wish: “Fade far away, dissolve, and quite forget”. He wants to vanish with the bird.
@step Find what he wants to escape: “The weariness, the fever, and the fret”, a human world of pain that the bird “hast never known”.
@answer He speaks to the nightingale, and he wants to fade away with it, out of a world of sickness and sorrow that the bird has never known.
:::

## How an ode moves

An ode rarely stands still. It usually moves through stages: the speaker describes the thing addressed, longs for something from it, and then turns, often back to the speaker’s own life. Keats’s Grecian urn is painted with a young man about to kiss a girl, and the speaker finds a strange comfort in the fact that the kiss will never come:

> Bold Lover, never, never canst thou kiss,\
> Though winning near the goal—yet, do not grieve;\
> She cannot fade, though thou hast not thy bliss,\
> For ever wilt thou love, and she be fair!
>
> — John Keats, “Ode on a Grecian Urn” (1820)

The lover is frozen just short of the kiss, so he will never have his “bliss”; but because she is frozen too, she “cannot fade”. Here we can see the tension the whole ode turns on: art stops time, which is both a loss and a gift. The later stanzas weigh both sides, and the famous ending gives the urn itself the last word.

:::tip Map the stanzas
For any ode, write one line for each stanza: what is addressed, what is felt, what is wished. The **turn** is the stanza where the line changes direction, as when Shelley, after three sections on the wind’s power over leaves, clouds and waves, turns to himself: “If I were a dead leaf thou mightest bear”.
:::

An ode names many things. In “Ode to a Nightingale” Keats mentions Death, an emperor and a clown, but the *thou* of the poem is still the bird. Find who is spoken *to*, not just who is spoken *about*.

## The meditative lyric

:::definition Meditative lyric
A **meditative lyric** follows a mind as it thinks. Many Romantic poems share one shape: a speaker in a particular place describes the scene; the scene stirs a memory or a question; the poem turns inward; and it returns to the scene, which is now seen differently.
:::

Wordsworth’s “Lines Written a Few Miles above Tintern Abbey” opens with a return to a valley (“Five years have passed”), and Coleridge’s “Frost at Midnight” opens in a silent cottage beside a sleeping baby. Both drift into memory and come back with a hope for someone else. Wordsworth’s Immortality ode works differently: it asks a question, and then tries to answer it.

:::example How the Immortality ode answers its question
The fourth stanza ends with a question: “Whither is fled the visionary gleam? / Where is it now, the glory and the dream?” The fifth stanza begins:

> Our birth is but a sleep and a forgetting:\
> The Soul that rises with us, our life’s Star,\
> Hath had elsewhere its setting,\
> And cometh from afar:\
> Not in entire forgetfulness,\
> And not in utter nakedness,\
> But trailing clouds of glory do we come\
> From God, who is our home;\
> Heaven lies about us in our infancy!
>
> — William Wordsworth, “Ode: Intimations of Immortality” (1807)

@step Recall the question. The speaker has lost the “glory” he once saw in meadows and streams, and asks where it has gone.
@step Read the answer as an idea, not a fact: the soul comes “from afar”, from God, and a newborn child still trails “clouds of glory”.
@step Follow the logic. If heaven is close in infancy, it must grow distant as we grow up, so the glory was never in the meadows: it was in the child’s eyes, and it fades with age.
@answer Stanza 5 answers the question with a myth of where we come from: the glory came with us from God and fades as we grow older. The thought has moved from loss, to question, to explanation, and it will move on to consolation.
:::

:::tip Watch the tenses
Meditative lyrics move in time as well as in thought. Mark the **present** (the scene in front of the speaker), the **past** (memory) and the **future** (hope or prophecy). In “Frost at Midnight” the present is the cottage, the past is the speaker’s schooldays, and the future is the child’s life among “lakes and sandy shores”.
:::

:::mistake Retelling instead of tracing
“The poet sees a valley, then talks about his sister” is a summary. Tracing the thought means saying *why* each stage leads to the next: the valley brings back the past, the past shows him how much he has changed, and the change lets him see his own younger self in his sister.
:::

:::summary
- An ode is a long, serious lyric that speaks to someone or something; that address is called apostrophe.
- To read an ode, find the addressee, what the speaker feels and what the speaker wants, then map the stanzas and find the turn.
- A meditative lyric follows a mind in motion: a scene, a memory or question, a turn inward, and a return to the scene, seen anew.
- Trace the thought, not just the events: say how each stage leads to the next, and watch the shifts between present, past and future.
:::
