The school has cancelled a field trip. Here is the same news written four times:

- *trip’s cancelled?? seriously, i was so ready for it, now it’s just a regular boring tuesday*
- *I am writing to ask whether the field trip to the science centre, which was cancelled last week, could be rescheduled for the spring.*
- *Friends, we waited all term for this trip. Let us show the school that we still want it, and sign the petition today!*
- *The cancellation of the field trip reveals how easily learning beyond the classroom is sacrificed when budgets tighten.*

None of these is simply “good writing” or “bad writing”. Each one is right for one reader and wrong for the other three: the first is a text to a friend, the second an email to the principal, the third a speech at an assembly and the last a sentence from an essay. In this lesson you will match **register** and **tone** to an **audience** and a purpose, notice when a piece of writing slips out of its register, and revise casual sentences into academic prose.

## Register, tone and audience

:::definition Register and tone
**Register** is the level of formality and the kind of language a situation calls for: the vocabulary, the sentence structure and how directly the writer speaks to the reader.

**Tone** is the attitude the writing conveys: respectful, urgent, ironic, warm, angry or calm.
:::

Register depends on the **audience**, who will read or hear the words, and the **purpose**, what the words are meant to do. A close friend needs no explanation and expects no ceremony. A stranger in charge needs a clear, polite request. A crowd needs to be stirred. An examiner needs a precise, impersonal argument.

Register and tone are separate choices. Two sentences in the same formal register can carry very different tones: *The committee’s decision deserves careful consideration* is respectful, while *The committee’s decision deserves careful consideration, if only as a warning* is ironic. So when you describe a piece of writing, name both: how formal it is, and what attitude it takes.

## Four registers

| Register | Suits | Features |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Formal and academic | an analytical essay | impersonal, precise and formal, with no slang and no direct appeal to the reader |
| Polite and professional | an email to someone in charge whom you have never met | polite, clear and to the point, without slang or jokes |
| Rousing and public | a speech to the whole school | addressed to a crowd, speaking to the listeners as “we” and building energy |
| Casual and conversational | a text message to a close friend | relaxed and personal, with slang, contractions and loose punctuation |

Notice how the features show up in the field-trip sentences. The essay sentence never says “I” or “you”, and it turns the event into an idea about budgets. The email begins with its purpose, “I am writing to ask”, and stays courteous. The speech greets its listeners, uses “we” and ends with a call to act. The text has no capital letters, doubled question marks and the casual “seriously”.

:::example Naming a register
Name the register: *Would it be possible to extend the deadline for the history project by two days? I would be grateful for your reply.*
@step Look at the vocabulary: “would it be possible”, “extend” and “grateful” are polite and careful, with no slang.
@step Look at how the writer speaks to the reader: directly, as “you”, with a clear request, so this is not an impersonal essay.
@step Look at the purpose: to ask someone in charge for something, briefly and courteously.
@answer The register is polite and professional: it suits an email to a teacher or someone in charge.
:::

:::tip Hear it in the reader’s voice
Imagine the reader receiving the words. Would your principal be startled by “seriously”? Would a friend laugh at “I am writing to ask whether …”? A register is wrong when it would surprise the person it is meant for.
:::

## Spotting a slip

A formal essay can be undone by a single casual phrase. The rest of the sentence is careful, so the slip stands out all the more:

*By the third act, Macbeth’s ambition is **through the roof**, and he no longer hesitates before killing.*

“Through the roof” belongs in conversation. A formal version keeps the same claim: *By the third act, Macbeth’s ambition has become boundless, and he no longer hesitates before killing.*

:::mistake Common slips in essays
- Intensifiers from speech: *super*, *totally*, *way*, *so* (as in “so important”).
- Vague quantities: *a ton of*, *loads of*, *a lot of*.
- Hedges from conversation: *kind of*, *sort of*, *pretty much*.
- Idioms and slang: *a no-brainer*, *goes overboard*, *drives me nuts*.
:::

## Revising into academic prose

When you revise a casual sentence for an essay, change the register and **keep the meaning**.

:::example Revising for an analytical essay
Revise for an essay: *The narrator is super annoying and you just can’t trust anything he says.*
@step Find the casual features: the intensifier “super”, the direct address “you”, the filler “just” and the contraction “can’t”.
@step Find the meaning that must survive: the narrator irritates, and his account is unreliable.
@step Replace the casual words with precise, impersonal ones, and keep both claims.
@answer *The narrator is irritating, and his account cannot be trusted.*
:::

:::mistake Formal but different
A revision that sounds formal but changes the claim has failed. If the original says the research is “kind of mixed”, then *the research firmly confirms it* is formal but wrong; *the research is inconclusive* keeps the meaning. Turning the sentence into a personal reaction (“I was so surprised …”) fails too, because an essay argues about the text, not about the writer’s feelings.
:::

:::note Shifting register on purpose
Skilled writers sometimes shift register deliberately. A speaker may drop into a casual phrase to sound like one of the crowd, and a novelist may set a character’s slang against a formal narrator to show a gap between them. A shift works when it is chosen for an effect; it fails when the writer simply forgets the reader.
:::

:::summary
- Register is the level of formality a situation calls for; tone is the attitude the writing conveys.
- Choose the register from the audience and the purpose.
- Academic writing is impersonal and precise; professional writing is polite and to the point; a speech addresses a crowd as “we”; a message to a friend is relaxed.
- One casual phrase can break a formal register; find it and replace it.
- When you revise into academic prose, keep the meaning and leave out personal reactions.
:::
