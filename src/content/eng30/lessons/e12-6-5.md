Two essays can make the same argument with the same evidence and still earn different marks, because one is easier to follow, sharper and more confident than the other. That difference is **style**. In this lesson you will revise prose for precision and concision, keep one consistent academic voice and shape sentences so that the most important idea lands with force.

## Precision and concision

:::definition Nominalization
A **nominalization** is an action turned into a noun: *decide* becomes *make a decision*, *explain* becomes *provide an explanation*. Too many of them make prose slow and vague, because the real action hides inside a noun while a weak verb (*make*, *give*, *provide*, *conduct*) does the work.
:::

| Wordy | Revised |
|---|---|
| The poet makes use of repetition. | The poet repeats … |
| The narrator gives an indication of his fear. | The narrator shows his fear. |
| There was a refusal by Hamlet to act. | Hamlet refused to act. |
| due to the fact that | because |
| in order to | to |

Concision does not mean cutting until meaning is lost. *The narrator failed* is shorter than *the narrator failed to explain his behaviour*, but it no longer says the same thing. Revise to remove empty words, not ideas.

:::example Untangling a sentence
Revise: *There was an attempt on the part of the speaker to give an explanation of the reasons for his silence.*
@step Find the buried actions: *attempt* and *explanation* hide the verbs *try* and *explain*.
@step Find the real subject, the person doing the action: the speaker.
@step Rebuild the sentence around the subject and the verbs, keeping every part of the meaning and a formal voice.
@answer *The speaker tried to explain why he was silent.*
:::

:::

Hedges are words that soften a claim: *perhaps*, *may*, *seems*, *in some ways*. One honest hedge, where the evidence really is limited, is a mark of careful thinking. Three or four in a row only blur the claim. *It could perhaps be argued that the ending may be somewhat ironic* hedges four times; *the ending can be read as ironic* keeps the one qualifier the claim needs. Cutting hedges must not swing you into overstatement, though: *the ending is the most ironic ever written* is no more honest than the original.

:::check e12-style-revision 2
:::

:::note Active and passive
The active voice (*the narrator hides the letter*) is usually clearer and shorter than the passive (*the letter is hidden by the narrator*). Use the passive when the doer is unknown or unimportant, or when you want the receiver of the action at the end of the sentence for emphasis: *by morning, the letter had been burned*.
:::

## One consistent voice

An academic voice is formal but plain. It writes about literature in the present tense (*Hamlet hesitates*, not *Hamlet hesitated*), stays in the third person rather than shifting to *you* and chooses the exact word rather than the casual one or the impressive one.

| Slip | Example | Revision |
|---|---|---|
| Slang | *The ending is a total downer.* | *The ending is bleak.* |
| Shift into *you* | *You can tell she is scared of him.* | *Her fear of him shows in what she leaves unsaid.* |
| Inflated diction | *The poem evinces the transience of dominion.* | *The poem shows that power does not last.* |

:::mistake Mistaking long words for a formal voice
A thesaurus can make a sentence sound grand and say less. *Crepuscular* is the right word only if no plain word, such as *twilit*, does the job, and only if your reader will understand it. Precision, not decoration, is what makes academic writing sound authoritative.
:::

## Emphasis and rhythm

:::definition Stress position
The **stress position** is the end of a sentence, where readers naturally give the most weight. Strong writers build context first and save the key idea for the last words.
:::

Compare two versions of the same sentence. *The ship sank on its first voyage, although it had been called unsinkable* ends on an old claim. *It had been called unsinkable, and it sank on its first voyage* ends on the fact that matters.

:::check e12-sentence-craft 1
:::

**Rhythm** comes from varying sentence length. A run of sentences with the same length and shape lulls the reader; a long sentence that builds pressure, followed by a short one, makes the short one land. The short sentence is a strong tool, so spend it on the moment that matters, not on a minor detail.

:::example Building to a climax
Revise this paragraph so that it builds to its most important moment: *She trained for the marathon for a year. She ran in rain and in snow. She studied the course map every night. She fell at the twentieth kilometre.*
@step Find the climax: the fall. Everything before it is build-up.
@step Combine the build-up into one longer sentence that gathers pressure.
@step Give the climax a short sentence of its own, at the end.
@answer *For a year she trained for the marathon, running through rain and snow and studying the course map every night until she knew each hill by heart. At the twentieth kilometre, she fell.*
:::

:::check e12-sentence-craft 2
:::

:::tip Read it aloud
Your ear catches what your eye misses. Read a paragraph aloud: where you run out of breath, a sentence is too long; where you sound like a drum, the sentences are too alike; where the important word is lost in the middle, move it to the end.
:::

:::

:::summary
- Turn nominalizations back into verbs and cut empty phrases, without cutting meaning.
- Keep one honest hedge where the evidence is limited; cut stacked hedges, but do not overstate.
- Keep one academic voice: present tense for literature, no *you*, no slang and no inflated diction.
- Put the key idea in the stress position at the end of the sentence, and vary sentence length so that a short sentence lands on the climax.
:::
