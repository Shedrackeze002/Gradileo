A reflective essay tells a piece of your own experience and thinks about what it meant. It sounds like the easiest kind of writing, since you are the expert on your own life, yet it is one of the hardest to do well. Many reflections tell a story and stop; many more announce a lesson that could have come from anywhere. In this lesson you will learn to move from a specific moment to an insight that the moment has earned.

## From experience to insight

:::definition Reflective writing
**Reflective writing** moves between two things: a specific **experience**, told in precise detail, and an **insight** into what that experience meant. The insight is **earned** when it grows out of the detail, so that it could only have come from this experience.
:::

Reflection fails in three common ways.

| Weakness | What it looks like |
|---|---|
| **Narration only** | A vivid account of what happened, which never says what it meant. |
| **Vague generality** | Large truths about life, courage or family with no specific detail at all. |
| **Tacked-on moral** | A real story followed by a lesson that has nothing to do with its details: *this taught me never to give up*. |

Here is a model paragraph from a reflective essay about a part-time job. Notice where the detail is and where the thinking begins.

> Every Thursday an old man paid for his groceries in coins, counting them out of a cloth purse one at a time while the line behind him grew. For months I hurried him, sliding the coins into my palm before he had finished. One week he paused, smiled and said that counting was the only arithmetic he still did. I had thought I was saving him time. I had been taking away the one part of the errand that was still his.

The first three sentences are the experience, told in detail: the coins, the cloth purse, the growing line. The last two are the insight, and they are earned, because they come from a particular moment (his remark about arithmetic) and turn back on the writer’s own behaviour. Nothing in them could have been written by someone who was not there.

An insight does not have to be a lesson about how to live. Often it is a changed understanding:

- **of another person**: *she had not been strict with me; she had been frightened for me*;
- **of yourself**: *I was not shy; I was afraid of being wrong in public*;
- **of an idea**: *home was not a place but a set of habits I had carried with me*.

:::example Earning the insight
Revise this paragraph, whose lesson is tacked on: *My father taught me to drive in an empty parking lot. I kept braking too hard, and he kept telling me to relax. After three weeks I passed my test. This experience taught me that practice makes perfect.*
@step Find the detail with the most life in it. The braking too hard, and his repeated *relax*, are the heart of the paragraph.
@step Ask what that detail showed you that you did not know before. Perhaps the hard braking was about fear of disappointing him rather than fear of the car.
@step Write the insight so that it grows from the detail, and cut the moral that could have come from anywhere.
@answer *My father taught me to drive in an empty parking lot, and I kept braking so hard that our heads jerked forward. He kept telling me to relax. It took me two weeks to notice that I braked hardest whenever he went quiet: I was not afraid of the car but of his silence, and once I asked him to keep talking, the car began to glide.*
:::

The opposite problem is just as common: a paragraph full of honest detail that never stops to think. The fix is not to bolt on a lesson but to find the question already inside the detail.

:::example Adding reflection to narration
Revise this paragraph, which tells what happened but never reflects: *At my cousin’s wedding I gave a speech in a language I had not spoken since I was six. My aunt translated it for me, and I read every word from the page while my hands shook. People clapped politely, and the band started playing.*
@step Keep the detail, and look for the moment with a question in it: why read every word from the page?
@step Ask what you understand now that you did not understand then. Perhaps the page was a shield against being heard making mistakes.
@step Add the *now* voice, marked by a phrase such as *at the time* or *only later*, and let the insight come from the detail.
@answer *At my cousin’s wedding I gave a speech in a language I had not spoken since I was six, reading every word of my aunt’s translation from the page while my hands shook. At the time I was relieved that people clapped at all. Only later did I see that I had hidden behind the page for fear of being heard making mistakes, and that the guests had been applauding the effort, not the grammar.*
:::

## Specific detail

Reflection is only as convincing as its detail. A vague detail tells the reader what to feel; a specific one lets the reader see, and then believe the insight that follows.

| Vague | Specific |
|---|---|
| My grandmother was a kind person. | My grandmother kept a jar of mint sweets by the door for the mail carrier. |
| The game was very stressful. | With four seconds left, I could hear the scoreboard clock buzz. |
| I felt nervous before my speech. | I read my first line three times before I noticed I had already said it. |

## Voice and shape

A reflective essay has two narrators: the person you were *then*, inside the experience, and the person you are *now*, looking back. Good reflection lets the reader hear both. The *then* voice gives the experience its freshness (what you saw, assumed and felt at the time); the *now* voice gives it meaning (what you understand that you did not understand then). Signals such as *at the time*, *I had thought* and *only later* mark the move from one to the other. In this pair of sentences, the first belongs to *then* and the second to *now*:

> At the time, I thought the coach had benched me to punish me. Only later did I see that she had kept me off the court so that I would watch how the others moved without the ball.

The shape of the essay can follow the same movement. Many strong reflections begin inside a single moment, step back to give just enough context, return to the moment and end on the insight. The ending does not need to tie everything up. An honest reflection can end with a question you are still thinking about, so long as the essay has shown why the question matters.

:::mistake The lesson in the last line
*In the end, this experience taught me that family is the most important thing* could end almost any essay, which is the problem. If your final sentence could be pasted onto someone else’s story, it has not been earned. Replace it with what *this* experience showed you that no other could.
:::

:::tip Find the detail that surprised you
The best insight often hides in a detail you nearly left out: a remark, a gesture, a moment when you acted differently from what you expected. Ask of each detail, *Why do I still remember this?* The answer is often your insight.
:::

:::note You choose what to share
Reflective writing is personal, but it is not a confession. You decide which experiences to write about and how much of them to reveal. A small, ordinary moment, honestly examined, makes a stronger reflection than a dramatic one told without thought.
:::

:::summary
- Reflective writing joins a specific experience, told in precise detail, to an insight the detail has earned.
- Narration with no reflection, vague generalities and a moral tacked on at the end all fall short.
- Let the reader hear both the *then* voice and the *now* voice, and mark the move between them.
- Test the insight: if it could end anyone’s essay, it has not been earned by yours.
:::
