:::note Content note
This lesson describes, without images, the propaganda of the First World War and of the Nazi regime in Germany.
:::

During the First World War, posters in several countries showed the enemy as a giant ape or a monster carrying off women and children. People who had never met a soldier from the other side learned to see him as a beast. Two decades later, the Nazi regime in Germany blamed Jewish people for the country’s defeat and its poverty, repeating the accusation in newspapers, films, speeches and schoolbooks until millions accepted it. That propaganda helped prepare the way for the Holocaust.

Propaganda is not only history. The same techniques appear in election campaigns, advertising and the posts that crowd your phone, where a false claim can travel around the world in an hour. In this lesson you will learn to recognize the techniques of **propaganda** and to test a claim in digital media before you believe it or share it.

## The techniques of propaganda

:::definition Propaganda
**Propaganda** is communication designed to spread a belief or win support for a cause by working on people’s feelings rather than their reason, often by distorting the truth or leaving out what does not suit it.
:::

| Technique | What it does |
|---|---|
| **Demonization** | shows the enemy as monstrous, evil or less than human |
| **Glittering generalities** | uses noble, vague words (freedom, greatness, honour) that stir feeling but promise nothing definite |
| **The big lie** | repeats a falsehood so huge and so often that people come to believe it |
| **Appeal to fear** | warns of a terrible danger that only the speaker’s side can prevent |
| **Scapegoating** | blames a whole group of people for problems they did not cause |
| **Transfer** | borrows the respect people feel for a flag, a faith or a hero and attaches it to a cause |

The phrase *the big lie* dates from the 1920s, when Adolf Hitler accused his enemies of using the technique; historians now use it to describe the Nazis’ own propaganda. Scapegoating and demonization often work together: first a group is blamed, then it is shown as less than human, and what is done to it begins to seem acceptable.

:::tip Close cousins
**Glittering generalities** use words; **transfer** uses symbols. A slogan promising “a proud new dawn” is a glittering generality. A candidate who speaks only in front of the national flag, so that its glory seems to belong to her, is using transfer.
:::

## Resisting propaganda

Propaganda works on feeling, so the way to resist it is to ask the question it hopes you will skip. Against glittering generalities, ask what the fine words actually commit the speaker to doing. Against an appeal to fear, ask what evidence there is for the danger, and whether other solutions exist. Against scapegoating, look for the real causes of the problem and ask whether the blamed group controls them. Against transfer, separate your respect for the symbol from the argument being made. And against demonization, remember that the people in the picture are human, and look for what they have really done.

## Testing claims in digital media

:::definition Misinformation and disinformation
**Misinformation** is false or misleading content, whoever shares it and for whatever reason. **Disinformation** is false content created or spread *deliberately* to deceive. A **deepfake** is a video, image or voice recording made or altered with software so that someone appears to say or do something they never did.
:::

Most false content online falls into five patterns, and each has a check that reveals it:

| Problem | What it is | The check that reveals it |
|---|---|---|
| **Out of context** | a real photo or clip from another time or place | search for the image, or a still from the clip, to see where and when it first appeared |
| **Manipulated media** | an image, video or voice that has been edited or faked | find the original, and see whether experts or reporters have examined it |
| **Accurate but misleading** | true facts arranged to suggest something false | read the full article and the study behind it, not just the headline |
| **Impostor source** | an account or site pretending to be a trusted organization | check the handle or web address against the organization’s real site |
| **Satire taken as news** | a joke article shared as if it were real | check what kind of site published it, and whether it calls itself satire |

:::definition Lateral reading
**Lateral reading** means leaving a page to find out what other, independent sources say about it, instead of judging it only by how it looks. Professional fact-checkers open new tabs within seconds: Who runs this site? Have trusted news outlets reported the same thing?
:::

:::example Testing a viral post
> A post shows a photo of a dam with water pouring over a huge crack, captioned “Millbrook Dam failing NOW — evacuate!” It has been shared 40,000 times in two hours. The account that posted it is three days old.

How should a careful reader test this post before sharing it?
@step Stop. The post is designed to frighten, and urgency is a reason to slow down, not to share.
@step Investigate the source: an account three days old, with no history, is not a trusted organization.
@step Check the image: a reverse image search shows whether the photo appeared earlier, somewhere else.
@step Read laterally: if a dam were really failing, the town’s emergency services and local news would say so.
@answer Until the town’s official channels or reliable local news confirm it, the post should not be believed or shared. It shows signs of being an **out-of-context** or **manipulated** image posted by an unreliable source, and the right checks are a reverse image search and lateral reading.
:::

:::mistake “It looks professional, so it must be true”
A polished logo, a confident tone and thousands of shares tell you nothing about accuracy. Fakes are easy to make look official. Judge a claim by what independent sources say about it, not by how it looks.
:::

:::summary
- **Propaganda** works on feeling through **demonization**, **glittering generalities**, **the big lie**, **appeals to fear**, **scapegoating** and **transfer**. Resist it by asking the question it hopes you will skip.
- **Misinformation** is false or misleading content; **disinformation** is spread deliberately; a **deepfake** is faked audio or video.
- Name the problem (out of context, manipulated, accurate but misleading, impostor, or satire) and choose the check that tests it.
- **Read laterally**: find out what independent sources say before you believe or share.
:::
