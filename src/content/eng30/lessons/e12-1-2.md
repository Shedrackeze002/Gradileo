Mr. Ryder is the most respected member of a society of light-skinned Black Americans in a Northern city soon after the Civil War. Chesnutt’s narrator describes how the society came to be:

> By accident, combined perhaps with some natural affinity, the society consisted of individuals who were, generally speaking, more white than black.
>
> — Charles W. Chesnutt, “The Wife of His Youth” (1898)

To read that sentence well, a reader needs to know something about colour and class in the years after slavery, and needs to hear the irony in “by accident”. Stories are written in particular times and places, and their characters live inside societies with rules. In this lesson you will use **context** to deepen a reading without letting it replace the text, and analyse how characters are shaped by, and push against, their **society**.

## Using context well

:::definition Context
**Context** is what surrounds a text: the history, society and beliefs of its time and place (historical and social context), the author’s life (biographical context), and the conventions of its kind of writing (literary context).
:::

Context helps when it explains why the words of the story matter. Sui Sin Far’s stories of Chinese American life in Seattle were written while US law barred most Chinese immigration (from 1882) and Chinese Americans faced pressure to prove that they belonged. That is why the word “Americanized”, set in quotation marks as “what is called by the Westerners”, carries such weight in “Mrs. Spring Fragrance”.

:::check e12-context 1
:::

:::mistake Three ways context goes wrong
- **Context replaces the text**: “Women in the 1890s had few rights, so Edna must be miserable throughout.” Context should explain what the story shows, not decide it.
- **Irrelevant context**: a true fact that has nothing to do with the point, such as where a book was first printed.
- **The biographical trap**: treating a character as the author, or the plot as the author’s diary.
:::

:::example Context that deepens a reading
In “The Wife of His Youth”, an old woman tells Mr. Ryder that she has searched for her husband for twenty-five years. Write a sentence that uses context well.
@step Start from the story: her search has lasted twenty-five years, and she still carries his picture.
@step Bring in only the context that explains it: slavery and the Civil War scattered countless families, and freed people searched for years for husbands, wives and children.
@step Let the context explain why the detail matters, without claiming the story is only about history.
@answer “Her twenty-five-year search echoes the many families that slavery and war scattered, which gives her loyalty, and Ryder’s choice, great weight.”
:::

## Characters and their societies

Characters may **conform** to the expectations around them, **resist** them, feel **torn** between them and their own wishes, or **exploit** the rules for their own ends. Often a story is about a character moving from one of these positions to another.

In *The Awakening*, Chopin describes the “mother-women” who surround Edna Pontellier on holiday:

> They were women who idolized their children, worshiped their husbands, and esteemed it a holy privilege to efface themselves as individuals and grow wings as ministering angels.
>
> — Kate Chopin, *The Awakening* (1899)

Edna will not efface herself. She tells a friend:

> “I would give up the unessential; I would give my money, I would give my life for my children; but I wouldn’t give myself.
>
> — Kate Chopin, *The Awakening* (1899)

:::check e12-character-society 1
:::

:::tip Name the expectation first
Before you describe a character’s relationship with society, state the expectation exactly: what is a wife, a son, a gentleman, an “Americanized” neighbour supposed to do? Then you can say precisely how the character meets, refuses or bends it.
:::

In “The Soft-Hearted Sioux”, the narrator comes home from nine years at a mission school:

> Wearing a foreigner’s dress, I walked, a stranger, into my father’s village.
>
> — Zitkala-Ša, “The Soft-Hearted Sioux” (1901)

He no longer fits the warrior’s life his family planned, and his village no longer recognizes him. He belongs fully to neither world, and the story follows the cost of that.

:::

In “Mrs. Spring Fragrance”, Sui Sin Far shows a family living between two sets of expectations:

> although the Chin Yuen parents lived in a house furnished in American style, and wore American clothes, yet they religiously observed many Chinese customs, and their ideals of life were the ideals of their Chinese forefathers.
>
> — Sui Sin Far, “Mrs. Spring Fragrance” (1912)

Their daughter, Laura, loves a young man of her own choosing, but her parents have promised her to someone else. She is torn between her parents’ customs and the life she wants, and Mrs. Spring Fragrance, who moves easily between both worlds, takes her side.

In “The Wife of His Youth”, Mr. Ryder has guarded the exclusiveness of the Blue Veins. At his ball he tells the story of a man whose wife from the days of slavery has found him, and asks what the man should do. The guests answer that he should acknowledge her. Then:

> “Ladies and gentlemen,” he said, “this is the woman, and I am the man, whose story I have told you. Permit me to introduce to you the wife of my youth.”
>
> — Charles W. Chesnutt, “The Wife of His Youth” (1898)

He chooses loyalty to his past over the standing his society prizes.

:::summary
- Context is the history, society, biography and literary conventions around a text.
- Use context to explain why the story’s own words matter; never let it replace them, and leave out facts that do not bear on the point.
- A character is not the author, and a plot is not the author’s life.
- Characters conform to, resist, are torn by or exploit the expectations of their society; name the expectation, then trace the response.
:::
