Here is how the lawyer who narrates Herman Melville’s “Bartleby, the Scrivener” introduces himself:

> All who know me, consider me an eminently safe man.
>
> — Herman Melville, “Bartleby, the Scrivener” (1853)

One reader hears a man who loves comfort and fears risk. Another hears a businessman whose world is measured in money and safety. A third notices how the line feels different on a second reading, once we know what happens to Bartleby. Each reader is using a different **critical lens**: a set of questions that brings some features of a text into focus. In this lesson you will recognize five lenses and choose the evidence that best supports a reading through each.

## Five lenses

:::definition Critical lenses
- A **historical** lens asks how the events and beliefs of the time shape the story.
- A **feminist** lens asks how the story shows the roles, power and voices of women and men.
- A **psychological** lens asks what hidden fears, desires and defences drive the characters.
- An **economic (class)** lens asks how money, work and social class shape the characters’ lives.
- A **reader-response** lens asks how readers make meaning as they read, and how different readers may respond.
:::

A lens is not a verdict. It is a way of asking questions, and the same story can support several readings at once. A strong essay names its lens, then proves its reading with evidence from the text.

:::

:::example One story, three lenses
In “The Beast in the Jungle”, John Marcher spends his life sure that something rare and strange will happen to him, while May Bartram waits beside him. Write one sentence of reading through each of three lenses.
@step **Psychological**: his certainty that a great fate awaits him is a defence that spares him the risk of loving May.
@step **Feminist**: May’s life is spent serving the needs of a man who never sees her as more than a companion in his waiting.
@step **Historical**: the story reflects the narrow, careful manners of upper-class London around 1900, where feelings were rarely spoken aloud.
@answer Each sentence asks a different question of the same story, and each would need its own evidence.
:::

The same lawyer from “Bartleby” describes one of his clients:

> I do not speak it in vanity, but simply record the fact, that I was not unemployed in my profession by the late John Jacob Astor; a name which, I admit, I love to repeat, for it hath a rounded and orbicular sound to it, and rings like unto bullion.
>
> — Herman Melville, “Bartleby, the Scrivener” (1853)

Through an **economic** lens, the lawyer’s pleasure in the very sound of a millionaire’s name shows how deeply money shapes his sense of himself: the name “rings like unto bullion”, like gold. Through a **reader-response** lens, we might notice instead how hard he works to win our trust (“I do not speak it in vanity”), and ask whether we should give it.

:::note Which lens fits?
Any lens can be tried on any story, but the strongest readings start from what the story keeps putting in front of us. A story set in a Wall Street office, full of wages, copying and a rich client’s name, invites an economic reading. A story about a marriage in which a husband inspects his wife’s sunburn invites a feminist one. Let the details suggest the lens.
:::

## Evidence for a reading

A reading is only as strong as the quotation that supports it. Choose the words that show what your claim says, not merely a line from the right story. At May Bartram’s grave, Marcher finally understands:

> The escape would have been to love her; then, *then* he would have lived.
>
> — Henry James, “The Beast in the Jungle” (1903)

This sentence is strong evidence for a psychological reading: the waiting that filled his life was what kept him from love.

:::check e12-lens-reading 1
:::

:::mistake A quotation that is only nearby
A quotation can come from the right scene and still not prove the claim. If your claim is that a husband treats his wife as property, the line about the sea in *The Awakening* is from the right book but the wrong point. The line you need is the one where Mr. Pontellier looks at Edna “as one looks at a valuable piece of personal property which has suffered some damage”.
:::

In Chekhov’s “The Lady with the Dog”, the narrator says of Gurov:

> He had begun being unfaithful to her long ago—had been unfaithful to her often, and, probably on that account, almost always spoke ill of women, and when they were talked about in his presence, used to call them “the lower race.”
>
> — Anton Chekhov (tr. Constance Garnett), “The Lady with the Dog” (1899)

A feminist reading can build on this: his contempt for women and his dependence on them go together.

A careful reader then tests the reading against the whole story. Gurov, who called women “the lower race”, falls truly in love for the first time with Anna Sergeyevna, so a feminist reading might ask whether the story shows a man changed by love, or a man who still cannot imagine a woman’s life apart from his own.

:::check e12-lens-reading 2
:::

:::tip Lens, claim, quotation, explanation
A paragraph of critical analysis usually moves in four steps: name the lens, state the claim, quote the evidence, and explain exactly how the words support the claim. The explanation is where the marks are.
:::

:::summary
- A critical lens is a set of questions: historical, feminist, psychological, economic (class) and reader-response lenses each bring different features into focus.
- One story can support several readings; name the lens you are using.
- Choose the quotation that shows exactly what your claim says, then explain how it does so.
- Test a reading against the whole story, including the parts that seem to argue against it.
:::
