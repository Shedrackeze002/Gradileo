On a winter evening in 1895, a London audience watched a grand lady interview a young man who wished to marry her daughter. He confessed that he had lost both his parents. Her reply brought the house down:

> To lose one parent, Mr. Worthing, may be regarded as a misfortune; to lose both looks like carelessness.
>
> — Lady Bracknell, in Oscar Wilde, *The Importance of Being Earnest* (1895)

The audience laughed, and many of them were the very people being laughed at. Lady Bracknell treats a family tragedy as a lapse in good manners, because in her world, family history is a question of social standing. In Grade 11 you found the target of a satire and named tools such as irony, exaggeration and parody. In this lesson you will tell two great modes of satire apart, name five more of its tools, and find the targets of three masters: Wilde, Alexander Pope and Jonathan Swift.

## Two modes of satire

Satire is named after two Roman poets who practised it in very different moods.

:::definition Horatian and Juvenalian satire
**Horatian satire**, named after the poet Horace, is gentle and amused. It laughs at **folly**, the silly, vain or foolish habits that people share, and invites readers to smile, perhaps at themselves.

**Juvenalian satire**, named after the poet Juvenal, is bitter and angry. It attacks **vice** and wrongdoing that cause real harm, and it aims to make readers indignant, not merely amused.
:::

Wilde is Horatian: his audience leaves the theatre delighted, not furious. Swift, in *Gulliver’s Travels* (1726), can be both. During Gulliver’s second voyage, the giant King of Brobdingnag listens to Gulliver proudly describe his country’s laws, politics and wars, and then gives his verdict:

> … I cannot but conclude the bulk of your natives to be the most pernicious race of little odious vermin that nature ever suffered to crawl upon the surface of the earth.
>
> — Jonathan Swift, *Gulliver’s Travels* (1726)

There is no gentleness here. The King condemns the cruelty and corruption that Gulliver has described as glories, and Swift means readers to feel the sting. That is Juvenalian satire.

:::tip Listen to the tone
To tell the modes apart, ask two questions. Is the target a folly (vanity, fussiness, snobbery) or a vice that does real harm (cruelty, greed, corruption)? And is the tone amused or furious?
:::

## Five tools of satire

:::definition Five more tools
- An **epigram** is a short, witty, memorable saying, often built on a surprising twist.
- **Mock-heroic** writing describes something trivial in the grand style of an epic.
- An **allegory** is a story whose characters and events stand for real people, groups or events.
- **Caricature** exaggerates a person’s or a type’s features to make them ridiculous.
- **Invective** is direct, harsh, abusive denunciation of a target.
:::

Wilde’s comedy is built from epigrams. Each one sets up an expectation and then flips it:

> Never speak disrespectfully of Society, Algernon. Only people who can’t get into it do that.
>
> — Lady Bracknell, in Oscar Wilde, *The Importance of Being Earnest* (1895)

Alexander Pope’s *The Rape of the Lock* (1714) is the great mock-heroic poem in English. In Pope’s day, *rape* in this title kept its older meaning: a seizing or carrying off. The poem is about a real quarrel between two families after a young lord snipped a lock of hair from a young woman’s head at a party. Pope tells the story in the style of an epic, with gods, battles and solemn invocations:

> What dire offence from am’rous causes springs,\
> What mighty contests rise from trivial things,
>
> — Alexander Pope, *The Rape of the Lock* (1714)

He even mocks the court. Queen Anne ruled three kingdoms (England, Scotland and Ireland), yet at her palace of Hampton Court:

> Here thou, great Anna! whom three realms obey,\
> Dost sometimes counsel take—and sometimes tea.
>
> — Alexander Pope, *The Rape of the Lock* (1714)

The verb *take* governs both “counsel” (advice on affairs of state) and “tea”. This figure, one word joined to two very different objects, is called **zeugma**, and here it puts the business of three kingdoms on the same level as a tea party.

Swift’s Lilliput is an allegory. Its people have fought wars over whether eggs should be broken at the big end or the small end:

> It is computed that eleven thousand persons have at several times suffered death, rather than submit to break their eggs at the smaller end.
>
> — Jonathan Swift, *Gulliver’s Travels* (1726)

Readers in Swift’s day saw the bitter quarrels between Catholics and Protestants, and between Britain and France, in the war between the Big-endians and the Little-endians.

## The comedy of manners and its targets

:::definition Comedy of manners
A **comedy of manners** is a play that satirizes the behaviour, customs and values of a fashionable social class, usually through witty dialogue, schemes about love and marriage, and characters who care more about appearances than about feeling.
:::

*The Importance of Being Earnest* is the most famous comedy of manners in English. Its targets are the upper class’s snobbery, its marriages arranged for money and rank, and its habit of putting style before sincerity.

:::example Finding the target
Miss Prism, a governess, describes the three-volume novel she once wrote: “The good ended happily, and the bad unhappily. That is what Fiction means.” What is the target of the satire?
@step Notice the twist: Miss Prism speaks as if a happy ending for the good were the *definition* of fiction.
@step Ask what that implies about real life: if this is what *fiction* means, then in life the good do not always end happily.
@step Ask whose attitude is being mocked: the tidy, moralizing novels of Wilde’s day, and the readers who wanted every story to reward virtue.
@answer The target is the comfortable, preachy fiction of the Victorian age, which pretended that the world always rewards the good and punishes the bad. Wilde laughs at it gently, so the satire is **Horatian**.
:::

:::mistake The literal target
The target of a satire is rarely what its words are about. Swift is not writing about eggs, Pope is not upset about tea, and Lady Bracknell’s real subject is not parents. Ask whose attitudes or values the passage makes ridiculous.
:::

:::summary
- **Horatian** satire laughs gently at folly; **Juvenalian** satire attacks vice with anger and scorn.
- Five tools of satire are the **epigram**, the **mock-heroic**, **allegory**, **caricature** and **invective**.
- A **comedy of manners**, such as Wilde’s *The Importance of Being Earnest*, satirizes a fashionable class through witty dialogue; Pope’s *The Rape of the Lock* is mock-heroic, and Swift’s Lilliput is an allegory.
- The target is rarely what the words are about. Ask whose attitudes or values are made ridiculous.
:::
