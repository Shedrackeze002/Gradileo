The word **disaster** hides a belief that most people who use it have long forgotten. It comes, through Italian *disastro*, from Latin *dis-*, “badly”, and *astrum*, “star”: a disaster was once a misfortune blamed on unlucky stars. Every word has a history like this, and knowing it can help you remember a word, work out an unfamiliar one and use it precisely. In this lesson you will use **etymology** to understand words, and then choose the precise **academic vocabulary** that strong literary analysis needs.

## Words have histories

:::definition Etymology
**Etymology** is the history of a word: where it came from and how its form and meaning have changed. A **root** is the core part of a word that carries its basic meaning.
:::

Many English words keep a picture from their origin. **Companion** comes from Latin *com-*, “with”, and *panis*, “bread”: a companion is someone you share bread with. **Sinister** comes from Latin *sinister*, “on the left”, because the left side was once thought unlucky, so the word still suggests that something evil is coming.

Some words come from stories and people. **Tantalize** comes from Tantalus, punished in Greek myth with food and water always just out of reach; **panic** comes from Pan, the Greek god whose sudden cry was said to fill people with fear. **Boycott** comes from Charles Boycott, a land agent in Ireland whom local people refused to deal with in 1880, and **sandwich** is named after the fourth Earl of Sandwich.

## Meanings shift

Words change their meanings over the centuries, sometimes completely. **Silly** comes from Old English *sælig*, “happy, blessed”; it later meant “innocent”, and only then “foolish”. **Nice** comes from Latin *nescius*, “not knowing”, and in Middle English it meant “foolish” before it softened into “pleasant”. **Awful** first meant “full of awe” or “inspiring awe”, and has weakened to “very bad”.

So when you read older texts, a familiar word may not mean what you expect. When Shakespeare or the King James Bible uses a word that seems odd, check its meaning at the time.

:::mistake Stories too good to be true
Some popular word histories are inventions. *Posh* does not come from “port out, starboard home”, and *sincere* does not come from Latin *sine cera*, “without wax”. Dictionaries reject both. Trust a good dictionary’s etymology over a story that only sounds clever.
:::

## Roots and cognates

Latin roots run through thousands of English words. *Nox, noctis* (night) gives **nocturnal**; *mater* (mother) gives **maternal**; *pes, pedis* (foot) gives **pedal** and **pedestrian**; *cor, cordis* (heart) gives **cordial**.

:::example Working out a word from its root
Work out the meaning of **nocturnal** in *Owls are nocturnal hunters.*
@step Find the root: *noct-*, from Latin *nox, noctis*, “night”.
@step The ending *-al* makes an adjective, “having to do with”.
@step Check the meaning against the sentence: owls hunt in the dark.
@answer **Nocturnal** means “active at night”, so the sentence says that owls hunt at night.
:::

:::tip Roots that only look alike
Check a root before you trust it. *Pedal* comes from Latin *pes*, “foot”, but *pediatrician* comes from Greek *pais, paidos*, “child”. The letters match; the histories do not.
:::

:::definition Cognates
**Cognates** are words in related languages that descend from the same ancestral word: English *night*, German *Nacht* and Latin *nox*; English *mother*, German *Mutter* and Latin *mater*.
:::

Cognates are relatives, not borrowings. English, German and Latin all descend from one ancient language, Proto-Indo-European, so many of their everyday words grew from the same roots.

## Precise verbs for analysis

Weak analysis leans on a few vague verbs: a text **shows**, **talks about** or **deals with** something. These verbs could describe almost anything a text does. Precise verbs say exactly what the writer is doing:

| Verb | Use it when the text … |
| --- | --- |
| juxtaposes | places two things side by side so that they are compared |
| foreshadows | hints at something that will happen later |
| undercuts | weakens something by working against it |
| reinforces | strengthens an idea that is already there |
| critiques | judges something and exposes its faults |
| implies | suggests something without stating it |
| evokes | calls up a memory or feeling |
| subverts | overturns what was expected or believed |

## Precise nouns

The same holds for nouns. A **motif** is an image or idea that recurs throughout a work. A **foil** is a character whose contrast with another brings out that character’s qualities. **Subtext** is the meaning beneath what is actually said, and a **connotation** is a feeling a word suggests beyond its dictionary meaning. **Ambiguity** is the quality of being open to more than one reading, and **tension** is the strain between two opposing forces or ideas.

## Revising vague analysis

:::example Making a sentence of analysis precise
Revise: *This quote shows that he is angry.*
@step Replace the vague verb: what do his words do? Suppose his short, clipped sentences hold something back.
@step Name the detail in the text instead of “this quote”.
@step Say exactly what the detail suggests, in a precise verb.
@answer *His short, clipped replies suggest an anger that he is struggling to keep under control.*
:::

:::mistake Length is not precision
*This quote shows that he is very angry and upset about what happened* is longer, but no more precise: “very” and “upset” add words, not meaning. A precise sentence names what the text does and how.
:::

:::

:::summary
- Etymology is a word’s history; a root carries its basic meaning.
- Words come from images, myths and people, and their meanings can shift completely over time.
- Trust dictionary etymologies, not clever stories, and check a root before you rely on it.
- Cognates descend from the same ancestral word in related languages.
- In analysis, replace “shows”, “talks about” and “deals with” with precise verbs and nouns, and name exactly what the text does.
:::
