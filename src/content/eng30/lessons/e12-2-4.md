Sooner or later, on a test, in an exam or in a book you pick up, you will meet a poem nobody has explained to you. You have no notes, no class discussion and perhaps no idea who the poet was. This is the **unseen poem**, and it is the truest test of everything this unit has taught: you have only the words on the page, and they are enough.

In this lesson you will practise a method for reading an unseen poem, moving from what is happening, through how the poem is built, to what it means.

## A method for the unseen poem

:::definition Unseen poem
An **unseen poem** is one you are asked to read and analyse without having studied it before. Questions on it usually move from the **situation** (who is speaking, where, and what is happening), through **structure** and **imagery**, to **tone** and **meaning**.
:::

Work through the poem in four passes, each one building on the last:

| Pass | Ask | Look for |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Situation | Who is speaking, to whom, where and when? What is happening? | pronouns, places, times of day, verbs of action |
| 2. Structure | How is the poem built, and where does it turn? | stanzas, a *but* or *yet*, a change of tense, a question and answer |
| 3. Imagery and sound | Which pictures and sounds stand out? What do they suggest? | comparisons, repeated words, striking sounds |
| 4. Tone and meaning | How does the speaker feel? What is the poem finally about? | word choice, what is said and what is held back |

:::tip Use everything you are given
Read the title, the poet’s name, any date and any glossed words before you read the poem: they are clues. Then read the poem twice, once straight through for the sense and once slowly, with a pencil.
:::

:::example Reading an unseen poem
Edward Thomas wrote this poem in January 1916, in an army camp, six months after he had joined the army. Read it, then work through the four passes.

> Rain, midnight rain, nothing but the wild rain\
> On this bleak hut, and solitude, and me\
> Remembering again that I shall die\
> And neither hear the rain nor give it thanks\
> For washing me cleaner than I have been\
> Since I was born into this solitude.\
> Blessed are the dead that the rain rains upon:\
> But here I pray that none whom once I loved\
> Is dying to-night or lying still awake\
> Solitary, listening to the rain,\
> Either in pain or thus in sympathy\
> Helpless among the living and the dead,\
> Like a cold water among broken reeds,\
> Myriads of broken reeds all still and stiff,\
> Like me who have no love which this wild rain\
> Has not dissolved except the love of death,\
> If love it be towards what is perfect and\
> Cannot, the tempest tells me, disappoint.
>
> — Edward Thomas, “Rain” (1917)

@step Situation: a speaker alone in a “bleak hut” at midnight, listening to heavy rain and thinking about his own death. Knowing the date and the camp, we can guess he is a soldier who may soon be sent to the front.
@step Structure: the whole poem is one long sentence that keeps running on, like the rain. It turns at line 8 (“But here I pray”) from his own death to the people he once loved, and then turns back to himself at the end.
@step Imagery and sound: the word “rain” returns again and again; the dead are “Blessed” because the rain falls on them; the lonely are like “broken reeds all still and stiff”.
@step Tone and meaning: bleak, lonely and calm rather than panicked. The rain has washed away every love but “the love of death”, which at least “Cannot … disappoint”.
@answer A soldier alone in a hut at night lets the rain carry his thoughts to death, to the suffering of others, and back to himself. The poem’s one unbroken sentence and its insistent rain make his loneliness feel endless, and its quiet ending, a love of death as the one thing that cannot disappoint, is all the more disturbing for being so calm.
:::

## Finding the turn

Most short poems change direction once, and the **turn** is often the key to the whole poem. Look for:

- a word like *but*, *yet* or *then* at the start of a line or stanza;
- a change of tense, from past to present or from present to future;
- a change of speaker, or a question followed by an answer;
- in a sonnet, line 9 or the closing couplet.

When you find the turn, describe both sides of it: what the poem was doing before, and what it does after. In “Rain” the turn at “But here I pray” moves from the speaker’s own death to his fear for others; naming both halves shows that you understand how the poem is built.

:::mistake Inventing a meaning the words cannot support
An unseen poem can be read in more than one way, but every reading must rest on the words. If you decide that “Rain” is about a farmer worried for his crops, look for the evidence: there is none. Before you choose an answer, point to the line that proves it.
:::

## Tone: what is said and what is held back

Tone is the speaker’s attitude, and in many unseen poems it is the hardest thing to name. Some poems state their feelings openly; others hold them back, and the restraint itself tells us how strong they are. A speaker who describes a terrible loss in calm, ordinary words may be in shock, or may be refusing to break down in public.

Precise words make precise answers. When a plain word comes to mind, reach for a sharper one:

| Instead of | Try |
|---|---|
| sad | grieving, mournful, desolate, wistful, resigned |
| happy | joyful, serene, content, exultant |
| angry | bitter, indignant, scornful, defiant |
| calm | restrained, detached, contemplative, numb |
| funny | wry, ironic, playful, sardonic |

Choose the word that fits *this* speaker. The speaker of “Rain” is not simply sad: he is desolate and resigned, and his calm is part of what makes the poem frightening.

:::tip Pair the tone word with evidence
Avoid one-word answers like “sad”. Choose a precise pair, such as *calm but grieving* or *bitter and ironic*, and point to the words that create it. If the tone shifts, say where.
:::

:::summary
- An unseen poem is one you analyse without having studied it. Use everything you are given: title, poet, date and glosses.
- Read in four passes: situation, structure, imagery and sound, then tone and meaning.
- Find the turn (a *but* or *yet*, a change of tense or speaker, line 9 of a sonnet) and describe both sides of it.
- Every reading must rest on the words of the poem; name tone precisely and support it with evidence.
:::
