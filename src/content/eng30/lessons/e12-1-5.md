Read the last sentence of James Joyce’s “The Dead” aloud, slowly:

> His soul swooned slowly as he heard the snow falling faintly through the universe and faintly falling, like the descent of their last end, upon all the living and the dead.
>
> — James Joyce, “The Dead” (1914)

The soft *s* and *f* sounds, the words “falling faintly” turned around into “faintly falling”, and the long, unhurried sentence all make the prose itself fall like snow. That is **style**: not what a writer says, but how. In this lesson you will analyse the features of a writer’s style and their effects, and recognize **tone** and **irony**, the attitudes that a narrator’s words carry.

## Style and authorial voice

:::definition Style
A writer’s **style** is their characteristic way of using language: **diction** (word choice, from plain to grand), **syntax** (sentence length and structure), **imagery**, and the **rhythm and sound** of the prose. The personality we sense behind those choices is the writer’s **authorial voice**.
:::

Style is easiest to see by contrast. Joyce’s last sentence is long, musical and full of repeated sound. Zitkala-Ša ends “The Soft-Hearted Sioux”, as the narrator waits for dawn and the gallows, in a very different way:

> Soon, soon I shall know, for now I see the east is growing red. My heart is strong. My face is calm. My eyes are dry and eager for new scenes.
>
> — Zitkala-Ša, “The Soft-Hearted Sioux” (1901)

The sentences are short and plain, and each one is a statement of control. The bare style matches the composure the narrator is claiming in the face of death.

:::example Naming a feature and its effect
The lawyer who narrates Melville’s “Bartleby, the Scrivener” mentions a famous client. He says the name John Jacob Astor “hath a rounded and orbicular sound to it, and rings like unto bullion.” Name the main feature of style and its effect.
@step Look at the diction: “hath”, “orbicular”, “rings like unto bullion” are grand, formal and old-fashioned.
@step Notice what they describe: the small pleasure of repeating a rich man’s name.
@step Grand words used for a small vanity are comic, and they show us the narrator’s love of money and standing.
@answer Formal, old-fashioned diction used for a small matter. It makes the narrator gently comic, and shows how much wealth and respectability mean to him.
:::

In “Kew Gardens”, Virginia Woolf’s style is built on imagery. A few square inches of earth fill a whole sentence:

> The light fell either upon the smooth, grey back of a pebble, or, the shell of a snail with its brown, circular veins, or falling into a raindrop, it expanded with such intensity of red, blue and yellow the thin walls of water that one expected them to burst and disappear.
>
> — Virginia Woolf, “Kew Gardens” (1919)

Close attention turns a flower bed into something vast and full of colour, the way a child might see it lying in the grass.

:::tip Feature, then effect
“Joyce uses repetition” is only half an answer. Always add what the feature does: “the repeated, reversed words slow the sentence into a hushed rhythm, so the prose seems to fall like the snow it describes.” The effect is where the marks are.
:::

## Tone and irony

:::definition Tone
**Tone** is the attitude a narrator’s words carry towards their subject: for example **ironic**, **sympathetic**, **detached** or **admiring**. A story’s tone can stay steady or **shift** as the story moves.
:::

:::definition Irony
**Irony** is a gap between what is said or expected and what is meant or happens.
- **Verbal irony**: the words say one thing while the speaker, or the writer, plainly means another.
- **Situational irony**: the outcome reverses what the characters expected.
- **Dramatic irony**: the reader understands something that a character does not.
:::

In “The Wife of His Youth”, Chesnutt’s narrator describes how the Blue Vein Society came to be made up of its light-skinned members:

> By accident, combined perhaps with some natural affinity, the society consisted of individuals who were, generally speaking, more white than black.
>
> — Charles W. Chesnutt, “The Wife of His Youth” (1898)

The sentence is said with a straight face, but the reader is meant to doubt “by accident”: the society chose its members by colour, and the narrator’s mild words let us see through its own excuse.

:::check e12-tone-irony 1
:::

Irony can also be gentle and affectionate. In Sui Sin Far’s “Mrs. Spring Fragrance”, the heroine, eager to be “Americanized”, comforts a young neighbour by quoting “a beautiful American poem written by a noble American named Tennyson”. Alfred Tennyson was English, so her eagerness has run ahead of her, and the story smiles at the slip without mocking her.

:::mistake Irony is not bad luck
Rain on the day of a picnic is unlucky, not ironic. Irony needs a gap between expectation and outcome, or between words and meaning: rain on the day of a picnic held to celebrate the end of a drought would be ironic. Look for the reversal, not just the misfortune.
:::

:::check e12-tone-irony 2
:::

A story’s tone can change completely. Melville’s lawyer begins by calling himself “a man who, from his youth upwards, has been filled with a profound conviction that the easiest way of life is the best.” By the last line, his comfortable self-satisfaction has broken open:

> Ah Bartleby! Ah humanity!
>
> — Herman Melville, “Bartleby, the Scrivener” (1853)

:::

:::summary
- **Style** is how a writer writes: diction, syntax, imagery, and rhythm and sound. Behind it we hear an **authorial voice**.
- Name a feature of style, then explain its effect on meaning or feeling.
- **Tone** is the narrator’s attitude; it may be ironic, sympathetic, detached or admiring, and it can shift.
- **Irony** is a gap between what is said or expected and what is meant or happens: verbal, situational or dramatic.
:::
