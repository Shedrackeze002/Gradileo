In March 1865 the American Civil War was almost won. Abraham Lincoln had been re-elected the autumn before, and the crowd that gathered for his inauguration expected a victory speech: praise for the Union army, and perhaps a promise to punish the South. Instead Lincoln spoke for about seven minutes, and he refused to blame one side alone:

> Both parties deprecated war, but one of them would make war rather than let the nation survive, and the other would accept war rather than let it perish, and the war came.
>
> — Abraham Lincoln, the Second Inaugural Address (1865)

Why would a winning leader speak so generously of the enemy? Because Lincoln was not trying to celebrate the war; he was trying to end it well. Every sentence of the address serves that one plan. In this lesson you will learn to name the **strategy** of a speech or essay, to explain how each part serves it, and to judge the **effect** a choice has on a particular audience at a particular moment.

## Strategy: the plan of the whole

:::definition Rhetorical strategy
A **rhetorical strategy** is the overall plan by which a speech or essay moves its audience from what they already think to what the writer wants them to think or do. The order in which the parts are placed to carry out that plan is the text’s **arrangement**.
:::

Most persuasive texts follow one of a handful of strategies. Learn to recognize these six:

| Strategy | How it works | It suits an audience that… |
|---|---|---|
| **Problem and solution** | shows what is wrong, then proposes a fix and explains why it would work | does not yet see the problem, or sees no way out |
| **Concession and turn** | grants a point to the other side, then turns that point to support the claim | already has a strong objection in mind |
| **Shared values and demand** | names values the audience already holds, then asks for action that honours them | agrees in principle but has not yet acted |
| **Story and lesson** | tells an anecdote or parable first, then draws out its lesson | would resist a bare argument but will follow a story |
| **Point-by-point refutation** | states each objection in turn and answers it with evidence | has heard many arguments against the claim |
| **Dilemma** | shows that every possible case, taken one at a time, leads to the same conclusion | might otherwise escape through an “unless” |

Booker T. Washington opened his address to the Cotton States Exposition in Atlanta, in 1895, with a parable:

> A ship lost at sea for many days suddenly sighted a friendly vessel. From the mast of the unfortunate vessel was seen a signal, “Water, water; we die of thirst!” The answer from the friendly vessel at once came back, “Cast down your bucket where you are.”
>
> — Booker T. Washington, the Atlanta Exposition Address (1895)

The ship had drifted to the mouth of the Amazon River, so the water all around it was fresh. Washington then drew the lesson twice over: he told Black Southerners to build their future where they already lived, and he told white employers to “cast down your bucket” among Black workers rather than look elsewhere. That is **story and lesson**: the image is easy to remember, and the lesson seems to grow out of it rather than being imposed.

:::note Two audiences
Washington spoke to a mostly white Southern audience thirty years after slavery ended. His speech was praised by white listeners, but many Black critics, among them W. E. B. Du Bois, later called it the “Atlanta Compromise” because it seemed to accept segregation in return for economic progress. Analysing his strategy does not mean approving of it.
:::

John Stuart Mill, arguing in *On Liberty* (1859) that no opinion should ever be silenced, uses a **dilemma**. Either the silenced opinion is right or it is wrong, and he shows that silencing it does harm either way:

> If the opinion is right, they are deprived of the opportunity of exchanging error for truth: if wrong, they lose, what is almost as great a benefit, the clearer perception and livelier impression of truth, produced by its collision with error.
>
> — John Stuart Mill, *On Liberty* (1859)

:::tip Concession and turn, or refutation?
Both deal with the other side. In a **concession and turn**, the writer *agrees* with an objection and then uses it (“Yes, the pool loses money, and that is exactly why…”). In a **refutation**, the writer *disagrees* with each objection and answers it with evidence.
:::

## How the parts serve the whole

Once you have named the strategy, ask what job each part does in it. Lincoln’s strategy in 1865 is **shared values and demand**: he reminds North and South of what they share, and then asks the victors to act on it. So, before he makes his demand, he places both sides on the same ground:

> Both read the same Bible and pray to the same God, and each invokes His aid against the other.
>
> — Abraham Lincoln, the Second Inaugural Address (1865)

:::example Explaining how a part serves the strategy
The address ends: “With malice toward none, with charity for all … let us strive on to finish the work we are in, to bind up the nation’s wounds”. How does the sentence about the Bible prepare listeners for this ending?
@step Name the strategy: shared values and demand. The demand comes at the end: charity, not revenge.
@step Ask what the Bible sentence does: it shows both sides as alike, people of one faith who prayed to the same God.
@step Connect the part to the plan: if the enemy is like us, then charity toward the enemy is natural, not weak.
@answer By showing North and South as people of the same faith, the sentence prepares the victorious North to accept Lincoln’s closing demand for “charity for all” instead of vengeance.
:::

## Effect on an audience

:::definition Effect, audience and kairos
The **effect** of a rhetorical choice is what it does to a particular **audience**: what they feel, believe or are ready to do because of it. **Kairos**, a Greek word for “the right moment”, is the fit between an appeal and its time and place. The same words can stir one audience and leave another cold, or move people in one week and be forgotten the next.
:::

In December 1776 the American army had lost battle after battle, and many soldiers planned to go home when their service ended. Thomas Paine’s pamphlet *The American Crisis* was read aloud to the troops in their camps:

> These are the times that try men’s souls. The summer soldier and the sunshine patriot will, in this crisis, shrink from the service of their country; but he that stands it now, deserves the love and thanks of man and woman.
>
> — Thomas Paine, *The American Crisis* (1776)

Paine divides his listeners into two kinds: the fair-weather soldier who leaves when things get hard, and the one who “stands it now”. No soldier hearing it wants to be the first kind, so the effect is to shame anyone tempted to leave and to honour everyone who stays. The moment matters too: read at the army’s lowest point, the words met their audience exactly when they were needed.

Audience can change everything. When Elizabeth Cady Stanton addressed a committee of Congress in 1892, she knew that its members had heard the case for women’s right to vote many times:

> We have been speaking before Committees of the Judiciary for the last twenty years, and we have gone over all the arguments in favor of a sixteenth amendment which are familiar to all you gentlemen; therefore, it will not be necessary that I should repeat them again.
>
> — Elizabeth Cady Stanton, “The Solitude of Self” (1892)

To a tired committee, a promise *not* to repeat the old arguments is the best way to win back its attention.

:::mistake Vague effects
“It makes the reader interested” or “it grabs the audience’s attention” could be said of almost any sentence. Name the audience precisely (the soldiers thinking of going home, the committee that has heard it all before) and say exactly what the choice makes them feel or do.
:::

:::summary
- A **rhetorical strategy** is the plan of a whole text. Six common ones are problem and solution, concession and turn, shared values and demand, story and lesson, point-by-point refutation, and dilemma.
- Explain each part by the job it does in the strategy: Lincoln shows both sides as alike so that his call for “charity for all” seems natural.
- The **effect** of a choice depends on its **audience** and its moment (**kairos**). Name the audience precisely and say what the choice makes them feel, believe or do.
:::
