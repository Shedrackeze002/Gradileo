Research writing asks you to do three things well: find sources, judge them and weave what they say into an argument that is still your own. In Grade 11 you learned to support a claim with evidence. In Grade 12 the evidence comes from other people, so you must decide whom to trust and how to use their findings honestly. Every source and finding in this lesson is invented, for practice.

## Judging a source

:::definition Reliable source
A **reliable source** for a research question is written by someone with real expertise, rests on wide evidence, is recent enough for the question, has no stake in one answer and answers the question you are actually asking.
:::

Those five tests have names, and each comes with a warning sign.

| Test | Ask | Warning sign |
|---|---|---|
| **Authority** | Who wrote it, and what do they know about this subject? | No named author, or an author whose expertise lies elsewhere. |
| **Evidence** | What does it rest on? | One person’s experience, or claims with no data behind them. |
| **Currency** | Is it recent enough for this question? | It was written before the thing it discusses had been tried or studied. |
| **Purpose** | Why was it written, and who gains? | A seller, sponsor or campaign that profits if one answer wins. |
| **Relevance** | Does it answer *this* question? | It studies a nearby question: adults instead of teenagers, work instead of school. |

Currency and relevance always depend on the question. A story published in 1922 is the best possible source for an essay on that story, while a 1922 article on heart medicine is useless for a report on heart medicine today.

:::

Few sources pass every test, so the real skill is weighing. A source can be useful and limited at once: say what it can show, name its limit and decide what else you need to check it against.

:::example Weighing a mixed source
A student is researching this question: *Does daily reading time in class improve students’ vocabulary?* The source (invented): *A 2025 report by a company that sells classroom reading software, finding that vocabulary scores rose in 15 schools after they began daily reading time using its program.*
@step Authority and evidence: it compares scores before and after in 15 schools, which is real evidence, not one person’s impression.
@step Purpose: the company sells the software and gains if the result is good. Also, every school used its program, so the effect of reading time cannot be separated from the effect of the software.
@step Weigh the two: the source is worth using, with its limit stated, and it should be checked against independent research.
@answer *Useful evidence that vocabulary rose, but the seller gains from the result and its schools all used its own software, so the finding needs checking against independent research before it can carry the claim.*
:::

:::check e12-source-evaluation 2
:::

:::mistake All or nothing
Rejecting a source for any flaw, or treating one study as the final word, are both mistakes. *A charity runs the program, so the report is worthless* goes too far; so does *the charity knows its own program best, so the question is settled*. Use the source, name its limit, and look for independent support.
:::

## Weaving sources into your argument

:::definition Synthesis and attribution
**Synthesis** combines findings from several sources so that together they serve your own point. **Attribution** names, in your sentence, where each finding comes from.
:::

When students use sources, four problems come up again and again.

| Problem | What it looks like |
|---|---|
| **Source dump** | Findings strung together, one sentence per source, with no point of the writer’s own. |
| **Misrepresentation** | The source is made to say more than it does: *found a link* becomes *proved*, *most* becomes *every*. |
| **Patchwriting** | The source’s own sentence, with a few words changed or moved, presented as the writer’s. |
| **Missing attribution** | The findings appear in the writer’s sentence, but the reader cannot tell where they came from. |

Here is a student’s claim, *schools should keep their libraries staffed*, with two invented sources.

- **The Westbrook Library study**: Students who visited the school library weekly read twice as many books a year as students who did not.
- **The Teen Reading Survey**: Most students said they chose books they had first seen on library displays.

:::example From source dump to synthesis
Revise this source dump: *The Westbrook Library study says weekly visitors read twice as many books. The Teen Reading Survey says students choose books from displays. These are two facts about libraries.*
@step Find the writer’s point. Both findings suggest that a library works because people run it, not just because it holds books.
@step Restate each finding accurately, in your own words: weekly visitors read twice as many books a year; most students found their books on displays.
@step Name each source in the sentence, with a verb that matches the strength of its evidence, and let the writer’s point lead.
@answer *A staffed library does more than store books: the Westbrook Library study found that weekly visitors read twice as many books a year, and in the Teen Reading Survey most students said they found their books on library displays, which someone has to build.*
:::

:::check e12-integrating-sources 1
:::

:::tip Verbs that tell the truth
The verb that introduces a source is a claim about it. Use *found*, *measured* or *reported* for results, *argues* or *claims* for opinions and *suggests* when the evidence is tentative. Save *proves* for mathematics: research rarely proves anything.
:::

:::mistake Patchwriting is not paraphrase
Moving a source’s clauses around, or swapping in a synonym here and there, keeps the source’s sentence and passes it off as yours. That is plagiarism even when the source is named. To paraphrase honestly, close the source, say the idea in your own way, then check it against the original for accuracy.
:::

:::check e12-integrating-sources 2
:::

:::note Citation styles
Your teacher or examination will tell you which citation style to use for the reference list. The skill in this lesson comes before any style: a sentence that names its sources, reports them accurately and makes them serve your point.
:::

:::check e12-integrating-sources 3
:::

:::summary
- Judge a source by its authority, evidence, currency, purpose and relevance to your question.
- Weigh mixed sources: say what each can show, name its limit, and check it against independent research.
- Synthesis makes several findings serve one point of your own, reported accurately and in your own words.
- Name each source in the sentence, with a verb that matches the strength of its evidence; avoid dumping, misrepresenting and patchwriting.
:::
