A reader trusts a careful writer. One wrong verb, a list that changes shape halfway through, or an opening phrase that seems to describe the wrong person can make a strong argument look careless, or even make it say something absurd: *When only five, Diego’s family moved to Nairobi* seems to claim that the whole family was five years old. In this lesson you will apply the rules that matter most in long, complex sentences: **agreement** in tricky places, **parallel** structure, logical **comparisons** and clear **modification** and reference.

## Agreement in tricky places

A verb agrees with its subject, but in some sentences the subject is hard to find.

:::definition Four agreement rules
1. **“The number of …” and “a number of …”.** *The number* is one number, so it takes a singular verb: *The number of applicants **has** risen.* *A number of* means “several”, so it takes a plural verb: *A number of applicants **have** withdrawn.*
2. **After “there”.** “There” is not the subject. The verb agrees with the subject that follows it: *There **is** one reason*; *there **are** several reasons.*
3. **“Either … or” and “neither … nor”.** The verb agrees with the nearer subject, the one after “or” or “nor”: *Neither the teachers nor the principal **has** signed.* *Neither Hana nor you **are** to blame.*
4. **“One of those who …”.** The clause describes the whole group, so its verb is plural: *Priya is one of those students who **are** always early.* In “the only one of those who …”, it describes the one person, so its verb is singular: *Priya is the only one of the students who **is** always early.*
:::

:::tip Put the nearer subject second
*Neither the principal nor the teachers have signed* is correct and sounds more natural than the version with “has”. When you write, you can usually put the plural subject second.
:::

:::example Finding the subject the verb agrees with
Choose the verb: *This is one of the books that (has / have) changed the way I think.*
@step Ask what “that” refers to: not “one”, but “the books”. Several books changed the way I think, and this is one of them.
@step “The books” is plural, so the verb in the clause is plural.
@answer *This is one of the books that **have** changed the way I think.*
:::

## Parallel structure

Items joined in a series, by “and”, “or” or “but”, should share one grammatical form: all nouns, all “-ing” forms, all “that” clauses, all “who” clauses.

*The job involves answering the phone, filing reports and **to greet** visitors* breaks the pattern. The first two duties are “-ing” forms, so the third must be too: *answering, filing and **greeting***.

:::mistake Breaking the pattern late
Long series are where parallelism usually fails, because the writer has forgotten how the list began. *The report found that the river was cleaner, that the fish had returned and **the return of the herons*** starts with two “that” clauses and ends with a noun phrase. Keep the pattern: *and **that the herons were nesting again***.
:::

:::example Making a series parallel
Correct: *The scholarship rewards students who work hard, helping others and who lead by example.*
@step Find the series: three qualities of the students, joined by “and”.
@step Find the form the series sets up: the first and last are “who” clauses, *who work hard* and *who lead by example*.
@step Rewrite the odd one out, *helping others*, in the same form.
@answer *The scholarship rewards students who work hard, who help others and who lead by example.*
:::

## Logical comparisons

A comparison must compare like with like. *The climate of Lagos is warmer than Toronto* compares a climate with a city. Write *warmer than **that of** Toronto*, or *warmer than Toronto’s*. With a plural, use “those”: *The prices in the market are lower than **those** in the shop.*

A comparative is formed once, either with “-er” or with “more”, never with both: *warmer*, not *more warmer*.

:::tip Name both sides
To test a comparison, say aloud what is being compared on each side of “than”. If the answer is “a climate … a city” or “prices … a shop”, the two sides do not match, and the sentence needs “that of” or “those”.
:::

## Modifiers: dangling and misplaced

:::definition Dangling modifier
A **dangling modifier** is an opening phrase that does not describe the subject that follows it. The reader attaches it to that subject anyway, often with absurd results.
:::

The most common kind is a shortened, or **elliptical**, clause: *When only five, Diego’s family moved to Nairobi.* The words *he was* have been left out of *When (he was) only five*, so the phrase attaches to the subject that follows, *Diego’s family*. To fix it, name Diego in the clause, *When Diego was only five, his family moved to Nairobi*, or make him the subject.

:::mistake “Once signed …”
*Once signed, the coach handed Omar the kit* says that the coach was signed. Say who signed what: *Once Omar had signed the form, the coach handed him the kit.*
:::

Placement matters for single words too, above all for **only**. In careful writing, “only” goes right before the word or phrase it limits, and moving it changes the meaning:

- *Only Leila passed the history test.* No one else passed it.
- *Leila passed only the history test.* She failed her other tests.
- *Leila passed the only history test.* There was just one history test.

:::note Clear reference
The same care applies to pronouns. In *Omar told Diego that he had won*, “he” could mean either boy. Repeat the name, or rebuild the sentence: *Omar told Diego, “You have won.”*
:::

:::summary
- “The number of” is singular; “a number of” is plural. After “there”, the verb agrees with the subject that follows.
- With “either … or” and “neither … nor”, the verb agrees with the nearer subject.
- In “one of those who”, the verb is plural; in “the only one of those who”, it is singular.
- Items in a series share one grammatical form, to the end of the list.
- Compare like with like (“than that of Toronto”), and form a comparative only once.
- An opening phrase must describe the subject that follows it; “only” goes right before what it limits; every pronoun needs one clear antecedent.
:::
