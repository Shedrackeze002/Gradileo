Gerard Manley Hopkins wrote “The Windhover” in 1877, but almost no one read it for forty years. Hopkins died in 1889, and his friend Robert Bridges did not publish his poems until 1918. Readers that year met lines like these:

> I caught this morning morning’s minion, king-\
> dom of daylight’s dauphin, dapple-dawn-drawn Falcon, in his riding
>
> — Gerard Manley Hopkins, “The Windhover” (1918)

A line that breaks in the middle of a word, stresses piled on stresses, sounds that tumble over each other: the poem seemed to belong to the new century, not the old one. In this lesson you will see how poets from Hopkins to the Imagists and the Harlem Renaissance reshaped old forms and invented new ones, and how the **sound** of a line can carry its **sense**.

## Reshaping the sonnet

You already know the two classic sonnets. The **Petrarchan** sonnet has an octave (eight lines, usually rhyming ABBAABBA) and a sestet (six lines), with a turn at line 9. The **Shakespearean** sonnet has three quatrains and a closing couplet. Modern poets kept the sonnet and bent it to new purposes.

:::definition Curtal sonnet
A **curtal sonnet** is Hopkins’s shortened sonnet, built in the same proportions as a Petrarchan sonnet: six lines and then four and a half, instead of eight and then six. “Pied Beauty” is one; its last line is just two words.
:::

Edna St. Vincent Millay used the strict Petrarchan sonnet for grief, and the Harlem Renaissance poets Claude McKay and Countee Cullen used the Shakespearean sonnet for anger, love and wonder. A tight, traditional form can hold feelings that pull against each other, and a closing couplet can land a final paradox like a blow.

:::example Finding the form and the turn
Identify the form of this sonnet by Millay, and say what happens at the turn.

> Mindful of you the sodden earth in spring,\
> And all the flowers that in the springtime grow,\
> And dusty roads, and thistles, and the slow\
> Rising of the round moon, all throats that sing\
> The summer through, and each departing wing,\
> And all the nests that the bared branches show,\
> And all winds that in any weather blow,\
> And all the storms that the four seasons bring.
>
> You go no more on your exultant feet\
> Up paths that only mist and morning knew,\
> Or watch the wind, or listen to the beat\
> Of a bird’s wings too high in air to view,—\
> But you were something more than young and sweet\
> And fair,—and the long year remembers you.
>
> — Edna St. Vincent Millay, “Mindful of you the sodden earth in spring” (1917)

@step Mark the rhymes of the first eight lines: *spring*, *sing*, *wing*, *bring* (A) and *grow*, *slow*, *show*, *blow* (B), in the pattern ABBAABBA. That is an octave.
@step Mark the last six: *feet*, *beat*, *sweet* (C) and *knew*, *view*, *you* (D), CDCDCD, with no closing couplet. That is a sestet.
@step Find the turn at line 9. The octave lists everything in the year that is “Mindful of you”; the sestet turns to the person, who will “go no more” on those paths.
@answer It is a Petrarchan sonnet. The octave gathers the whole natural year that remembers the dead person; the sestet turns to the loss itself and ends in consolation: “the long year remembers you.”
:::

## Imagism and free verse

Around 1912 a group of poets in London and America began calling themselves **Imagists**. They wanted poems that presented a thing directly, cut every word that did not do work, and followed the rhythm of a musical phrase rather than the tick of a regular metre.

:::definition Free verse
**Free verse** has no regular metre and usually no rhyme. Its line breaks are chosen, not counted: a line ends where a breath, a picture or a thought ends. Whitman wrote it in long, sweeping lines; the Imagists wrote it in short, sharp ones.
:::

Amy Lowell, who led the Imagists in America, explained her own free verse like this:

> I prefer to call them poems in “unrhymed cadence”, for that conveys their exact meaning to an English ear. They are built upon “organic rhythm”, or the rhythm of the speaking voice with its necessity for breathing, rather than upon a strict metrical system.
>
> — Amy Lowell, preface to *Sword Blades and Poppy Seed* (1914)

Read the opening of one of her poems and notice where each line stops:

> Little cramped words scrawling all over the paper\
> Like draggled fly’s legs,\
> What can you tell of the flaring moon\
> Through the oak leaves?
>
> — Amy Lowell, “The Letter” (1915)

Each line is a breath and a picture. The cramped words of a letter are set against a moonlit night that they cannot capture, and no rhyme or metre gets in the way.

:::

:::tip Form is a choice
Whatever the form, ask why *this* poem takes *this* shape. A sonnet’s tight rhymes can hold back strong feeling; a turn can mark a change of mind; a short, unrhymed line can isolate one image so that nothing distracts from it.
:::

## Sound and sense

Hopkins wrote in what he called **sprung rhythm**: he counted only the stressed syllables in a line and let any number of unstressed ones fall between them. Stresses can crowd together (“dapple-dawn-drawn”), so his lines rush, stop and surge like the falcon they describe. Every poet in this unit uses sound in the same way, to make a line *do* what it *says*.

| Device | What it is | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Alliteration | the same first consonant repeated | “dapple-dawn-drawn” |
| Onomatopoeia | a word that imitates the sound it names | “whirr”, “murmurous” |
| Caesura | a strong pause inside a line | “I fall upon the thorns of life! I bleed!” |
| Enjambment | a sentence running on over a line break | “king- / dom” |
| Repetition | a word or pattern used again | “Quietly shining to the quiet Moon” |

:::example From device to effect
In “Pied Beauty” Hopkins praises God for “dappled things”: anything spotted, streaked or patched. Explain how the sound of this line supports its meaning.

> Landscape plotted and pieced—fold, fallow, and plough;
>
> — Gerard Manley Hopkins, “Pied Beauty” (1918)

@step Name the devices: alliteration on “p” (*plotted*, *pieced*, *plough*) and on “f” (*fold*, *fallow*), and a dash and commas that break the line into pieces.
@step Hear the sound: short, heavily stressed words, each set apart from the next.
@step Connect it to the sense: the fields are a patchwork of sheep-fold, unploughed land and ploughed land, each a different colour.
@answer The repeated sounds and the broken-up list make the line itself a patchwork, like the patched and dappled countryside it describes.
:::

:::mistake Naming the device and stopping
“Hopkins uses alliteration” earns little on its own. Always finish the sentence: the device creates *this* sound, and that sound supports *this* meaning.
:::

:::summary
- Modern poets reshaped the sonnet: Hopkins’s curtal sonnet shrinks it, while Millay, McKay and Cullen used strict sonnets to hold strong and conflicting feelings.
- To identify a sonnet, mark the rhymes, count the lines and find the turn; then ask why the poem takes that shape.
- Free verse has no regular metre or rhyme; its line breaks follow breath, image and thought. The Imagists used short, precise lines; Whitman used long ones.
- Sprung rhythm counts only stresses. Sound devices such as alliteration, onomatopoeia, caesura, enjambment and repetition should always be linked to the meaning they support.
:::
