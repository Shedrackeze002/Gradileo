:::note Content note
This lesson quotes Ida B. Wells’s *Southern Horrors* (1892), which investigated lynching in the American South. The passage quoted is about how newspapers reported those crimes.
:::

In the 1890s, when a Black man in the American South was lynched, the major newspapers usually reported the killing in the words of the mob: the victim was a criminal, and the crowd had delivered rough justice. Ida B. Wells checked those reports one by one and found that many of the accusations were false. She concluded:

> The assertion has been substantiated throughout these pages that the press contains unreliable and doctored reports of lynchings, and one of the most necessary things for the race to do is to get these facts before the public.
>
> — Ida B. Wells, *Southern Horrors* (1892)

Wells saw that the power to tell a story about a group of people is a kind of power over them. The stories a society repeats about a group shape how others treat that group: who is trusted, who is feared, whose suffering counts. In Grade 11 you learned to spot stereotypes in the media. In this lesson you will ask the harder questions: who gets to tell the story, through whose eyes we see it, and how a group can take its own story back.

## Who speaks, and through whose eyes?

:::definition Voice and the gaze
**Voice** is the question of who is allowed to speak for themselves in a media text, and who is only spoken *about*. A group that appears in a story but never speaks has no voice in it.

**The gaze** is the point of view that the camera or the narration takes. When a story is told through the eyes of an outsider, its subjects are seen as that outsider sees them, and what seems normal to the outsider becomes the story’s norm.
:::

To find the voices in a text, count who is quoted, who is interviewed, who narrates and who has the last word. Then count who is shown but not heard. To find the gaze, ask whose experience the camera or the narrator shares: who looks, and who is looked at?

A travel documentary that follows a visiting presenter through a village, describing the people as “charming” and “untouched by time”, takes the outsider’s gaze. The villagers may be on screen in every scene, yet the film tells us more about the visitor’s expectations than about their lives.

A gaze need not belong to one person. It can belong to a whole class, a gender or a culture, whenever a text assumes that its audience shares the outsider’s point of view and treats everyone else as the object of that view.

## Frames, stereotypes and tokens

:::definition Framing, stereotype and tokenism
**Framing** is the choice of what to emphasize when a group appears: showing it over and over in one narrow role, or always in one light. Repeated often enough, a narrow frame becomes a **stereotype**, a fixed and simplified picture of everyone in the group.

**Tokenism** is including one member of a group just to appear fair or diverse, without giving the group any real place or power in the text.
:::

A stereotype can seem flattering (“naturally good at sport”, “always cheerful”) and still do harm, because it reduces individuals to a single trait and sets limits on what others expect of them.

Framing works through repetition. One news story about a teenager who caused an accident is simply news. A year in which teenagers appear almost only in stories about accidents, crime and noise is a frame, and viewers who see it every night come to expect the worst of young people.

:::mistake Tokenism or voice?
Both concern who is included, so they are easy to confuse. If a group is **missing** from a text altogether, or appears without speaking, the problem is **voice**. If one member of the group is **present** but only for show, with no real role or time to speak, the problem is **tokenism**.
:::

## Counter-narratives

:::definition Counter-narrative
A **counter-narrative** is a story told by a group about itself, which challenges the dominant story that others have told about it.
:::

Representation matters because audiences learn from what they see again and again, often without noticing. A group shown only as victims may be pitied but not respected; a group shown only as dangerous may be feared and mistreated; a group that never appears at all may be forgotten when decisions are made. Those who own and fund the media decide which stories are told most often, so representation reflects power, and it also reinforces it.

*Southern Horrors* is a counter-narrative. Wells gathered facts that the major papers had ignored or distorted and published them for the public to judge. She called on Black readers to support their own newspapers, which were, in her words, “the only ones which will print the truth”. Counter-narratives today take many forms: community newspapers, documentaries made by the people they are about, and social media accounts run by a group in its own words.

:::example Analysing representation
> A television film about a family that has just arrived in Canada as refugees is narrated from beginning to end by the volunteer who helped them settle. The family appears in almost every scene, unpacking, shopping and starting school, but none of them ever speaks to the camera.

Analyse how the family is represented, and suggest a fairer approach.
@step Ask who speaks: only the volunteer. The family is shown but never heard.
@step Ask through whose eyes we see: the volunteer’s. The family’s new life is described as the volunteer experienced it.
@step Name the problems: the family has no **voice**, and the film takes an outsider’s **gaze**.
@step Suggest a fix that addresses those problems, not some other one.
@answer The family is spoken *about* but never allowed to speak, and seen only through the volunteer’s gaze. A fairer film would let the family tell their own story, in their own words and, if they wish, their own language, so that the film becomes closer to a counter-narrative.
:::

Use these questions to analyse any media text:

| Ask | The idea it tests |
|---|---|
| Who speaks, and who is only spoken about? | voice |
| Through whose eyes do we see the story? | the gaze |
| In what roles does this group appear, again and again? | framing and stereotype |
| Is one member of a group present only for show? | tokenism |
| Who is telling this group’s story, and is it the group itself? | counter-narrative |

:::tip Making representation fairer
Match the fix to the problem. Missing voices are fixed by letting the group speak; an outsider’s gaze by telling the story from inside the group; a narrow frame by showing the full range of the group’s lives; and tokenism by giving the group a real share of the time, the roles and the decisions.
:::

:::summary
- Representation is a form of power: the stories a society repeats about a group shape how others treat that group.
- **Voice** is who speaks for themselves and who is only spoken about; **the gaze** is whose point of view the camera or narration takes.
- **Framing** a group in one narrow role, again and again, creates a **stereotype**; **tokenism** includes one member for show.
- A **counter-narrative**, like Ida B. Wells’s *Southern Horrors*, is a group’s own story challenging the dominant one.
:::
