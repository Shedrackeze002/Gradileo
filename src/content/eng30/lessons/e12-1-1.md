A short story can sit inside one restless mind, hand the telling from person to person, or stand back like a camera. It can cover nine years in a sentence and stretch two minutes across a page. In Virginia Woolf’s “The Mark on the Wall”, almost nothing happens except thinking:

> How readily our thoughts swarm upon a new object, lifting it a little way, as ants carry a blade of straw so feverishly, and then leave it....
>
> — Virginia Woolf, “The Mark on the Wall” (1917)

In this lesson you will analyse how modern fiction handles **narrative technique** (whose mind we are in, and how the telling moves) and **time** (how a story speeds up, slows down, and moves back and forward).

:::note Read the stories
Every story in this lesson is in the public domain: [“The Dead”](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/2814) by James Joyce, [“Kew Gardens” and “The Mark on the Wall”](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/29220) by Virginia Woolf, [“Bartleby, the Scrivener”](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/11231) by Herman Melville, [“The Beast in the Jungle”](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/1093) by Henry James and [“The Soft-Hearted Sioux”](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/10376) by Zitkala-Ša.
:::

## Whose mind are we in?

:::definition Four narrative techniques
- **Stream of consciousness** follows the flow of one mind: thoughts, memories and sensations drifting by association, often without tidy order.
- **Multiple narrators** hand the story from one teller to another, each telling their own part.
- **Shifting focalization** keeps a third-person narrator but moves the point of view from one character’s perceptions to another’s.
- **External narration** reports only what could be seen and heard, and never enters anyone’s thoughts.
:::

**Focalization** means whose perceptions the narration is filtered through. It is not the same as who narrates: a third-person narrator can still see everything through one character’s eyes. Near the end of Joyce’s “The Dead”, Gabriel watches his wife on the stairs:

> There was grace and mystery in her attitude as if she were a symbol of something. He asked himself what is a woman standing on the stairs in the shadow, listening to distant music, a symbol of.
>
> — James Joyce, “The Dead” (1914)

The narration is filtered through Gabriel. He sees a picture; he does not see what she is feeling. The reader will soon learn what he cannot: the song has made Gretta remember a boy who loved her and died.

:::tip Listen for borrowed words
Joyce opens “The Dead” with “Lily, the caretaker’s daughter, was literally run off her feet.” She was not literally off her feet: “literally” is the word *Lily* would use. A third-person narration that takes on a character’s own idiom brings us close to that character without a single quotation mark.
:::

:::example Naming a technique and its effect
In “Kew Gardens”, Woolf describes a flower bed, then follows a man walking past (“Fifteen years ago I came here with Lily,” he thought), then a snail crossing the soil, then two other people. Name the technique and its effect.
@step The narration stays in the third person but enters different minds in turn, and even the snail’s slow journey.
@step That is shifting focalization: the point of view passes from one perceiver to the next.
@step Ask what it does: each human story is glimpsed for a moment and then left behind, beside the unhurried life of the flower bed.
@answer Shifting focalization. It makes each passer-by’s inner life a brief fragment, and sets human dramas against the slow, indifferent world of the garden.
:::

:::

:::mistake A first-person story is not always stream of consciousness
“I went to the shop and bought bread” is first person, but it is not stream of consciousness. Stream of consciousness imitates the movement of a mind: associations, half-finished thoughts, memories that interrupt the present. Look at how the thoughts move, not only at the pronoun.
:::

## Handling time

Every story chooses how much of its time to show. It can **compress** years into a sentence, **expand** a moment into a page, or step out of order.

:::definition Ways of handling time
- **Compression**: a long stretch of time passes in a few words.
- **Expansion**: a brief moment is drawn out in detail.
- **Analepsis (flashback)**: the telling jumps back to an earlier time.
- **Prolepsis (flash-forward)**: the telling jumps ahead to a later time, or imagines it.
:::

In Zitkala-Ša’s “The Soft-Hearted Sioux”, the narrator’s family plans his future around a winter fire. The next section begins:

> Nine winters’ snows had buried deep that night when my old grandmother, together with my father and mother, designed my future with the glow of a camp fire upon it.
>
> — Zitkala-Ša, “The Soft-Hearted Sioux” (1901)

Nine years at a mission school pass in one sentence. The story skips the long change and lands us at its result: a young man who walks into his father’s village “a stranger”.

Lying awake at the end of “The Dead”, Gabriel looks ahead:

> Soon, perhaps, he would be sitting in that same drawing-room, dressed in black, his silk hat on his knees. The blinds would be drawn down and Aunt Kate would be sitting beside him, crying and blowing her nose and telling him how Julia had died.
>
> — James Joyce, “The Dead” (1914)

This is prolepsis: a funeral that has not happened yet. It shows how death has entered Gabriel’s thoughts, and it prepares the story’s final vision of the snow falling “upon all the living and the dead”.

:::note Time and meaning
In Henry James’s “The Beast in the Jungle”, whole years pass between the chapters while John Marcher waits for something rare to happen to him. The story’s handling of time mirrors his life: the years slip by, unlived, while he watches for “the Beast”.
:::

:::summary
- **Stream of consciousness** follows one mind’s associations; **multiple narrators** take turns; **shifting focalization** moves the view between characters; **external narration** stays outside every mind.
- Focalization is whose eyes we see through, which can differ from who tells the story.
- Stories **compress** and **expand** time, and move back (**analepsis**) and forward (**prolepsis**).
- Always ask what a technique does: what it lets the reader see, what it hides, and how it shapes our sense of the characters.
:::
