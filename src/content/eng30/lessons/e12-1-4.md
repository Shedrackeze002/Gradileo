Anton Chekhov’s “The Lady with the Dog” follows two lovers, each married to someone else, who meet in secret in Moscow. It does not end with a wedding, a parting or a scandal. It ends like this:

> And it seemed as though in a little while the solution would be found, and then a new and splendid life would begin; and it was clear to both of them that they had still a long, long road before them, and that the most complicated and difficult part of it was only just beginning.
>
> — Anton Chekhov (tr. Constance Garnett), “The Lady with the Dog” (1899)

The solution only “seemed” near, and the hardest part is “only just beginning”. Chekhov leaves the lovers, and the reader, on the road. In this lesson you will analyse **deliberate ambiguity**, where a story supports more than one reading on purpose, and the different kinds of **ending** a story can choose.

## Deliberate ambiguity

:::definition Ambiguity
A passage or story is **ambiguous** when it supports more than one reading and the text does not settle which is right. **Deliberate ambiguity** is a choice: the writer gives real evidence on more than one side and leaves the question open, so the reader has to hold both possibilities at once.
:::

Ambiguity is not the same as vagueness. A vague story simply leaves things unclear; an ambiguous one gives precise details that point in different directions. At the end of Joyce’s “The Dead”, Gabriel lies awake beside Gretta after learning that a boy once died for love of her. Two moments sit side by side:

> Generous tears filled Gabriel’s eyes. He had never felt like that himself towards any woman but he knew that such a feeling must be love.
>
> — James Joyce, “The Dead” (1914)

> His own identity was fading out into a grey impalpable world: the solid world itself which these dead had one time reared and lived in was dissolving and dwindling.
>
> — James Joyce, “The Dead” (1914)

The first suggests a man made more generous by what he has learned. The second suggests a self dissolving into a world of the dead. Is the ending hopeful or bleak? Joyce gives evidence for both.

:::check e12-ambiguity 1
:::

:::mistake Two ways to mishandle ambiguity
- **Choosing a side and ignoring the rest**: “The ending is hopeful because of the generous tears” leaves out the fading identity. A strong reading accounts for the evidence against it.
- **Treating it as a free-for-all**: ambiguity does not mean any reading will do. “Gabriel plans to leave Gretta” has no support in the text at all.
:::

:::example Weighing both sides
At the end of Zitkala-Ša’s “The Soft-Hearted Sioux”, the narrator waits in a cell to be hanged. He asks, “Will the loving Jesus grant me pardon and give my soul a soothing sleep? or will my warrior father greet me and receive me as his son?” and then says, “My heart is strong. My face is calm.” Does he die at peace?
@step Gather the evidence for peace: the short, steady sentences, “strong”, “calm”, and eyes “dry and eager for new scenes”.
@step Gather the evidence against: he still does not know which world, the mission’s or his father’s, will receive him.
@step Write a reading that holds both.
@answer “He faces death with calm courage, yet the question of who will greet him shows that he dies still divided between the two worlds that shaped him.”
:::

:::check e12-ambiguity 2
:::

:::tip Hold both sides in one sentence
Words such as *yet*, *while* and *even as* let one sentence carry both readings: “Gabriel grows more generous even as his old sense of himself dissolves.” A sentence like that shows that you have seen the ambiguity and chosen to explain it rather than flatten it.
:::

## How stories end

:::definition Four kinds of ending
- A **closed** ending settles the main conflict and makes its meaning clear.
- An **open** ending leaves the main conflict unresolved, for the reader to imagine.
- An **ironic** ending reverses what the characters, or the reader, expected.
- An **epiphanic** ending turns on a sudden moment in which a character sees a truth they had missed.
:::

Charles W. Chesnutt’s “The Wife of His Youth” has a closed ending. Mr. Ryder asks his guests what a man should do when the wife of his youth finds him again; they answer that he should acknowledge her, and he presents the old woman to them as his wife. The dilemma is settled, and its meaning is plain.

Edith Wharton’s “The Other Two” ends very differently. Waythorn has been uneasy about his wife’s two former husbands. In the last scene, both of them are in his library when she comes in and offers tea:

> She glanced about for Waythorn, and he took the third cup with a laugh.
>
> — Edith Wharton, “The Other Two” (1904)

The man who wanted his wife’s past kept at a distance ends up as simply the third of her husbands, drinking tea with the other two. The outcome reverses what he wanted, so the ending is ironic, and his laugh shows that he has accepted it.

:::check e12-endings 1
:::

An ending can be more than one kind at once. At May Bartram’s grave, John Marcher in “The Beast in the Jungle” suddenly sees that “he had been the man of his time, *the* man, to whom nothing on earth was to have happened.” That is an epiphany, and it is also a bitter irony: the rare fate he spent his life waiting for was the waiting itself.

:::note An ending that deflates
Woolf’s “The Mark on the Wall” spends pages drifting through thoughts set off by a small dark mark. Then someone says, “I don’t see why we should have a snail on our wall,” and the story ends: “Ah, the mark on the wall! It was a snail.” After all that speculation, the answer is tiny and ordinary. The anticlimax suggests that the journey of thought mattered more than the answer.
:::

:::check e12-endings 2
:::

:::mistake An open ending is not an unfinished story
Writers choose open endings on purpose. Melville never tells us why Bartleby “would prefer not to”; the narrator passes on one “vague report” about Bartleby’s past and admits he “could never ascertain” whether it was true. Ask what the openness makes the reader do: here, it leaves us as puzzled as the narrator, and as unable to help.
:::

:::check e12-endings 3
:::

:::summary
- **Deliberate ambiguity** gives real evidence for more than one reading; a strong reading accounts for all of it, and does not treat ambiguity as a licence for any reading at all.
- Endings can be **closed**, **open**, **ironic** or **epiphanic**, and one ending can be more than one of these.
- Ask what an ending does: what it settles, what it leaves open, and how it changes the meaning of everything before it.
:::
