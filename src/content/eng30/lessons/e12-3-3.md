:::note Spoiler
This lesson discusses the ending of *Hamlet*: the poisoned fencing match in which Gertrude, Laertes, Claudius and Hamlet all die.
:::

Hamlet has promised the Ghost revenge, yet by the end of Act 2 the King is still alive and Hamlet is still unsure. Then a company of actors arrives at the castle, and he sees a way to find the truth:

> The play’s the thing\
> Wherein I’ll catch the conscience of the King.
>
> — Hamlet, *Hamlet*, Act 2, Scene 2

*Hamlet* is a play about delay, and Shakespeare fills that delay with drama: a play inside the play, a ghost, a prince who may or may not be mad, and an audience who often knows more than the characters do. In this lesson you will recognize the play’s dramatic devices, explain what they do, and explain its ironies.

## Kinds of speech

:::definition Who hears the words?
- A **soliloquy** is a speech in which a character alone on stage speaks their thoughts aloud.
- An **aside** is a short remark to the audience that the other characters on stage do not hear. In the script it is marked “Aside”.
- A **monologue** is a long speech by one character, spoken to other characters on stage.
- **Dialogue** is a conversation between two or more characters, speaking in turn.
:::

An aside can change everything we know. Polonius has just remarked that people often hide wickedness behind a show of devotion, and the King, unheard by anyone on stage, answers:

> **Polonius.** We are oft to blame in this,\
> ’Tis too much prov’d, that with devotion’s visage\
> And pious action we do sugar o’er\
> The devil himself.
>
> **Claudius.** [*Aside.*] O ’tis too true!\
> How smart a lash that speech doth give my conscience!\
> The harlot’s cheek, beautied with plastering art,\
> Is not more ugly to the thing that helps it\
> Than is my deed to my most painted word.\
> O heavy burden!
>
> — *Hamlet*, Act 3, Scene 1

This is the first time we hear Claudius himself admit his guilt. From now on the audience knows that the Ghost spoke the truth, while Hamlet is still waiting for his proof.

:::mistake A speech is not a soliloquy just because it is long
A soliloquy is spoken alone. When Gertrude describes Ophelia’s drowning, she speaks at length, but to Claudius and Laertes, so her speech is a monologue. Ask who is on stage and who is meant to hear.
:::

## The devices of Hamlet

| Device | What it is | In *Hamlet* |
|---|---|---|
| Play within a play | a performance staged by characters inside the play | the actors perform *The Murder of Gonzago*, in which a man poisons a sleeping king in his garden, to test Claudius |
| The Ghost | a supernatural figure who drives the plot | it demands revenge, but Hamlet cannot be sure whether it is honest or a devil |
| Feigned madness | a character pretending to be mad | Hamlet decides “To put an antic disposition on”, so that his strange words can hide his purpose |
| Foil | a character who contrasts with another | Laertes and Fortinbras, who act at once to avenge their fathers while Hamlet hesitates |
| Pun | a play on words | “I am too much i’ the sun”: too much called the King’s son |
| Comic relief | a funny scene that eases the tension of a tragedy | the joking gravediggers just before Ophelia’s funeral |
| Foreshadowing | a hint of something to come | before the duel, Hamlet feels “how ill all’s here about my heart” |
| Stage direction | an unspoken instruction in the script | “Aside”, “Dies”, or Gertrude “Scattering flowers” on Ophelia’s grave |

The devices and the delay are bound together. The play within the play is Hamlet’s reason for waiting: he will not kill a king on the word of a spirit that “May be the devil”. His madness may be a mask that gives him time and freedom, or something more than a mask; it is never easy to tell. And his foils keep the question of his delay before us, because every time Laertes or Fortinbras acts, Hamlet is measured against them. When Hamlet tells his mother that he is “not in madness” at all, we have to decide whether to believe him:

> That I essentially am not in madness,\
> But mad in craft.
>
> — Hamlet, *Hamlet*, Act 3, Scene 4

:::tip Ask what the device does
Naming a device is only the first step. Then ask what it does: what the audience learns, feels or understands because of it. The play within the play does not just entertain the court; it gives Hamlet his proof and shows the King’s guilt to everyone watching.
:::

## Irony

:::definition Three kinds of irony
- **Dramatic irony**: the audience knows something important that a character does not.
- **Verbal irony**: a speaker says something different from, or opposite to, what is meant.
- **Situational irony**: what happens is the opposite of what was intended or expected.
:::

The prayer scene is built on irony. Hamlet finds Claudius on his knees and decides not to kill him, because a man killed at prayer might go to heaven. But we have just heard Claudius say “Pray can I not”, and as soon as Hamlet has gone, the King rises:

> My words fly up, my thoughts remain below.\
> Words without thoughts never to heaven go.
>
> — Claudius, *Hamlet*, Act 3, Scene 3

| Irony | Example | What makes it ironic |
|---|---|---|
| Dramatic | Hamlet spares the King “now he is praying” | the audience knows that Claudius cannot truly pray |
| Verbal | “Thrift, thrift, Horatio!” | Hamlet praises the saving of money but means the opposite |
| Situational | Laertes is “justly kill’d with mine own treachery” | the poisoned sword meant for Hamlet kills Laertes too |

:::example Naming an irony and its effect
Hamlet suddenly asks Ophelia, “Where’s your father?” She answers, “At home, my lord.” What kind of irony is this, and what is its effect?
@step Find the gap: the audience knows that Polonius is hiding nearby with the King, watching, so her answer is not true.
@step Name it: Ophelia is not being ironic herself; she is telling a lie her father needs. The irony lies in what the audience knows and the characters do not, so it is dramatic irony.
@step Explain the effect: we watch with growing tension, knowing that Ophelia is caught between her father and Hamlet, and that Hamlet may already suspect that they are being watched.
@answer It is dramatic irony. Because the audience knows that Polonius and the King are listening, the question turns a private conversation into a test that Ophelia fails, and it raises the tension of the whole scene.
:::

:::summary
- A soliloquy is spoken alone, an aside is heard only by the audience, a monologue is a long speech to others, and dialogue is a conversation.
- *Hamlet*’s devices include the play within the play, the Ghost, feigned madness, foils, puns, comic relief, foreshadowing and stage directions. Always ask what a device does as well as what it is.
- Dramatic irony is a gap between what the audience knows and what a character knows; verbal irony says the opposite of what is meant; situational irony turns an intention upside down, as when the plotters fall into their own traps.
:::
