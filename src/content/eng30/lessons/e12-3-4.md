> **Hamlet.** But I do prophesy th’election lights\
> On Fortinbras. He has my dying voice.\

>
> **Horatio.** Now cracks a noble heart. Good night, sweet prince,\
> And flights of angels sing thee to thy rest.

> — *Hamlet*, Act 5, Scene 2

By the end of the play eight of its characters are dead, and Hamlet asks Horatio to live on “To tell my story”. What does that story mean? In this lesson you will state and support the themes of *Hamlet*, and weigh the different ways readers have interpreted it.

## Revenge tragedy

:::definition Revenge tragedy
A **revenge tragedy** is a kind of play, popular in Shakespeare’s time, in which a person sets out to avenge a terrible wrong, often at the urging of a ghost, and which ends in bloodshed that destroys the avenger as well as the guilty.
:::

*Hamlet* has all the parts: a murder, a ghost, a delay, a madness, a play, and a stage full of bodies. But its hero questions revenge at every turn. He asks whether the Ghost is honest, whether killing a man at prayer is revenge at all, and whether thinking makes cowards of us. The play keeps the shape of a revenge tragedy while asking what revenge is worth.

## The big ideas

| Topic | What the play suggests | A line that shows it |
|---|---|---|
| Thought and action | Thinking too long about a deed can weaken the will to act. | “Thus conscience does make cowards of us all” (Hamlet, Act 3, Scene 1) |
| Corruption | Corruption at the top of a state spreads to everyone around it. | “Something is rotten in the state of Denmark.” (Marcellus, Act 1, Scene 4) |
| Appearance and reality | People hide their true natures behind false appearances. | “That one may smile, and smile, and be a villain!” (Hamlet, Act 1, Scene 5) |
| Death | Death comes to everyone, and erases the differences between great and humble. | “Imperious Caesar, dead and turn’d to clay” (Hamlet, Act 5, Scene 1) |
| Revenge | Revenge can destroy the avenger as surely as the guilty. | “I am justly kill’d with mine own treachery.” (Laertes, Act 5, Scene 2) |
| Providence | Accepting what one cannot control can bring peace and readiness. | “There’s a divinity that shapes our ends” (Hamlet, Act 5, Scene 2) |

:::definition Theme statement
A **theme statement** is a complete sentence that says what the play suggests about life or people in general. A topic (“death”) is not a theme, and neither is a summary of the plot. A strong theme statement is general (no character names), supported by the play, and free of orders (“Never seek revenge”) and absolutes (“Avengers always die”).
:::

:::mistake A character’s opinion is not the play’s theme
When Hamlet cries “Frailty, thy name is woman!”, he is speaking in grief and disgust at his mother’s marriage. That is his view at one moment, not a message that the whole play endorses. Before you build a theme on a single line, ask who says it, when, and whether the rest of the play agrees.
:::

:::example Choosing the best evidence
Claim: “Hamlet blames himself for being slow to take his revenge.” Which quotation proves it better: “To be, or not to be, that is the question” (Act 3, Scene 1) or “How all occasions do inform against me, / And spur my dull revenge” (Act 4, Scene 4)?
@step Check what the first line is about: whether to go on living or not. It is famous, but it never mentions revenge.
@step Check the second: everything Hamlet sees seems to accuse him and to “spur” a revenge that he himself calls “dull”, that is, sluggish.
@answer The second quotation. It names his revenge and blames him for its slowness, so it proves the claim directly. “To be, or not to be” would suit a claim about life and death instead.
:::

## Weighing interpretations

:::definition Interpretation
An **interpretation**, or reading, is an argument about what a play means or why a character acts as they do. Evidence **supports** a reading when it makes the reading more likely. Evidence **complicates** a reading when it gives a reason to doubt it, without simply disproving it.
:::

*Hamlet* leaves many questions open, and readers have argued about them for more than four centuries. Here are three, with evidence on each side:

| The question | Evidence for one reading | Evidence that complicates it |
|---|---|---|
| Is Hamlet’s madness only an act? | He plans “To put an antic disposition on”, and tells his mother he is “mad in craft”. | He tells Laertes that “His madness” did the wrong, and he has “lost all my mirth” without knowing why. |
| Does Hamlet delay because he thinks too much? | Resolution is “sicklied o’er with the pale cast of thought”. | He kills Polonius in an instant, and he has good reason to test the Ghost before he acts. |
| Did Gertrude know nothing of the murder? | Her shocked “As kill a king?”, and the Ghost’s command to “leave her to heaven”. | She sees “black and grained spots” on her soul, and speaks of her “sick soul” and her “guilt”. |

Notice that the complicating evidence does not settle the question the other way. Gertrude’s guilt may be about her hasty marriage rather than any murder; Hamlet’s claim of madness may be an honest confession or a convenient excuse. That is why these questions are still debated.

:::tip Weigh, don’t settle
The aim is not to prove that one reading is “correct” and every other reading wrong. A strong interpretation states a view, supports it with evidence, and deals honestly with the evidence against it. Be wary of any argument that lets one quotation cancel all the others, or that refuses to weigh the text at all.
:::

:::example Weighing a reading
Some readers argue that Hamlet’s madness is only an act. Weigh this reading against the evidence.
@step Find the strongest support: right after meeting the Ghost, Hamlet warns his friends that he may “put an antic disposition on”, and later he tells his mother that he is “not in madness, / But mad in craft”.
@step Face the complications: at Ophelia’s grave he leaps in after Laertes and grapples with him, and before the duel he tells Laertes that “His madness is poor Hamlet’s enemy”.
@step Weigh them: the plan comes before any madness appears, so the act seems deliberate. But grief and shock may carry him beyond his act at times, and his apology may be honest, or may be a way of excusing what he did.
@answer The evidence suggests that Hamlet’s madness begins as a deliberate act, a mask that hides his purpose. Yet the play leaves room to think that, at moments of grief and anger, the mask and the man are hard to tell apart.
:::

:::summary
- *Hamlet* is a revenge tragedy that questions revenge: its hero doubts the Ghost, the act, and himself.
- Its themes include thought and action, corruption, appearance and reality, death, revenge and providence. State a theme as a complete, general sentence, and support it with the quotation that proves it most directly.
- An interpretation is an argued reading. Evidence can support it or complicate it; a strong reading weighs both and does not pretend that the question is closed.
:::
