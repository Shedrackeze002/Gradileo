Two writers can describe the same rainy street and make it feel completely different. The difference is **style**, and two of its main ingredients are **diction** (word choice) and **syntax** (sentence structure). In this lesson you will learn to describe both and explain their effects.

## Diction

:::definition Diction
**Diction** is a writer’s choice of words. It can be formal or colloquial, plain or ornate, gentle or harsh.
:::

| Kind of diction | Example | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| **Formal** | The committee has postponed its decision. | serious, official, distant |
| **Colloquial** (informal) | The committee’s gone and put it off again. | natural, chatty, like real speech |
| **Concrete** | The radiator clanked and hissed. | vivid and specific |
| **Abstract** | The atmosphere was uncomfortable. | general and vague |

Words carry **connotations**, the feelings and associations beyond their dictionary meaning. A writer who says “the sky bled” rather than “the sky turned red” brings pain and danger into an image that is usually peaceful.

:::example Reading diction
@step “Damp clawed at the walls. Something had gnawed the skirting boards, and the air choked with dust.”
@step Look at the verbs: *clawed*, *gnawed*, *choked*. All of them suggest attack, hunger or suffocation.
@answer The diction makes the room feel grim and threatening, almost as if it were alive and hostile.
:::

## Syntax

:::definition Syntax
**Syntax** is the way words are arranged into sentences: their length, word order and structure.
:::

Syntax creates rhythm, and rhythm shapes how we feel.

- **A short sentence after long ones** hits hard and draws attention to itself.
- **A long sentence full of commas and “and”** can feel breathless, rushed or chaotic.
- **A fragment** (a group of words punctuated as a sentence but missing a main clause) is usually an error, but writers use it deliberately for shock or emphasis.
- **Inverted word order**, as in “Silent was the house”, puts weight on the first word and sounds formal.

:::example Syntax as a shock
@step “We spent the morning on the beach, building towers and digging moats and chasing the waves as they slid back down the sand. Then the siren.”
@step The first sentence is long and flowing, like a lazy, happy morning.
@step The second is a two-word fragment.
@answer The sudden short fragment shatters the peaceful mood, just as the siren shatters the morning.
:::

:::mistake Connect syntax to meaning
“The writer uses short sentences” is an observation, not analysis. Say what the short sentences do: create tension, mimic a heartbeat, show shock.
:::

:::summary
- **Diction** is word choice: formal or colloquial, concrete or abstract, with powerful **connotations**.
- **Syntax** is sentence structure: length, order and shape.
- Short sentences and fragments create impact; long sentences can feel breathless or calm.
- Always link diction and syntax to their effect on tone and mood.
:::
