Most of the time, making verbs and pronouns agree is easy. The hard cases come when the real subject is hidden or the pronoun could point in two directions. In this lesson you will handle **subject-verb agreement** in tricky sentences and make **pronouns** agree with clear antecedents.

## Subject-verb agreement in hard cases

A singular subject takes a singular verb, and a plural subject takes a plural verb. The trick is to find the **real subject**.

| Hard case | Rule | Example |
|---|---|---|
| a phrase between subject and verb | ignore the phrase; find the subject | The **box** of chocolates **is** on the table. |
| *each*, *every*, *either*, *neither* | singular | **Each** of the players **has** a shirt. |
| *either … or*, *neither … nor* | agree with the **nearer** subject | Neither the teacher nor the **students are** ready. |
| *there is / there are* | agree with what comes after | There **are** three **reasons**. |
| nouns ending in -s that are singular | singular | **Mathematics is** my strongest subject. |
| collective nouns (*team*, *class*, *committee*) | usually singular when the group acts as one | The **team is** celebrating its victory. |

:::example Finding the real subject
@step “The list of names \_\_\_ on the door.”
@step Cross out the phrase “of names”. What is on the door? The **list**.
@step *List* is singular.
@answer “The list of names **is** on the door.”
:::

:::mistake Be consistent with collective nouns
British English often allows a plural verb when the members of a group act separately: “The team are arguing among themselves.” Whichever you choose, keep the verb and the pronoun consistent. “The team is celebrating their victory” mixes the two.
:::

## Pronoun agreement and reference

:::definition Antecedent
An **antecedent** is the noun that a pronoun refers back to. In “Leo lost his bag”, *Leo* is the antecedent of *his*.
:::

A pronoun must **agree** with its antecedent and **point clearly** to it.

- **Unclear:** When Lena met Ruth, she was nervous. (Who was nervous?)
- **Clear:** When Lena met Ruth, Lena was nervous.

For words such as *everyone* and *somebody*, singular *they* is widely accepted: “Everyone should bring **their** own lunch.” *His or her* is also correct, but it can sound stiff.

A group acting as one takes *its*: “The committee announced **its** decision.” Remember that *it’s* means *it is*.

After a preposition such as *between*, *for* or *with*, use the object pronoun: “between you and **me**“, not “between you and I”.

:::summary
- Find the **real subject**; ignore phrases such as “of the players”.
- *Each* and *every* are singular; with *neither … nor*, agree with the nearer subject.
- A pronoun must agree with its **antecedent** and point to it clearly; use *me* after a preposition.
:::
