An argument can be full of feeling and still be badly reasoned. In this lesson you will learn the two main kinds of reasoning, **deductive** and **inductive**, judge whether evidence is strong enough, and spot four more **fallacies**.

## Deductive and inductive reasoning

:::definition Premise
A **premise** is a statement that an argument assumes or offers as a reason. The conclusion is what the premises are meant to prove.
:::

| Kind | How it works | Example | How strong? |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Deductive** | from a general rule to a conclusion about a case | All mammals breathe air. Whales are mammals. So whales breathe air. | **certain**, if the premises are true |
| **Inductive** | from examples to a general conclusion | Every swan I have seen is white. So swans are probably white. | only **probable**: black swans exist |

Most everyday arguments are inductive, so the key question is: **is there enough evidence?**

:::example Testing an inductive argument
@step “We asked ten customers in our café, and eight prefer our coffee. So most people in the town prefer our coffee.”
@step The evidence is ten customers, all of whom chose to visit this café.
@step Ten is too few, and café regulars are not a fair sample of the whole town.
@answer The conclusion is weak: the evidence is too small and not representative enough to support a claim about the whole town.
:::

A deductive argument can also fail if a **premise** is false. “All birds can fly; penguins are birds; so penguins can fly” follows logically, but the first premise is wrong, so the conclusion is too.

## More fallacies

In Grade 8 you met the bandwagon, the false choice, the hasty generalization and the attack on the person. Here are four more.

| Fallacy | What it does | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Straw man** | distorts the other side into an extreme version that is easy to attack | “My opponent wants to reduce homework. So he thinks students should never learn anything at all.” |
| **Slippery slope** | claims one small step will lead, without evidence, to an extreme result | “If we let students choose one book, soon there will be no school at all.” |
| **Circular reasoning** | uses the claim as its own proof | “This website is trustworthy because it says it is the most reliable site online.” |
| **False appeal to authority** | relies on someone who is not an expert on the subject | “A famous footballer says this toothpaste is the best, so it must be.” |

:::example Why a straw man is unfair
@step The real argument: homework should be reduced.
@step The straw man: students should never learn anything.
@step The speaker attacks the second, which almost no one believes.
@answer Defeating a weaker, distorted version proves nothing about the real argument.
:::

:::mistake Experts can be cited
Quoting an expert is not a fallacy when they are an expert *on that subject*. A dentist’s view on toothpaste is relevant evidence; a footballer’s is not.
:::

:::summary
- **Deductive** reasoning goes from a rule to a certain conclusion; **inductive** reasoning goes from examples to a probable one.
- Check that premises are true and that evidence is enough and representative.
- Fallacies: **straw man**, **slippery slope**, **circular reasoning** and **false appeal to authority**.
:::
