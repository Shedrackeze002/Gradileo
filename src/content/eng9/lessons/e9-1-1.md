A short story can be read in one sitting, but a novel asks readers to stay with it for days or weeks. To keep them, novelists make careful choices about **structure** (the order and shape in which the story is told) and **pacing** (how fast it moves). In this lesson you will learn to name these choices and explain their effects.

## How a novel is structured

| Structure | What it is | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| **Chronological order** | events told in the order they happen | clear and easy to follow |
| **Flashback** | a jump back to an earlier time | reveals the past that explains the present |
| **Parallel plots** | two or more storylines running side by side, often in different times or places | invites readers to compare them |
| **Multiple narrators** | different chapters told by different characters | shows how differently people see the same events |

Some novels also begin at the end. A book that opens with a funeral and then goes back to tell the year before it replaces the question “What will happen?” with “How did it come to this?” Readers already know the ending, so they read for causes and meaning instead of surprises.

:::example Naming a structure
@step Chapter 1 is set in 1916, as a soldier writes a letter home from the trenches.
@step Chapter 2 is set today, as his great-granddaughter finds that letter in an attic.
@step The chapters keep alternating between the two times.
@answer This is a **parallel plot** structure. The two stories mirror each other, so readers keep comparing the past and the present.
:::

:::mistake Structure is a choice
Do not just say what the structure is. Ask why the writer chose it. A flashback placed just before a big decision, for example, can show us exactly what is driving that decision.
:::

## Pacing

:::definition Pacing
**Pacing** is the speed at which a story unfolds for the reader. Writers speed up and slow down to control tension and focus.
:::

| To speed up | To slow down |
|---|---|
| short sentences and paragraphs | long sentences and detailed description |
| quick action and dialogue | a character’s thoughts and memories |
| summary (“Three years passed.”) | stretching a single moment over a page |
| a **cliffhanger** at the end of a chapter | a quiet chapter after a dramatic one |

Writers spend the most words on what matters most. A novel might cover ten years in a paragraph and then spend a whole chapter on one afternoon, which tells you the afternoon is where the story really lies.

:::example Slowing time
@step “The ball rose. It hung against the white sky for what felt like an hour. I could hear my own breath, the creak of the bench, a dog somewhere far away. Then it began to fall.”
@step In real life this takes about two seconds.
@step The writer stretches it with sensory details, so we wait with the narrator.
@answer Slowing the pace at the key moment builds suspense and shows how much it matters.
:::

Thrillers often alternate fast chase scenes with quiet ones. The quiet scenes let tension build again, so the next burst of action hits harder.

:::summary
- Structures include **chronological order**, **flashbacks**, **parallel plots** and **multiple narrators**.
- Starting at the end turns “what happens?” into “how and why?”
- **Pacing** is controlled by sentence length, action, detail and summary.
- Writers slow down at important moments and use **cliffhangers** to pull readers on.
:::
