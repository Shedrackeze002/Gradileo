*The Tempest* asks big questions. Who has the right to rule? What does it mean to be free? Is revenge or mercy the stronger choice? In this lesson you will explore the play’s main **themes** and explain what its most famous **lines** reveal.

## The themes

**Power and the right to rule.** Almost every character struggles over power. Antonio stole Prospero’s dukedom; Antonio and Sebastian plot to kill Alonso; Caliban and Stephano plot to kill Prospero. Prospero himself rules the island absolutely, through magic.

**Freedom.** Ariel longs to be free, and keeps reminding Prospero of his promise. Caliban is enslaved and resents it. Even Prospero, in the epilogue, asks the audience to set him free.

**Colonialism.** Many modern readers link the play to the age of European exploration, when Shakespeare was writing. A newcomer, Prospero, takes an island and rules its native, Caliban, raising questions about who owns land and who has the right to rule.

**Forgiveness.** With all his enemies at his mercy, Prospero decides:

> The rarer action is\
> In virtue than in vengeance.
>
> — William Shakespeare, *The Tempest*, Act 5, Scene 1

He means that mercy is a rarer and nobler act than revenge. He forgives his enemies instead of punishing them.

:::example Reading the ending
@step Prospero breaks his staff and drowns his book, giving up the magic that gave him power.
@step He frees Ariel and prepares to return to ordinary life as Duke of Milan.
@answer Giving up his magic suggests that he accepts the limits of power. Because this is one of Shakespeare’s last plays, many readers also see Prospero’s farewell to his “art” as Shakespeare’s own farewell to the stage.
:::

## Key lines

| Line | Speaker and scene | What it reveals |
|---|---|---|
| “You taught me language, and my profit on’t / Is, I know how to curse.” | Caliban, 1.2 | his bitterness: the only use he has found for his masters’ language is to curse them |
| “Hell is empty, / And all the devils are here.” | Ferdinand’s cry during the storm, as Ariel reports it, 1.2 | the terror of the shipwreck |
| “We are such stuff / As dreams are made on, and our little life / Is rounded with a sleep.” | Prospero, 4.1 | life is brief and unreal, like a dream that ends in sleep |
| “O brave new world, / That has such people in’t!” | Miranda, 5.1 | her wonder at seeing other people, for the first time |
| “‘Tis new to thee.” | Prospero’s reply, 5.1 | he knows these men are not as good as she thinks |
| “This thing of darkness I / Acknowledge mine.” | Prospero, of Caliban, 5.1 | he accepts some responsibility for Caliban, and perhaps for his own darker side |

:::example Reading a line in context
@step Miranda sees the shipwrecked lords and cries, “O brave new world, / That has such people in’t!” Here *brave* means fine or splendid.
@step Among the people she admires are Antonio and Sebastian, who plotted murder.
@step Prospero answers dryly: “‘Tis new to thee.”
@answer Her wonder is moving, but his reply reminds the audience that the “new world” is full of old wickedness. The exchange sets innocence beside experience.
:::

:::mistake Who, when, to whom
A quotation means more in context. Before you explain a line, ask who is speaking, at what moment, and to whom. Caliban’s curse, for example, is spoken to the very people who taught him language.
