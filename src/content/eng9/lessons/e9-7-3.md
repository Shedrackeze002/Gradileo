In an exam, the clock is part of the task. A clear method helps you answer the question that was actually asked, and finish. In this lesson you will **analyse an essay prompt** and **plan** under time limits.

## Reading the prompt

The most costly mistake in a timed essay is misreading the question. Read it carefully and underline two things: the **command word** and the **focus**.

| Command word | What it asks |
|---|---|
| **Analyse** | examine how something works and why |
| **Explain** | make clear how or why something happens |
| **Compare** | look at similarities, and often differences too |
| **Contrast** | look at differences |
| **To what extent** | make a judgement that weighs evidence on both sides |
| **How does … present** | focus on the writer’s methods and their effects |

:::example Unpicking a prompt
@step Prompt: “How does Shakespeare present the theme of power in *The Tempest*?”
@step Command words: “How does … present”. These ask about **methods**, such as language, structure and staging.
@step Focus: “the theme of power”.
@answer The essay must explain Shakespeare’s methods and what they show about power. Retelling the plot, or writing about Shakespeare’s life, would not answer it.
:::

## Planning and managing time

A few minutes of planning keeps the essay focused and saves time later. In a 45-minute essay, about **5 to 10 minutes** of planning is sensible.

A quick plan is a map, not a draft. It needs:

1. your **thesis** in one sentence;
2. the **main point** of each body paragraph;
3. the **key evidence** for each point.

A simple timetable for 45 minutes might be: plan for 5 to 10 minutes, write for about 30, and check for the last 5.

:::mistake Finish the argument
If time is running out with a paragraph still unwritten, write a brief version of the point and a short conclusion. A complete argument, even if brief, is stronger than an unfinished one.
:::

Save a few minutes at the end to proofread and to check that every paragraph answers the prompt.
