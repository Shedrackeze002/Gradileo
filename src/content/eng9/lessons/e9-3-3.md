Shakespeare’s stage had almost no scenery. To create a magical island, he relied on **language**, **music** and the actors’ voices. In this lesson you will look at the speeches that let us into characters’ minds (**soliloquies**, **asides** and the **epilogue**) and at how Shakespeare’s verse, prose and songs build the world of the play.

## Soliloquies, asides and the epilogue

| Device | What it is | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| **Soliloquy** | a speech in which a character alone on stage speaks their thoughts aloud | lets the audience hear private thoughts and decisions |
| **Aside** | a line spoken to the audience that other characters do not hear | shares secrets and creates dramatic irony |
| **Epilogue** | a speech to the audience at the end of the play | steps outside the story to close it |

In Act 5, Prospero makes a solemn speech in which he calls on the spirits who have helped him, and then gives up his power.

> I’ll break my staff,\
> Bury it certain fathoms in the earth,\
> And deeper than did ever plummet sound\
> I’ll drown my book.
>
> — William Shakespeare, *The Tempest*, Act 5, Scene 1

:::example Reading Prospero’s farewell
@step His **staff** and his **book** are the sources of his magic.
@step He will bury the staff deep in the earth, and drown the book deeper than any *plummet* (a weight on a line for measuring depth) has ever reached.
@step The images are of depths that can never be reached again.
@answer Prospero is giving up his magic for good. The long, solemn speech lets the audience share this turning point in his inner life.
:::

In the **epilogue**, Prospero, now without magic, speaks directly to the audience. Only their applause, he says, can release him from the island, so he asks them to set him free. The play ends with power passing from the magician to the audience.

## Building the island with words

**Songs.** Ariel’s songs fill the island with music. When Ferdinand believes his father has drowned, Ariel sings: “Full fathom five thy father lies.” A *fathom* is about six feet (1.8 metres) of water depth, so the song pictures the King thirty feet under the sea, changed by the waters into “something rich and strange”.

**Verse and prose.** As in his other plays, Shakespeare usually gives verse to serious feeling and prose to comedy. Stephano and Trinculo, the comic drunks, speak prose. Caliban, often called a monster, usually speaks in verse, and some of the most beautiful lines in the play are his:

> Be not afeard; the isle is full of noises,\
> Sounds and sweet airs, that give delight and hurt not.
>
> — William Shakespeare, *The Tempest*, Act 3, Scene 2

:::example Caliban’s verse
@step Caliban is comforting Stephano and Trinculo, who are frightened by Ariel’s invisible music.
@step His words are gentle and musical: *sounds and sweet airs* (tunes) that *give delight and hurt not*.
@answer Giving Caliban this verse shows his deep love for the island’s beauty, and complicates the idea that he is only a beast.
:::

**Hyperbole and description.** Ferdinand’s cry during the storm, “Hell is empty, / And all the devils are here”, uses exaggeration to capture the panic of the shipwreck. Characters’ descriptions of the island, from the sweet air to the “barren place”, build the setting in the audience’s imagination.

:::mistake Staging matters
When you write about a play, remember it was written to be performed. Ask how a scene would look and sound on stage: the music, the invisible Ariel, the sudden vanishing of the masque.
:::

:::summary
- A **soliloquy** reveals a character’s private thoughts; an **aside** is shared with the audience; the **epilogue** speaks to the audience at the end.
- Prospero’s farewell to magic and his epilogue mark the play’s turning point and close.
- Songs, verse and description create the island on a bare stage.
- Caliban’s verse reveals a depth beyond the “monster” others see.
:::
