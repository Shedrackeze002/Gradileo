The **sonnet** has been a favourite form of poets for over seven hundred years: fourteen lines, a strict pattern, and a turn where everything changes. In this lesson you will compare the two main kinds of sonnet, find the **volta**, and explain how poets use **sound** to shape meaning.

## Two kinds of sonnet

| | Shakespearean (English) sonnet | Petrarchan (Italian) sonnet |
|---|---|---|
| **Shape** | three **quatrains** (four lines each) and a rhyming **couplet** | an **octave** (eight lines) and a **sestet** (six) |
| **Rhyme scheme** | ABAB CDCD EFEF GG | ABBAABBA, then CDECDE or similar |
| **The turn (volta)** | often at the couplet, sometimes at line 9 | usually at line 9 |

:::definition Volta
The **volta** is the turn in a sonnet, where the poem shifts in argument, tone or direction. Words like *but*, *yet* and *then* often mark it.
:::

Christina Rossetti’s “Remember” is a Petrarchan sonnet.

> Remember me when I am gone away,\
> Gone far away into the silent land;\
> When you can no more hold me by the hand,\
> Nor I half turn to go yet turning stay.\
> Remember me when no more day by day\
> You tell me of our future that you planned:\
> Only remember me; you understand\
> It will be late to counsel then or pray.\
> Yet if you should forget me for a while\
> And afterwards remember, do not grieve:\
> For if the darkness and corruption leave\
> A vestige of the thoughts that once I had,\
> Better by far you should forget and smile\
> Than that you should remember and be sad.
>
> — Christina Rossetti, “Remember” (1862)

:::example Finding the volta
@step The octave (lines 1 to 8) repeats one request: “Remember me.”
@step Line 9 begins “Yet”, and the request changes.
@step The sestet says that if remembering would make her loved one sad, it would be better to forget and smile.
@answer The volta comes at “Yet if you should forget me for a while”. It turns a plea to be remembered into a selfless wish for the other person’s happiness.
:::

Shakespeare’s Sonnet 116 argues that true love never changes. Its final couplet stakes everything on that claim: “If this be error and upon me proved, / I never writ, nor no man ever loved.” If he is wrong, he says, he never wrote a word and no one has ever loved, which is plainly impossible.

## Sound devices

| Device | What it repeats | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Alliteration** | consonant sounds at the start of words | **M**en **m**arched asleep. **M**any had lost their boots |
| **Assonance** | vowel sounds | the l**ow** m**oa**n of the l**o**nely r**oa**d |
| **Sibilance** | *s* and *sh* sounds | And the **s**ilken, **s**ad, un**c**ertain ru**s**tling of each purple curtain (Edgar Allan Poe, “The Raven”, 1845) |
| **Onomatopoeia** | words that imitate sounds | buzz, plop, hiss |

Sound often echoes meaning. Owen’s heavy, repeated *m* sounds mimic the slow, dull trudge of the march. Poe’s soft *s* sounds make the curtain seem to whisper. Harsh sounds such as *k* and *g* in “Knock-kneed, coughing like hags” make the experience feel ugly and painful.

:::mistake Say what the sound does
Spotting alliteration is easy. The marks come from explaining its effect: does the sound speed the line up or slow it down, make it soft or harsh, soothing or violent?

:::summary
- A **sonnet** has 14 lines. **Shakespearean**: three quatrains and a couplet. **Petrarchan**: an octave and a sestet.
- The **volta** is the turn, often marked by *but* or *yet*.
- **Alliteration**, **assonance**, **sibilance** and **onomatopoeia** shape how a line sounds and feels.
:::
