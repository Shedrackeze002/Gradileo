Some stories mean more than they say. A locked gate can stand for grief; a race between animals can stand for any challenge in life. In this lesson you will interpret **symbols** and **motifs**, and learn how an **allegory** turns a whole story into a second meaning.

## Symbols and motifs

:::definition Symbol and motif
A **symbol** is an object, person or place that stands for a bigger idea, while keeping its ordinary meaning. A **motif** is an image or idea that keeps recurring through a text.
:::

> The garden had been Grandpa’s. Since the funeral, Grandma had kept its gate locked, and nobody had seen her go in. Weeds climbed the walls.

:::example Interpreting a symbol
@step The garden belonged to the grandfather and has been locked since his death.
@step The weeds show it has been neglected for a long time.
@step So the locked, overgrown garden stands for something Grandma has shut away: her grief.
@answer Later, when Grandma unlocks the gate and starts pulling up weeds, the change in the symbol shows that she is beginning to heal.
:::

A motif works through repetition. A caged bird that appears three times in a novel about a girl whose parents control her life mirrors her own lack of freedom.

:::mistake Not everything is a symbol
A good reading of a symbol is supported by evidence from the text: repetition, emphasis, or a clear link to a character or theme. If you cannot point to that evidence, the object may simply be part of the setting.
:::

## Allegory

:::definition Allegory
An **allegory** is a story in which the characters and events stand for ideas or real events. It works on two levels at once: the surface story and its hidden meaning.
:::

| Allegory | Surface story | Second meaning |
|---|---|---|
| Aesop’s fable of the tortoise and the hare | a slow tortoise beats a fast but careless hare | any challenge, where steady effort beats careless speed |
| Plato’s allegory of the cave (about 375 BCE) | prisoners chained in a cave take shadows on the wall for reality | most people see only a limited picture of the truth; leaving the cave is learning |
| George Orwell’s *Animal Farm* (1945) | farm animals overthrow their farmer, but the pigs become tyrants | the Russian Revolution of 1917, which ended in Stalin’s dictatorship |

A **fable** usually teaches a lesson through animals; a **parable** is a short story, usually about people, that teaches a moral or religious lesson.

:::example Symbol or allegory?
@step A story about a lonely boy includes a broken kite that he keeps in his room. The kite is a symbol: one element that stands for his lost hopes.
@step In *Animal Farm*, nearly every animal and event maps onto something in history: the pigs onto the leaders, the farm onto the country, the rebellion onto the revolution.
@answer A symbol is one element; an **allegory** is a whole system of meanings, where the entire story points to a second level.
:::

:::summary
- A **symbol** stands for a bigger idea; a **motif** is a recurring image or idea.
- Support every reading of a symbol with evidence; watch for changes to a symbol.
- An **allegory** works on two levels, with the whole story standing for a second meaning.
- **Fables** and **parables** are short allegorical stories that teach a lesson.
:::
