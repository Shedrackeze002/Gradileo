The greatest speeches are remembered not just for what they said but for how they said it. **Rhetorical devices** are patterns of language that make words persuasive and memorable. In this lesson you will identify four of the most powerful and explain their effects.

## Four rhetorical devices

| Device | What it is | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Anaphora** | repeating the same words at the start of several sentences or clauses | We will not wait. We will not be silent. We will not give up. |
| **Tricolon** (the rule of three) | three parallel words, phrases or clauses in a row | government of the people, by the people, for the people |
| **Antithesis** | opposite ideas set side by side in a balanced structure | It was the best of times, it was the worst of times. |
| **Rhetorical question** | a question asked for effect, not for an answer | Who among us does not want a safer town for our children? |

The antithesis example opens Charles Dickens’s novel *A Tale of Two Cities* (1859). The tricolon comes from Abraham Lincoln’s Gettysburg Address (1863), given during the American Civil War.

Devices often work together. Lincoln also said:

> But, in a larger sense, we can not dedicate—we can not consecrate—we can not hallow—this ground.
>
> — Abraham Lincoln, the Gettysburg Address (1863)

:::example Spotting combined devices
@step “We can not” begins each of the three phrases: that is **anaphora**.
@step There are exactly three phrases, each with a verb of making something holy: that is a **tricolon**.
@step The three verbs grow in solemnity, from *dedicate* to *consecrate* to *hallow*.
@answer Lincoln combines anaphora and a tricolon to build to a climax: the soldiers who died have already made the ground sacred, more than any words could.
:::

## The effect of rhetoric

Each device has typical effects.

- **Anaphora** builds rhythm and drives a key idea home through repetition. It can stir emotion like a drumbeat.
- **A tricolon** sounds complete and is easy to remember, and it often builds to a climax on the third item.
- **Antithesis** sharpens a contrast, so the choice or the idea seems clear-cut.
- **A rhetorical question** pulls the audience into the argument and makes the answer seem obvious.

Small word choices can be just as powerful. In 1852, the abolitionist Frederick Douglass, who had escaped from slavery, was invited to speak at a Fourth of July celebration.

> What, to the American slave, is your 4th of July? I answer: a day that reveals to him, more than all other days in the year, the gross injustice and cruelty to which he is the constant victim.
>
> — Frederick Douglass, “What to the Slave Is the Fourth of July?” (1852)

:::example Explaining the effect
@step Douglass asks a **rhetorical question** and then answers it himself.
@step The question forces his audience to see their celebration of freedom from the view of an enslaved person.
@step He says “**your** 4th of July”, not “our”: a single pronoun shows that enslaved people are shut out of the freedom being celebrated.
@answer The question and the pronoun together expose the gap between the nation’s ideal of liberty and the reality of slavery.
:::

:::mistake Name it, then explain it
“Lincoln uses a tricolon” earns little. Say what the device makes the audience feel, notice or remember, and why that serves the speaker’s purpose.
:::

:::summary
- **Anaphora** repeats openings; a **tricolon** groups three; **antithesis** balances opposites; a **rhetorical question** is asked for effect.
- Devices often combine, as in Lincoln’s “we can not dedicate—we can not consecrate—we can not hallow”.
- Always explain the effect on the audience, including the effect of small word choices.
:::
