In Grade 8 you met iambs and trochees. Now you will **scan** lines yourself to find their metre, and look at the tools poets use to bend rhythm: **enjambment**, **caesura** and the deliberate broken beat.

## Scansion

:::definition Scansion
**Scansion** is marking the stressed (DUM) and unstressed (da) syllables in a line to find its metre.
:::

| Foot | Pattern | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Iamb** | da-DUM | to-DAY |
| **Trochee** | DUM-da | GAR-den |
| **Spondee** | DUM-DUM | true minds |

To scan a line, read it aloud naturally, tap the beats, and count the feet.

:::example Scanning Poe
@step “Once upon a midnight dreary, while I pondered, weak and weary,” (Edgar Allan Poe, “The Raven”, 1845)
@step Mark the stresses: ONCE up-ON a MID-night DREAR-y, WHILE i PON-dered, WEAK and WEAR-y.
@step The pattern is DUM-da, repeated eight times.
@answer The line is **trochaic** (eight trochees). The insistent beat gives the poem its haunting, chant-like mood.
:::

Shakespeare’s Sonnet 116 is mostly iambic pentameter, but it opens “LET me NOT”, landing on a stress straight away instead of the expected da-DUM. The forceful start makes the speaker sound firm and certain. Poets often break the beat on purpose like this, to make a word stand out.

:::mistake Read it naturally
Do not force a pattern onto a line. Say it as you would in conversation, then listen for the stresses. Real poems vary their metre, and the variations are often where the meaning lies.
:::

## Enjambment and caesura

:::definition Enjambment and caesura
**Enjambment** is when a sentence runs on from one line to the next without a pause. A **caesura** is a pause in the middle of a line, often marked by punctuation. A line that ends with punctuation is **end-stopped**.
:::

> Let me not to the marriage of true minds\
> Admit impediments.
>
> — William Shakespeare, Sonnet 116 (1609)

The thought runs from “true minds” straight on to “Admit impediments”: **enjambment** pushes the reader into the next line, so the sense flows on.

In “Dulce et Decorum Est”, Wilfred Owen packs his lines with caesuras:

> Men marched asleep. Many had lost their boots\
> But limped on, blood-shod. All went lame; all blind;

:::example The effect of caesura
@step “All went lame; all blind;” is broken by a semicolon into tiny phrases.
@step Each pause makes the reader stop and start again.
@answer The broken rhythm feels like exhausted, halting steps, mirroring the soldiers’ stumbling march.
:::

Form can mirror meaning. Neat, end-stopped lines feel complete and controlled, which can suit a poem about order. Lines that spill over can suit overflowing emotion or a rush of thought.

:::summary
- **Scansion** marks stresses to find the metre: **iambs** (da-DUM), **trochees** (DUM-da) and **spondees** (DUM-DUM).
- Poets break the regular beat to stress important words.
- **Enjambment** runs on; a **caesura** pauses; **end-stopped** lines feel complete.
- Ask how the rhythm matches the meaning.
:::
