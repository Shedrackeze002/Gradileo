A paragraph where every sentence has the same shape soon sounds like a ticking clock. In this lesson you will identify the four **sentence types** and use **sentence variety** to control rhythm and emphasis.

## Four sentence types

:::definition Clauses
A **main clause** has a subject and a verb and can stand alone as a sentence. A **subordinate clause** cannot stand alone; it usually begins with a word such as *because*, *although*, *when* or *if*.
:::

| Type | Made of | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Simple** | one main clause | The dog barked. |
| **Compound** | two or more main clauses, joined by *and*, *but*, *so* or a semicolon | The dog barked, and the cat ran away. |
| **Complex** | one main clause and at least one subordinate clause | When the doorbell rang, the dog barked. |
| **Compound-complex** | two or more main clauses and at least one subordinate clause | When the storm hit, the lights went out, and we lit candles. |

:::example Counting clauses
@step “Although it was late, Ada kept reading, but her eyes were closing.”
@step Main clauses: “Ada kept reading” and “her eyes were closing”. That is two.
@step Subordinate clause: “Although it was late”. That is one.
@answer Two main clauses plus a subordinate clause make it **compound-complex**.
:::

## Sentence variety

Compare these two versions.

> I woke at six. It was raining. The rain was on the roof. I pulled the covers up. I lay listening.

> I woke at six. Rain was drumming on the roof, so I pulled the covers up and lay listening.

The first is all short, simple sentences, so it sounds flat. The second mixes a short sentence with a longer compound one, so the rhythm moves.

Ways to vary sentences:

- **Length**: follow long sentences with a short one to make a point land.
- **Type**: mix simple, compound and complex sentences.
- **Openings**: begin with a subordinate clause (“Because the road was flooded, the bus turned back”) or a phrase (“After hours of searching, we found it”) instead of always starting with the subject.

:::mistake A fragment on purpose
A one-word sentence such as “Silence.” at the end of a tense paragraph can create a powerful pause. Used sparingly and on purpose, it works; used by accident or too often, it reads as an error.
:::

:::summary
- **Simple**: one main clause. **Compound**: two or more main clauses. **Complex**: a main clause and a subordinate clause. **Compound-complex**: both.
- Vary sentence length, type and openings to control rhythm.
- A short sentence after long ones adds emphasis.
:::
