**Close reading** means reading slowly and carefully, paying attention to every word, image and sound, and asking what each one adds. It is the core skill of literary analysis. In this lesson you will close read a poem step by step and explain how its **imagery** creates an effect.

## Reading closely, step by step

1. **Read the whole poem** slowly, more than once, for its overall sense. Read it aloud if you can.
2. **Look at the title.** It often frames the meaning or names the key image.
3. **Find the speaker and the situation.** Who is speaking, to whom, and about what?
4. **Mark key words and images.** Underline words with strong connotations and any contrasts.
5. **Ask what changes.** Look for shifts in tone or idea.
6. **Consider context.** When and by whom was it written? Context can open up a fuller reading, but it never replaces the words.

Here are the first lines of a poem by Paul Laurence Dunbar, one of the first African American poets to win wide fame.

> We wear the mask that grins and lies,\
> It hides our cheeks and shades our eyes,—\
> This debt we pay to human guile;\
> With torn and bleeding hearts we smile,\
> And mouth with myriad subtleties.
>
> — Paul Laurence Dunbar, “We Wear the Mask” (1895), first lines

:::example Close reading Dunbar
@step The title names the central image: a **mask**, something worn to hide the real face.
@step The mask “grins and lies”, so it is a false, smiling face.
@step “With torn and bleeding hearts we smile” sets inner suffering against an outward show of happiness: that contrast is the heart of the poem.
@step “This debt we pay to human guile” suggests that a cruel, deceitful world (*guile* means cunning deceit) forces people to hide their pain.
@step Context: Dunbar wrote in 1895, when Black Americans faced fierce racism. The “we” can be read as the people who had to hide their pain to survive it.
@answer The poem shows people forced to smile while suffering inside, and the context deepens it into a protest against a society that demands that mask.
:::

## Imagery

:::definition Imagery
**Imagery** is language that appeals to the senses (sight, sound, touch, taste and smell) to create a picture in the reader’s mind.
:::

Wilfred Owen fought as a soldier in the First World War and was killed a week before it ended. His poems show the war as the soldiers lived it.

> Bent double, like old beggars under sacks,\
> Knock-kneed, coughing like hags, we cursed through sludge,\
> Till on the haunting flares we turned our backs\
> And towards our distant rest began to trudge.\
> Men marched asleep. Many had lost their boots\
> But limped on, blood-shod. All went lame; all blind;
>
> — Wilfred Owen, “Dulce et Decorum Est” (1920), first lines

:::example Analysing imagery
@step “Like old beggars under sacks”: the young soldiers are bent, exhausted and wretched, more like old beggars than heroes.
@step “Coughing like hags, we cursed through sludge”: we **hear** the coughing and cursing and **feel** the thick mud.
@step “Blood-shod”: having lost their boots, they march on feet so wounded that blood covers them like shoes.
@answer Every image strips away the idea that war is glorious, and shows soldiers as broken, filthy and suffering.
:::

:::mistake Name the sense, then the effect
“Owen uses imagery” says nothing. Say which sense the image appeals to and what feeling it creates in the reader.
