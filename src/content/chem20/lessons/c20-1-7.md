In lesson 1.4 you met **polar bonds**. When two atoms with different electronegativities share electrons, the more electronegative atom pulls the shared pair closer. It gains a small negative charge, $\delta-$, and the other atom is left with a small positive charge, $\delta+$.

A whole molecule can be polar too, with one side slightly negative and the other slightly positive. But here is the surprise: a molecule can be full of polar bonds and still be **non-polar**. Whether a molecule is polar depends on two things — its **bonds** and its **shape**.

## Polar bonds and dipoles

In hydrogen chloride, chlorine (electronegativity 3.2) is more electronegative than hydrogen (2.2), so the shared pair sits closer to the chlorine atom:

$$\overset{\delta+}{\ce{H}}\ce{-}\overset{\delta-}{\ce{Cl}}$$

Anything with a positive end and a negative end like this is called a **dipole**, which means "two poles".

:::definition Dipole
A **dipole** is a pair of equal and opposite partial charges, $\delta+$ and $\delta-$, a small distance apart. A polar bond is a dipole, and so is a polar molecule.
:::

:::definition Polar molecule
A **polar molecule** has an uneven spread of charge: one side is partly negative ($\delta-$) and the opposite side is partly positive ($\delta+$). A **non-polar molecule** has its charge spread evenly, with no negative or positive side.
:::

For a molecule made of just **two atoms**, the single bond decides everything:

- two **identical** atoms, as in $\ce{N2}$ or $\ce{Cl2}$, pull equally on the shared electrons. The bond is non-polar, so the molecule is **non-polar**;
- two **different** atoms, as in $\ce{HCl}$ or $\ce{HBr}$, form a polar bond. With only one bond there is nothing to cancel it, so the molecule is **polar**.

## Shape decides: do the pulls cancel?

With three or more atoms, picture each polar bond as a rope in a tug of war, pulling electron charge towards its more electronegative atom. If equal pulls point in opposite directions, they cancel and the molecule is non-polar. If they do not cancel, the molecule has a negative side and a positive side: it is polar.

:::example Carbon dioxide
Is carbon dioxide, $\ce{CO2}$, polar or non-polar?
@step Bonds: oxygen (3.4) is more electronegative than carbon (2.6), so each $\ce{C=O}$ bond is polar, with $\delta-$ on each oxygen and $\delta+$ on the carbon.
@step Shape: carbon has two bonded atoms and no lone pairs, so $\ce{O=C=O}$ is linear, with the oxygens on opposite sides of the carbon.
@step The two pulls are equal and point in exactly opposite directions, so they cancel. Neither end of the molecule is more negative than the other.
@answer $\ce{CO2}$ is **non-polar**, even though both of its bonds are polar.
:::

:::example Water
Is water, $\ce{H2O}$, polar or non-polar?
@step Bonds: oxygen (3.4) is more electronegative than hydrogen (2.2), so each $\ce{O-H}$ bond is polar, with $\delta-$ on the oxygen and $\delta+$ on each hydrogen.
@step Shape: oxygen has two bonded atoms and two lone pairs, so the molecule is V-shaped (bent), with a bond angle of about $104.5^\circ$.
@step Both bonds pull electron charge towards the oxygen at the point of the V. They lean the same way, so they cannot cancel.
@answer $\ce{H2O}$ is **polar**: the oxygen side is $\delta-$ and the side with the two hydrogens is $\delta+$.
:::

:::mistake Polar bonds do not always make a polar molecule
"It has polar bonds, so it must be polar" is a very common error. $\ce{CO2}$ has two polar bonds and is non-polar. A molecule needs polar bonds to be polar, but its **shape** decides whether their pulls cancel.
:::

The shapes from lesson 1.6 fall into two groups:

| Shape | Lone pairs on the central atom | With identical outer atoms, the molecule is | Examples |
|---|---|---|---|
| linear | 0 | non-polar | $\ce{CO2}$, $\ce{CS2}$ |
| trigonal planar | 0 | non-polar | $\ce{BF3}$ |
| tetrahedral | 0 | non-polar | $\ce{CH4}$, $\ce{CCl4}$, $\ce{SiH4}$ |
| V-shaped (bent) | 2 | polar | $\ce{H2O}$, $\ce{H2S}$, $\ce{OF2}$ |
| trigonal pyramidal | 1 | polar | $\ce{NH3}$, $\ce{PCl3}$, $\ce{NF3}$ |

In the first three shapes, identical outer atoms are spread evenly around the central atom, so every pull is balanced by the others. In V-shaped and trigonal pyramidal molecules, the lone pairs on the central atom crowd the bonds over to one side. The pulls all lean the same way, so these molecules are polar.

:::definition Symmetry
A molecule has **symmetry** (it is **symmetrical**) when identical outer atoms are spaced evenly around the central atom, as in a linear, trigonal planar or tetrahedral molecule with no lone pairs on the central atom. In a symmetrical molecule, the pulls of the bonds cancel.
:::

:::example Three outer atoms, two shapes
Boron trifluoride, $\ce{BF3}$, and ammonia, $\ce{NH3}$, each have three identical outer atoms joined by polar bonds. Which is polar?
@step $\ce{BF3}$: boron has three bonded atoms and no lone pairs, so the molecule is trigonal planar, with the fluorines $120^\circ$ apart. Three equal pulls spread evenly around a flat triangle cancel.
@step $\ce{NH3}$: nitrogen has three bonded atoms and one lone pair, so the molecule is trigonal pyramidal. The three hydrogens form the base of the pyramid with the nitrogen at the top, and all three pulls lean up towards the nitrogen, so they do not cancel.
@answer $\ce{BF3}$ is **non-polar**; $\ce{NH3}$ is **polar**, with the nitrogen $\delta-$ and the hydrogens $\delta+$.
:::

:::note Bonds that are only slightly polar
Any difference in electronegativity makes a bond at least slightly polar. The $\ce{S-H}$ bond has a difference of only 0.4, so lesson 1.4's rule calls it non-polar covalent, yet it still pulls a little. In V-shaped $\ce{H2S}$, two of these small pulls lean the same way, so $\ce{H2S}$ is polar — just less polar than water.
:::

## Same shape, different outer atoms

Symmetry needs **identical** outer atoms as well as an even shape. If one outer atom is different, the pulls no longer balance — even in a linear, trigonal planar or tetrahedral molecule.

Compare four tetrahedral molecules, each with carbon in the centre:

| Molecule | Outer atoms | Polar or non-polar? |
|---|---|---|
| $\ce{CH4}$ | 4 H | non-polar |
| $\ce{CH3Cl}$ | 3 H and 1 Cl | polar |
| $\ce{CHCl3}$ | 1 H and 3 Cl | polar |
| $\ce{CCl4}$ | 4 Cl | non-polar |

In $\ce{CCl4}$, four equal $\ce{C-Cl}$ pulls point to the four corners of a tetrahedron and cancel. Swap even one chlorine for a hydrogen and the balance is lost: the side with the chlorines becomes $\delta-$.

:::example Chloroform
Is chloroform, $\ce{CHCl3}$, polar or non-polar?
@step Bonds: chlorine (3.2) is more electronegative than carbon (2.6), so the three $\ce{C-Cl}$ bonds are polar. The $\ce{C-H}$ bond is only very slightly polar.
@step Shape: carbon has four bonded atoms and no lone pairs, so the molecule is tetrahedral.
@step The outer atoms are not identical. Three chlorines pull strongly, and the hydrogen on the fourth corner does not balance them.
@answer $\ce{CHCl3}$ is **polar**: the side with the chlorines is $\delta-$ and the side with the hydrogen is $\delta+$.
:::

Two more molecules have even shapes but different outer atoms:

- **Hydrogen cyanide**, $\ce{H-C#N}$, is linear, but its two ends differ. Nitrogen is the most electronegative of its atoms and hydrogen the least, so both pulls point the same way, towards the nitrogen. They add up instead of cancelling, and $\ce{HCN}$ is polar.
- **Methanal**, $\ce{CH2O}$, is trigonal planar, but one outer atom is oxygen and two are hydrogen. Nothing balances the strong pull of the $\ce{C=O}$ bond, so methanal is polar.

:::tip Three questions for polarity
1. **Does it have polar bonds?** A molecule with no polar bonds at all is non-polar, whatever its shape. That includes $\ce{N2}$ and $\ce{Cl2}$, and also $\ce{CS2}$ (C and S are both 2.6) and $\ce{CH4}$ (the C–H difference is only 0.4).
2. **What is its shape?** Use VSEPR theory from lesson 1.6.
3. **Is it symmetrical, with identical outer atoms?** If yes (linear, trigonal planar or tetrahedral, all outer atoms the same), it is non-polar. If not, it is polar.
:::

## Evidence for polar molecules

How can you tell whether a liquid is made of polar molecules? Charge a plastic rod by rubbing it with a cloth, then hold it near a thin, steady stream of the liquid running from a burette.

- A stream of **water** bends towards the rod. Its polar molecules turn so that their oppositely charged ends face the rod, and they are pulled towards it. The stream bends towards a positively charged rod **and** towards a negatively charged one.
- A stream of a **non-polar** liquid, such as hexane, does not bend noticeably. Its molecules have no charged sides to turn towards the rod.

:::example Predicting the stream test
A charged rod is held near thin streams of two liquids, $\ce{CCl4}$ and $\ce{CHCl3}$. Which stream bends towards the rod?
@step $\ce{CCl4}$ is tetrahedral with four identical chlorines, so its pulls cancel: it is non-polar.
@step $\ce{CHCl3}$ is tetrahedral, but one outer atom is hydrogen, so its pulls do not cancel: it is polar.
@answer The $\ce{CHCl3}$ stream bends towards the rod; the $\ce{CCl4}$ stream does not.
:::

:::note Like dissolves like
Polarity also helps explain what dissolves in what. Polar substances tend to dissolve in polar liquids such as water, and non-polar substances tend to dissolve in non-polar liquids. That is why oil, which is non-polar, does not mix with water. You will use this idea, **"like dissolves like"**, when you study solutions.
:::

:::summary
- A polar bond has partial charges, $\delta+$ and $\delta-$, because the atoms' electronegativities differ.
- A **polar molecule** has a $\delta-$ side and a $\delta+$ side: it is a **dipole**.
- Two atoms: identical atoms give a non-polar molecule ($\ce{N2}$, $\ce{Cl2}$); different atoms give a polar one ($\ce{HCl}$, $\ce{HBr}$).
- A molecule is polar when it has polar bonds **and** its shape does not let their pulls cancel.
- **Symmetrical** molecules (linear, trigonal planar or tetrahedral, with identical outer atoms) are non-polar: $\ce{CO2}$, $\ce{BF3}$, $\ce{CCl4}$.
- V-shaped and trigonal pyramidal molecules are polar: $\ce{H2O}$, $\ce{NH3}$. So are molecules whose outer atoms differ: $\ce{CH3Cl}$, $\ce{HCN}$, $\ce{CH2O}$.
- A stream of a polar liquid bends towards a charged rod; a stream of a non-polar liquid does not.
:::
