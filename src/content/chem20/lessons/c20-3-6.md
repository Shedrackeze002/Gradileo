Lemon juice and vinegar taste sour. Soap and many cleaning products feel slippery. Pure water is neither. Long before anyone knew about ions, chemists sorted solutions into **acids** and **bases** by what they do, and the two groups turned out to cancel each other out.

In this lesson you will tell acidic, basic and neutral solutions apart by experiment, explain the difference with hydronium and hydroxide ions, and predict the salt and water that form when an acid neutralises a base.

## Acidic, basic and neutral solutions

A description built only on what you can observe is called **empirical**. The empirical tests for acids and bases are quick and need no theory at all. The most common use an indicator.

:::definition Indicator
An **indicator** is a substance whose colour depends on whether a solution is acidic, basic or neutral. Litmus paper, bromothymol blue and phenolphthalein are common indicators.
:::

| Test | Acidic solution | Basic solution | Neutral solution |
|---|---|---|---|
| Litmus paper | blue litmus turns red | red litmus turns blue | neither litmus paper changes colour |
| Bromothymol blue | yellow | blue | green |
| Phenolphthalein | colourless | pink | colourless |
| Magnesium ribbon | reacts, releasing bubbles of hydrogen gas | no reaction | no reaction |
| pH at $25^\circ\text{C}$ | below 7 | above 7 | 7 |
| Conductivity | conducts electricity | conducts electricity | conducts only if it contains ions |
| Neutralises | bases | acids | neither |

Acids react with active metals such as magnesium and zinc, releasing hydrogen gas: $\ce{Mg(s) + 2HCl(aq) -> MgCl2(aq) + H2(g)}$. The **pH** scale, which lesson 3.7 explains, measures how acidic or basic a solution is: below 7 is acidic, 7 is neutral and above 7 is basic, and the further the pH is from 7, the more acidic or basic the solution. Acidic and basic solutions both conduct electricity, because they contain ions. A neutral solution conducts only if it has ions too: salt water does, but sugar water does not.

:::example Reading the tests
A colourless solution turns phenolphthalein pink. Is it acidic, basic or neutral, and what would it do to red litmus paper and to bromothymol blue?
@step Phenolphthalein turns pink only in a basic solution.
@step A basic solution turns red litmus blue and turns bromothymol blue indicator blue.
@answer The solution is basic. It turns red litmus paper blue and bromothymol blue indicator blue.
:::

:::mistake One test may not be enough
Phenolphthalein is colourless in acidic **and** neutral solutions, so a colourless result does not prove that a solution is acidic. In the same way, red litmus stays red in both acidic and neutral solutions. To show that a solution is acidic, use blue litmus, which turns red, or bromothymol blue, which turns yellow.
:::

:::note Never taste a lab chemical
Sour taste and a slippery feel are clues in the kitchen, but acids and bases in the lab can burn your skin and eyes. Use indicators instead, and wear eye protection.
:::

## Hydronium and hydroxide ions

Why do so many different acids behave alike? In the 1880s the chemist Svante Arrhenius suggested that acids release hydrogen ions, $\ce{H+}$, in water, and bases release hydroxide ions, $\ce{OH-}$. A hydrogen ion is a bare proton, though, and it never floats free in water: it attaches at once to a water molecule, forming a **hydronium ion**, $\ce{H3O+}$. The **modified Arrhenius theory** takes this into account.

:::definition Acid and base
An **acid** is a substance that reacts with water to form **hydronium ions**, $\ce{H3O+}$. A **base** is a substance that forms **hydroxide ions**, $\ce{OH-}$, in water. Hydronium ions make a solution acidic; hydroxide ions make it basic.
:::

Hydrogen chloride is a molecular compound, but in water each molecule gives a hydrogen ion to a water molecule. This is **ionisation**: a molecular substance reacting with water to form ions. It differs from the dissociation of lesson 3.1, in which an ionic compound separates into ions it already contains.

$$\ce{HCl(aq) + H2O(l) -> H3O+(aq) + Cl-(aq)}$$

A metal hydroxide is ionic, so it simply dissociates into metal ions and hydroxide ions: $\ce{NaOH(s) -> Na+(aq) + OH-(aq)}$. Barium hydroxide releases two hydroxide ions for each formula unit: $\ce{Ba(OH)2(s) -> Ba^2+(aq) + 2OH-(aq)}$.

Ammonia, $\ce{NH3}$, has no hydroxide in its formula, yet its solution turns red litmus blue. It is a base because it reacts with water: a water molecule gives a hydrogen ion to the ammonia molecule, which leaves a hydroxide ion behind. Only a small fraction of the ammonia molecules react, but the hydroxide ions they form are enough to make the solution basic.

$$\ce{NH3(aq) + H2O(l) -> NH4+(aq) + OH-(aq)}$$

:::example An acid ionising
Write the equation for hydrogen bromide, $\ce{HBr}$, ionising in water to make an acidic solution.
@step The acid gives a hydrogen ion to a water molecule, forming the hydronium ion, $\ce{H3O+}$.
@step What is left of the acid is the bromide ion, $\ce{Br-}$. The charges balance: $0$ on the left, $1+$ and $1-$ on the right.
@answer $\ce{HBr(aq) + H2O(l) -> H3O+(aq) + Br-(aq)}$
:::

:::mistake Charges on the wrong ions
The hydrogen leaves the acid as a **positive** ion and joins water, so the rest of the acid becomes a **negative** ion: $\ce{Cl-}$, never $\ce{Cl+}$. A hydroxide keeps its $\ce{OH-}$ ion whole: $\ce{KOH}$ gives $\ce{K+}$ and $\ce{OH-}$, not $\ce{KO-}$ and $\ce{H+}$.
:::

## Which solutions are acidic, basic or neutral?

The theory tells you what to look for in a formula. Acids form $\ce{H3O+}$ ions in water; hydroxides and ammonia form $\ce{OH-}$ ions; substances that form neither are neutral.

| Acidic | Basic | Neutral |
|---|---|---|
| hydrochloric acid, $\ce{HCl(aq)}$ | sodium hydroxide, $\ce{NaOH(aq)}$ | sodium chloride, $\ce{NaCl(aq)}$ |
| nitric acid, $\ce{HNO3(aq)}$ | potassium hydroxide, $\ce{KOH(aq)}$ | potassium nitrate, $\ce{KNO3(aq)}$ |
| sulfuric acid, $\ce{H2SO4(aq)}$ | lithium hydroxide, $\ce{LiOH(aq)}$ | calcium chloride, $\ce{CaCl2(aq)}$ |
| acetic acid, $\ce{CH3COOH(aq)}$ | barium hydroxide, $\ce{Ba(OH)2(aq)}$ | sucrose, $\ce{C12H22O11(aq)}$ |
| hydrofluoric acid, $\ce{HF(aq)}$ | ammonia, $\ce{NH3(aq)}$ | ethanol, $\ce{C2H5OH(aq)}$ |

The acids are the ones named in lesson 1.3; most of their formulas begin with H. Acetic acid is written $\ce{CH3COOH}$ because only the last hydrogen, the one in the COOH group, ionises. Salts such as sodium chloride, potassium nitrate and calcium chloride form ions, so their solutions conduct, but the ions are neither $\ce{H3O+}$ nor $\ce{OH-}$. Molecular compounds such as sucrose and ethanol form no ions at all. Not every salt is neutral, though: some react with water to make an acidic or basic solution, as you will see in later chemistry courses.

:::mistake An OH does not make a base
Ethanol, $\ce{C2H5OH}$, has OH in its formula, but it is a molecular compound: the OH is bonded into the molecule and never comes off as an $\ce{OH-}$ ion, so ethanol is neutral. In the same way, an H in a formula does not make an acid. Ammonia, $\ce{NH3}$, has three hydrogen atoms and is a base.
:::

## Neutralisation

Add a base to an acid and the hydroxide ions take the hydrogen ions from the hydronium ions, forming water: $\ce{H3O+(aq) + OH-(aq) -> 2H2O(l)}$. The ions that made one solution acidic and the other basic are used up.

:::definition Neutralisation
**Neutralisation** is the reaction of an acid with a base. An acid and a hydroxide base make a salt and water: acid + hydroxide base → salt + water.
:::

:::definition Salt
A **salt** is an ionic compound. The salt from a neutralisation is made of the metal ion from the base and the anion from the acid.
:::

The metal ion comes from the base: $\ce{Na+}$, $\ce{K+}$ and $\ce{Li+}$ from $\ce{NaOH}$, $\ce{KOH}$ and $\ce{LiOH}$, and $\ce{Ca^2+}$, $\ce{Ba^2+}$ and $\ce{Mg^2+}$ from $\ce{Ca(OH)2}$, $\ce{Ba(OH)2}$ and $\ce{Mg(OH)2}$. The anion is what is left of the acid once its hydrogen ions are gone:

| Acid | $\ce{HCl}$ | $\ce{HBr}$ | $\ce{HNO3}$ | $\ce{H2SO4}$ | $\ce{H3PO4}$ |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Anion | chloride, $\ce{Cl-}$ | bromide, $\ce{Br-}$ | nitrate, $\ce{NO3-}$ | sulfate, $\ce{SO4^2-}$ | phosphate, $\ce{PO4^3-}$ |

Write the salt's formula just as in lesson 1.2: take the fewest ions whose charges cancel.

:::example A simple salt
$\ce{HBr(aq) + KOH(aq) -> salt + H2O(l)}$. Write the formula of the salt.
@step The metal ion from the base is $\ce{K+}$; the anion from the acid is $\ce{Br-}$.
@step One $\ce{K+}$ balances one $\ce{Br-}$.
@answer The salt is potassium bromide, $\ce{KBr}$: $\ce{HBr(aq) + KOH(aq) -> KBr(aq) + H2O(l)}$.
:::

:::example A salt with brackets
$\ce{HNO3(aq) + Ca(OH)2(s) -> salt + H2O(l)}$. Write the formula of the salt.
@step The metal ion is $\ce{Ca^2+}$; the anion is $\ce{NO3-}$.
@step Two $\ce{NO3-}$ ions balance one $\ce{Ca^2+}$. A polyatomic ion taken more than once goes in brackets.
@answer The salt is calcium nitrate, $\ce{Ca(NO3)2}$.
:::

:::note Why some hydroxides are solids
By the solubility rules of lesson 3.2, hydroxides have low solubility except those of group 1 ions, $\ce{NH4+}$, $\ce{Sr^2+}$ and $\ce{Ba^2+}$. So $\ce{Ca(OH)2}$ and $\ce{Mg(OH)2}$ are written as solids, (s), while $\ce{NaOH}$, $\ce{KOH}$, $\ce{LiOH}$ and $\ce{Ba(OH)2}$ are written (aq). A solid hydroxide still neutralises an acid: it dissolves as its hydroxide ions react.
:::

## Balancing a neutralisation

Each $\ce{H+}$ the acid gives up meets one $\ce{OH-}$ from the base and makes one water molecule. To balance the equation:

1. **Write the salt's formula** from its ions.
2. **Balance the metal and the anion**: choose coefficients for the base and the acid that give the salt the right number of each.
3. **Count the water**: one $\ce{H2O}$ for each $\ce{H+}$ neutralised.
4. **Check** every element, and use the lowest whole numbers.

:::example Sulfuric acid and sodium hydroxide
Balance the neutralisation of $\ce{H2SO4}$ by $\ce{NaOH}$, which makes sodium sulfate and water.
@step The salt is $\ce{Na2SO4}$: two $\ce{Na+}$ balance one $\ce{SO4^2-}$.
@step Two sodium ions need $\ce{2NaOH}$; one sulfate ion needs one $\ce{H2SO4}$.
@step $\ce{H2SO4}$ gives up 2 $\ce{H+}$, which meet the 2 $\ce{OH-}$: 2 water molecules.
@answer $\ce{H2SO4 + 2NaOH -> Na2SO4 + 2H2O}$. Check: 4 H and 6 O on each side.
:::

:::example Phosphoric acid and calcium hydroxide
Balance the neutralisation of $\ce{H3PO4}$ by $\ce{Ca(OH)2}$, which makes calcium phosphate and water.
@step The salt is $\ce{Ca3(PO4)2}$: three $\ce{Ca^2+}$ ($6+$) balance two $\ce{PO4^3-}$ ($6-$).
@step Three calcium ions need $\ce{3Ca(OH)2}$; two phosphate ions need $\ce{2H3PO4}$.
@step $\ce{2H3PO4}$ give up $2 \times 3 = 6$ $\ce{H+}$, which meet the $3 \times 2 = 6$ $\ce{OH-}$: 6 water molecules.
@answer $\ce{2H3PO4 + 3Ca(OH)2 -> Ca3(PO4)2 + 6H2O}$. Check: 3 Ca, 2 P, 12 H and 14 O on each side.
:::

:::tip Count the hydrogen ions
The number of water molecules always equals the number of $\ce{H+}$ ions the acid gives up, which equals the number of $\ce{OH-}$ ions from the base. In $\ce{2HCl + Ca(OH)2 -> CaCl2 + 2H2O}$, two $\ce{H+}$ meet two $\ce{OH-}$ and make two waters.
:::

:::summary
- Acidic solutions turn blue litmus red and bromothymol blue yellow, react with magnesium to release hydrogen, and have a pH below 7.
- Basic solutions turn red litmus blue and phenolphthalein pink, neutralise acids, and have a pH above 7. Neutral solutions have a pH of 7 and change neither litmus paper.
- Modified Arrhenius theory: acids react with water to form hydronium ions, $\ce{H3O+}$; bases form hydroxide ions, $\ce{OH-}$.
- $\ce{HCl(aq) + H2O(l) -> H3O+(aq) + Cl-(aq)}$ is ionisation; $\ce{NaOH(s) -> Na+(aq) + OH-(aq)}$ is dissociation. Ammonia is a base because it reacts with water to form $\ce{OH-}$.
- Salts such as $\ce{NaCl}$ and molecular compounds such as sucrose and ethanol form neither ion, so their solutions are neutral.
- Acid + hydroxide base → salt + water. The salt holds the metal ion from the base and the anion from the acid.
- To balance, match the metal and the anion first, then add one $\ce{H2O}$ for each $\ce{H+}$ neutralised.
:::
