At room temperature, methane, $\ce{CH4}$, is a gas: it boils at about −162 °C. Water, $\ce{H2O}$, is a liquid that boils at 100 °C. Yet the two molecules are about the same size, and each has 10 electrons. Why do water molecules cling to each other so much more tightly?

The answer lies in the attractions **between** molecules. In this lesson you will meet three of them — London dispersion forces, dipole–dipole forces and hydrogen bonds — and use them to explain why some substances boil at much higher temperatures than others.

## Forces inside and between molecules

Two different kinds of attraction hold a molecular substance together:

- **intramolecular** bonds are the covalent bonds that hold the atoms together *inside* each molecule;
- **intermolecular forces** are attractions that act *between* neighbouring molecules.

:::definition Intermolecular force
An **intermolecular force** is an attraction between neighbouring molecules. Intermolecular forces are much weaker than the covalent bonds inside molecules.
:::

When water boils, its molecules move apart from one another, but each molecule stays whole: steam is still made of $\ce{H2O}$ molecules. Boiling overcomes only the intermolecular forces. Breaking all the $\ce{O-H}$ bonds in some water would take more than twenty times as much energy as boiling it.

:::mistake Boiling does not break molecules apart
When water boils, its molecules do **not** split into hydrogen and oxygen. Only the attractions *between* the molecules are overcome; the bonds inside each molecule stay as they were.
:::

:::definition Boiling point
The **boiling point** of a liquid is the temperature at which bubbles of its gas form throughout the liquid. The stronger the intermolecular forces, the more energy the molecules need to escape from one another, so the **higher** the boiling point. (The boiling points in this lesson are for normal air pressure.)
:::

## London dispersion forces

The electrons in a molecule are always moving. At any instant, a few more of them may happen to be on one side than the other, giving the molecule a tiny, short-lived dipole. That dipole disturbs the electrons of a neighbouring molecule, giving it a dipole too, and the two molecules attract. These flickering attractions are called **London dispersion forces**, after the physicist Fritz London. They are often just called London forces.

:::definition London dispersion force
A **London dispersion force** is a weak attraction between molecules caused by short-lived dipoles as their electrons move. London dispersion forces act between **all** molecules, polar or non-polar, and they are **stronger when the molecules have more electrons**.
:::

More electrons make a larger electron cloud that is more easily pushed out of shape, so the short-lived dipoles are bigger. To count the electrons in a molecule, add up the atomic numbers of its atoms. The halogens show the pattern clearly:

| Molecule | Electrons | Boiling point | State at room temperature |
|---|---|---|---|
| $\ce{F2}$ | 18 | about −188 °C | gas |
| $\ce{Cl2}$ | 34 | about −34 °C | gas |
| $\ce{Br2}$ | 70 | about 59 °C | liquid |
| $\ce{I2}$ | 106 | about 184 °C | solid |

:::example More electrons, higher boiling point
Which has the higher boiling point, propane, $\ce{C3H8}$, or methane, $\ce{CH4}$?
@step Both are non-polar, so only London dispersion forces act between their molecules.
@step Count the electrons: $\ce{C3H8}$ has $3 \times 6 + 8 \times 1 = 26$, and $\ce{CH4}$ has $6 + 4 \times 1 = 10$.
@step More electrons mean stronger London dispersion forces.
@answer Propane, $\ce{C3H8}$, has the higher boiling point: about −42 °C, against about −162 °C for methane.
:::

## Dipole–dipole forces

Polar molecules have permanent dipoles. Neighbouring polar molecules tend to line up so that the $\delta+$ end of one is close to the $\delta-$ end of the next, and these opposite charges attract. This is an **extra** attraction, on top of the London dispersion forces that every molecule has.

:::definition Dipole–dipole force
A **dipole–dipole force** is the attraction between the $\delta+$ end of one polar molecule and the $\delta-$ end of a neighbouring polar molecule. Dipole–dipole forces act only between **polar** molecules.
:::

:::example Same electrons, different polarity
Hydrogen chloride, $\ce{HCl}$, and fluorine, $\ce{F2}$, have the same number of electrons. Which has the higher boiling point?
@step Electrons: $\ce{HCl}$ has $1 + 17 = 18$ and $\ce{F2}$ has $2 \times 9 = 18$, so their London dispersion forces are similar.
@step $\ce{HCl}$ is made of two different atoms, so it is polar: dipole–dipole forces act between its molecules as well. $\ce{F2}$ has two identical atoms, so it is non-polar, with London dispersion forces only.
@answer $\ce{HCl}$ has the higher boiling point (about −85 °C, against about −188 °C for $\ce{F2}$), because dipole–dipole forces add to its London dispersion forces.
:::

## Hydrogen bonds

Something special happens when hydrogen is bonded directly to nitrogen, oxygen or fluorine. These atoms are so electronegative (N 3.0, O 3.4, F 4.0) that they pull the shared electrons well away from the small hydrogen atom, leaving it strongly $\delta+$. That hydrogen is strongly attracted to a lone pair on an N, O or F atom of a neighbouring molecule.

:::definition Hydrogen bond
A **hydrogen bond** is the attraction between a hydrogen atom bonded directly to N, O or F in one molecule and a lone pair on an N, O or F atom of another molecule. For molecules of similar size, hydrogen bonds are the strongest of the three intermolecular forces.
:::

Water, $\ce{H2O}$, ammonia, $\ce{NH3}$, hydrogen fluoride, $\ce{HF}$, and methanol, $\ce{CH3OH}$, all form hydrogen bonds. Each water molecule can form up to four: two through its hydrogen atoms and two through the lone pairs on its oxygen.

:::note Not really a bond
Despite its name, a hydrogen bond is an intermolecular force, not a covalent bond. It is much weaker than the covalent $\ce{O-H}$ bond inside a water molecule.
:::

:::mistake "It contains hydrogen, so it forms hydrogen bonds"
The hydrogen must be bonded **directly to N, O or F**. $\ce{CH4}$, $\ce{H2S}$ and $\ce{HCl}$ all contain hydrogen but form no hydrogen bonds. In methanol, $\ce{CH3OH}$, only the hydrogen on the oxygen can form hydrogen bonds; the three on the carbon cannot.
:::

The forces add up. Every molecule has London dispersion forces; polar molecules also have dipole–dipole forces; and molecules with H on N, O or F have hydrogen bonds as well:

| Substance | Polar? | H bonded to N, O or F? | Forces between its molecules |
|---|---|---|---|
| $\ce{CH4}$ | no | no | London dispersion forces |
| $\ce{HCl}$ | yes | no | London dispersion forces and dipole–dipole forces |
| $\ce{NH3}$ | yes | yes | London dispersion forces, dipole–dipole forces and hydrogen bonds |

:::example Naming the forces
Which intermolecular forces act between molecules of ammonia, $\ce{NH3}$?
@step London dispersion forces act between all molecules.
@step $\ce{NH3}$ is trigonal pyramidal, so it is polar: dipole–dipole forces act too.
@step Each hydrogen is bonded directly to nitrogen, and the nitrogen has a lone pair, so hydrogen bonds form.
@answer All three: London dispersion forces, dipole–dipole forces and hydrogen bonds.
:::

Hydrogen bonding explains water's unusually high boiling point for such a small molecule. Methane, with the same 10 electrons but no hydrogen bonds, boils at about −162 °C; water does not boil until 100 °C.

:::example Why water boils so high
Which has the higher boiling point, water, $\ce{H2O}$, or hydrogen sulfide, $\ce{H2S}$?
@step Electrons: $\ce{H2S}$ has $2 \times 1 + 16 = 18$, more than the 10 in $\ce{H2O}$, so $\ce{H2S}$ has the stronger London dispersion forces.
@step But in water, H is bonded to O, so its molecules form hydrogen bonds. In $\ce{H2S}$, H is bonded to S, so there are none.
@step The hydrogen bonds matter far more than the difference in electrons.
@answer Water boils at 100 °C; $\ce{H2S}$ boils at only about −60 °C.
:::

## Comparing boiling points

:::tip Comparing boiling points in order
A higher boiling point means stronger intermolecular forces. To compare two substances, ask:
1. **Hydrogen bonds?** If only one of them forms hydrogen bonds, it has the higher boiling point.
2. **Polar?** If that does not decide it, a polar substance boils higher than a non-polar one.
3. **Electrons?** If they are still alike, the one with more electrons has stronger London dispersion forces and the higher boiling point.
:::

| Higher boiling point | Lower boiling point | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| $\ce{C2H6}$, about −89 °C | $\ce{CH4}$, about −162 °C | more electrons (18 against 10) |
| $\ce{CCl4}$, about 77 °C | $\ce{CH4}$, about −162 °C | more electrons (74 against 10) |
| $\ce{HF}$, about 20 °C | $\ce{HCl}$, about −85 °C | hydrogen bonds |
| $\ce{NH3}$, about −33 °C | $\ce{PH3}$, about −88 °C | hydrogen bonds |
| $\ce{CH3OH}$, about 65 °C | $\ce{C2H6}$, about −89 °C | hydrogen bonds (both have 18 electrons) |
| $\ce{HCl}$, about −85 °C | $\ce{F2}$, about −188 °C | polar (both have 18 electrons) |

:::example Same electrons, different forces
Methanol, $\ce{CH3OH}$, boils at about 65 °C, but ethane, $\ce{C2H6}$, boils at about −89 °C. Explain the difference.
@step Electrons: $\ce{CH3OH}$ has $6 + 4 \times 1 + 8 = 18$, and $\ce{C2H6}$ has $2 \times 6 + 6 \times 1 = 18$. Their London dispersion forces are similar.
@step Methanol has an H bonded directly to O, so its molecules form hydrogen bonds (and it is polar). Ethane is non-polar, with London dispersion forces only.
@answer Hydrogen bonds hold methanol molecules together much more strongly, so methanol has the much higher boiling point.
:::

:::note Size still matters
The order above works for molecules of similar size. A very large non-polar molecule can have London dispersion forces strong enough to beat a small polar one: iodine, $\ce{I2}$, with 106 electrons, boils at about 184 °C — higher than water.
:::

:::summary
- **Intramolecular** bonds hold atoms together inside a molecule; **intermolecular forces** act between molecules and are much weaker.
- Boiling overcomes intermolecular forces; stronger forces mean a **higher boiling point**.
- **London dispersion forces** act between all molecules and are stronger when molecules have more electrons.
- **Dipole–dipole forces** act between polar molecules, on top of London forces.
- **Hydrogen bonds** form when H bonded to N, O or F is attracted to a lone pair on an N, O or F of another molecule. They are the strongest of the three for molecules of similar size.
- Compare boiling points in order: hydrogen bonding first, then polarity, then the number of electrons.
- Hydrogen bonding gives water its unusually high boiling point: $\ce{H2O}$ boils at 100 °C, $\ce{H2S}$ at about −60 °C.
:::
