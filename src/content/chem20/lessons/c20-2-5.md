A weather balloon is released at ground level and rises through the sky. As it climbs, the air pressure around it falls, which lets it swell; but the air also gets colder, which makes it shrink. Which effect wins? Pressure, volume and temperature often change all at once, yet each of the gas laws so far holds one of them fixed.

In this lesson you will combine Boyle's, Charles's and Gay-Lussac's laws into one equation, use it to find any one quantity, and predict how a gas changes when its conditions are multiplied by simple factors.

## Three laws in one

Boyle's law says that the volume of a gas is inversely proportional to its pressure. Charles's law says that the volume is directly proportional to the Kelvin temperature, and Gay-Lussac's law says the same of the pressure. All three fit into a single equation.

:::definition Combined gas law
For a fixed amount of gas, $\dfrac{P_1V_1}{T_1} = \dfrac{P_2V_2}{T_2}$, with both temperatures in kelvins. The pressure times the volume, divided by the Kelvin temperature, stays the same.
:::

Each of the three earlier laws is the combined gas law with one quantity held constant. A quantity that does not change appears on both sides of the equation, so it cancels:

| Held constant | It cancels, leaving | Law |
|---|---|---|
| temperature, $T_1 = T_2$ | $P_1V_1 = P_2V_2$ | Boyle's law |
| pressure, $P_1 = P_2$ | $\dfrac{V_1}{T_1} = \dfrac{V_2}{T_2}$ | Charles's law |
| volume, $V_1 = V_2$ | $\dfrac{P_1}{T_1} = \dfrac{P_2}{T_2}$ | Gay-Lussac's law |

:::tip One law to remember
If you remember the combined gas law, you have all three of the others: cross out whatever stays the same.
:::

## Solving for any one quantity

Rearranging the combined gas law gives each of the quantities after a change:

- $V_2 = V_1 \times \dfrac{P_1}{P_2} \times \dfrac{T_2}{T_1}$
- $P_2 = P_1 \times \dfrac{V_1}{V_2} \times \dfrac{T_2}{T_1}$
- $T_2 = T_1 \times \dfrac{P_2}{P_1} \times \dfrac{V_2}{V_1}$

Each fraction corrects for one change, and you can check that it is the right way up. In the formula for $V_2$, a rise in pressure must make the volume smaller, so the pressure fraction must then be less than 1: $\dfrac{P_1}{P_2}$, not $\dfrac{P_2}{P_1}$. A rise in temperature must make the volume larger, so the temperature fraction must then be more than 1: $\dfrac{T_2}{T_1}$.

:::example Kelvin temperatures
A gas occupies $5.0\ \text{L}$ at $120\ \text{kPa}$ and $300\ \text{K}$. What volume does it occupy at $200\ \text{kPa}$ and $360\ \text{K}$, to the nearest hundredth?
@step The pressure rises, which shrinks the gas: multiply by $\dfrac{120}{200}$. The temperature rises, which expands it: multiply by $\dfrac{360}{300}$.
@step $V_2 = V_1 \times \dfrac{P_1}{P_2} \times \dfrac{T_2}{T_1} = 5.0 \times \dfrac{120}{200} \times \dfrac{360}{300} = 5.0 \times 0.6 \times 1.2 = 3.60\ \text{L}$
@answer $V_2 = 3.60\ \text{L}$
:::

:::example Celsius temperatures
A gas occupies $8.0\ \text{L}$ at $100\ \text{kPa}$ and $20^\circ\text{C}$. What volume does it occupy at $250\ \text{kPa}$ and $-10^\circ\text{C}$, to the nearest hundredth? Use $T(\text{K}) = T(^\circ\text{C}) + 273.15$.
@step $T_1 = 20 + 273.15 = 293.15\ \text{K}$ and $T_2 = -10 + 273.15 = 263.15\ \text{K}$
@step $V_2 = 8.0 \times \dfrac{100}{250} \times \dfrac{263.15}{293.15} = 2.872\ldots$
@answer $V_2 = 2.87\ \text{L}$. Both changes, a higher pressure and a lower temperature, make the gas smaller.
:::

The same equation gives a new pressure or a new temperature.

:::example A new pressure
A gas occupies $5.0\ \text{L}$ at $120\ \text{kPa}$ and $15^\circ\text{C}$. It is moved into a $3.0\ \text{L}$ container and brought to $60^\circ\text{C}$. What is its new pressure, to the nearest whole number? Use $T(\text{K}) = T(^\circ\text{C}) + 273.15$.
@step $T_1 = 15 + 273.15 = 288.15\ \text{K}$ and $T_2 = 60 + 273.15 = 333.15\ \text{K}$
@step A smaller volume and a higher temperature both raise the pressure, so both fractions must be more than 1.
@step $P_2 = P_1 \times \dfrac{V_1}{V_2} \times \dfrac{T_2}{T_1} = 120 \times \dfrac{5.0}{3.0} \times \dfrac{333.15}{288.15} = 231.23\ldots$
@answer $P_2 = 231\ \text{kPa}$
:::

:::example A new temperature
A gas occupies $4.0\ \text{L}$ at $150\ \text{kPa}$ and $25^\circ\text{C}$. At what temperature in degrees Celsius will it occupy $6.5\ \text{L}$ at $110\ \text{kPa}$? Round to the nearest degree. Use $T(\text{K}) = T(^\circ\text{C}) + 273.15$.
@step $T_1 = 25 + 273.15 = 298.15\ \text{K}$
@step $T_2 = T_1 \times \dfrac{P_2}{P_1} \times \dfrac{V_2}{V_1} = 298.15 \times \dfrac{110}{150} \times \dfrac{6.5}{4.0} = 355.30\ \text{K}$
@step Change back to degrees Celsius: $355.30 - 273.15 = 82.15^\circ\text{C}$
@answer $T_2 = 82^\circ\text{C}$, to the nearest degree.
:::

## Predicting changes by factors

Often you need to know only **how** a quantity changes, not its new value. From the combined gas law, $\dfrac{V_2}{V_1} = \dfrac{T_2/T_1}{P_2/P_1}$: the volume is multiplied by the factor for the Kelvin temperature and divided by the factor for the pressure. With one quantity held constant, this gives:

| Change | Held constant | Result |
|---|---|---|
| pressure tripled | temperature | volume multiplied by $\tfrac{1}{3}$ |
| Kelvin temperature halved | pressure | volume multiplied by $\tfrac{1}{2}$ |
| volume cut to a quarter | temperature | pressure multiplied by $4$ |
| Kelvin temperature doubled | volume | pressure multiplied by $2$ |
| amount of gas doubled | temperature and pressure | volume multiplied by $2$ |

The last row needs one more idea. At the same temperature and pressure, twice as many gas particles need twice as much room, so the volume is directly proportional to the amount of gas. This is Avogadro's law, in lesson 2.6. The combined gas law itself assumes the amount of gas stays the same.

:::example Two changes at once
The pressure of a gas is doubled and its Kelvin temperature is halved. The amount of gas stays the same. What happens to its volume?
@step $\dfrac{V_2}{V_1} = \dfrac{T_2/T_1}{P_2/P_1}$
@step The temperature factor is $\tfrac{1}{2}$ and the pressure factor is $2$, so $\dfrac{V_2}{V_1} = \dfrac{\tfrac{1}{2}}{2} = \tfrac{1}{4}$.
@step Both changes shrink the gas: the higher pressure squeezes it, and the lower temperature slows its particles.
@answer It is multiplied by $\tfrac{1}{4}$.
:::

:::note The weather balloon
As a weather balloon climbs, the pressure around it falls by a much larger fraction than its Kelvin temperature does. Dividing by the small pressure factor outweighs multiplying by the temperature factor, so the balloon keeps growing, until it bursts high in the atmosphere.
:::

## The Celsius trap

Factors work only with Kelvin temperatures. A change that looks like doubling in degrees Celsius is much smaller in kelvins.

:::example Warming a gas from 25 to 50 degrees
At constant pressure, a gas is warmed from $25^\circ\text{C}$ to $50^\circ\text{C}$. What happens to its volume?
@step The Celsius temperature doubles, but Charles's law uses kelvins: $T_1 = 25 + 273.15 = 298.15\ \text{K}$ and $T_2 = 50 + 273.15 = 323.15\ \text{K}$.
@step The volume is multiplied by $\dfrac{323.15}{298.15} \approx 1.084$.
@answer It increases by about 8%. It does not double.
:::

:::mistake Doubling the Celsius temperature
Doubling a Celsius temperature does not double the Kelvin temperature, so it does not double the volume or the pressure. To double the volume of a gas at $25^\circ\text{C}$, its Kelvin temperature must double, from $298.15\ \text{K}$ to $596.30\ \text{K}$, which is $323.15^\circ\text{C}$.
:::

:::summary
- **Combined gas law**: $\dfrac{P_1V_1}{T_1} = \dfrac{P_2V_2}{T_2}$ for a fixed amount of gas, with temperatures in kelvins.
- Hold the temperature, pressure or volume constant and it cancels, leaving Boyle's, Charles's or Gay-Lussac's law.
- Solve for one quantity: $V_2 = V_1 \times \dfrac{P_1}{P_2} \times \dfrac{T_2}{T_1}$, and similarly for $P_2$ and $T_2$. Check that each fraction is the right way up.
- To predict a change, multiply the volume by the Kelvin temperature factor and divide it by the pressure factor.
- At constant temperature and pressure, the volume is directly proportional to the amount of gas.
- Doubling a Celsius temperature does not double the volume: warming from $25^\circ\text{C}$ to $50^\circ\text{C}$ increases it by only about 8%.
:::
