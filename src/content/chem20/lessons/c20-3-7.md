Lemon juice, black coffee and household ammonia all contain hydronium ions, but in wildly different amounts. A solution of $0.10\ \text{mol/L}$ hydrochloric acid has $[\ce{H3O+}] = 0.10\ \text{mol/L}$, while at $25^\circ\text{C}$ a $0.10\ \text{mol/L}$ solution of sodium hydroxide has $[\ce{H3O+}] = 1.0 \times 10^{-13}\ \text{mol/L}$: $10^{12}$ times less. Comparing numbers like these is awkward, so chemists use the **pH scale** instead.

In this lesson you will find the pH from an ion concentration and the concentration from the pH, link pH to **pOH**, and see why one step on the pH scale is a factor of ten.

## Why a logarithmic scale?

In lesson 3.6 you met the hydronium ion, $\ce{H3O+}$, which makes a solution acidic. Square brackets around a formula mean "the concentration of", in moles per litre, so $[\ce{H3O+}]$ is the hydronium ion concentration. In everyday solutions it runs from about $1\ \text{mol/L}$ down to about $10^{-14}\ \text{mol/L}$. On an ordinary scale, every solution except the most acidic would be squashed up against zero.

A **logarithm** solves the problem. The base-10 logarithm of a number, written $\log$, is the power of ten that gives the number: $\log 1000 = 3$, $\log 0.01 = -2$ and $\log 10^{-5} = -5$. Taking the log turns each power of ten into one step of 1, so a huge range of concentrations fits on a short scale.

:::definition Logarithmic scale
A scale on which each step of 1 stands for a factor of 10. The pH scale is logarithmic: going down one pH unit means ten times as much $\ce{H3O+}$.
:::

:::definition pH
$\text{pH} = -\log[\ce{H3O+}]$. The minus sign makes the pH of most solutions a positive number, usually between 0 and 14.
:::

| $[\ce{H3O+}]$ (mol/L) | $1.0 \times 10^{-1}$ | $1.0 \times 10^{-3}$ | $1.0 \times 10^{-7}$ | $1.0 \times 10^{-11}$ |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| pH | 1.00 | 3.00 | 7.00 | 11.00 |

:::example A power of ten
A solution has $[\ce{H3O+}] = 1.0 \times 10^{-4}\ \text{mol/L}$. What is its pH, to two decimal places?
@step The log of $10^{-n}$ is $-n$, so $\log(1.0 \times 10^{-4}) = -4$.
@answer $\text{pH} = -\log(1.0 \times 10^{-4}) = 4.00$
:::

:::check c20-ph 1
:::

Most concentrations are not exact powers of ten. Use the log key on your calculator, then change the sign.

:::example Any concentration
A solution has $[\ce{H3O+}] = 3.2 \times 10^{-5}\ \text{mol/L}$. What is its pH, to two decimal places?
@step $\text{pH} = -\log[\ce{H3O+}] = -\log(3.2 \times 10^{-5})$
@step The calculator gives $\log(3.2 \times 10^{-5}) = -4.4948...$, so the pH is $4.4948...$
@answer $\text{pH} = 4.49$
:::

:::tip Significant figures in a pH
The number of decimal places in a pH equals the number of significant figures in the concentration. $3.2 \times 10^{-5}$ has two significant figures, so its pH, $4.49$, has two decimal places. The digit before the decimal point only shows the power of ten, so it does not count. That is why $1.0 \times 10^{-4}$ gives $4.00$, not $4$.
:::

:::check c20-ph 2
:::

## From pH back to concentration

To undo a logarithm, raise ten to its power. Because $\text{pH} = -\log[\ce{H3O+}]$, the concentration is

$$[\ce{H3O+}] = 10^{-\text{pH}}$$

:::example A whole-number pH
A solution has a pH of 6.00. What is its hydronium ion concentration, $[\ce{H3O+}]$, in mol/L?
@step $[\ce{H3O+}] = 10^{-6.00}$
@answer $[\ce{H3O+}] = 10^{-6} = 1.0 \times 10^{-6}\ \text{mol/L}$
:::

:::tip Typing scientific notation
Type scientific notation like `4.7e-5` or `4.7*10^-5`. So $1.0 \times 10^{-6}$ is typed as `1.0e-6` or `1.0*10^-6`.
:::

:::check c20-ph-conc 1
:::

For any other pH, use the $10^x$ key on your calculator with $x = -\text{pH}$. The significant-figure rule works in reverse: a pH with two decimal places gives a concentration with two significant figures.

:::example A pH with decimals
A solution has a pH of 3.75. What is $[\ce{H3O+}]$, in mol/L, to two significant figures?
@step $[\ce{H3O+}] = 10^{-3.75} = 0.00017782...$
@answer $[\ce{H3O+}] = 1.8 \times 10^{-4}\ \text{mol/L}$, typed as `1.8e-4`
:::

:::mistake Dropping the minus sign
$10^{3.75}$ is about 5600, which is far too large: no solution holds $5600\ \text{mol/L}$ of hydronium ions. For any positive pH, $[\ce{H3O+}]$ must be less than $1\ \text{mol/L}$, so check that the exponent is negative.
:::

:::check c20-ph-conc 2
:::

## pOH and the hydroxide ion

Even pure water contains a few ions. A tiny fraction of its molecules pass a hydrogen ion to one another: $\ce{2H2O(l) <=> H3O+(aq) + OH-(aq)}$. In pure water at $25^\circ\text{C}$, $[\ce{H3O+}] = [\ce{OH-}] = 1.0 \times 10^{-7}\ \text{mol/L}$, and in any aqueous solution at that temperature the product of the two concentrations stays the same:

$$K_w = [\ce{H3O+}][\ce{OH-}] = 1.0 \times 10^{-14}$$

When one concentration goes up, the other goes down, so basic solutions still contain some hydronium ions, and acidic ones some hydroxide ions. The hydroxide ion has its own logarithmic scale.

:::definition pOH
$\text{pOH} = -\log[\ce{OH-}]$, and in reverse, $[\ce{OH-}] = 10^{-\text{pOH}}$.
:::

Taking $-\log$ of both sides of $K_w$ turns the product into a sum: at $25^\circ\text{C}$, $\text{pH} + \text{pOH} = 14.00$. So you can always go from one scale to the other.

:::example pH from the hydroxide ion
A solution has $[\ce{OH-}] = 2.5 \times 10^{-3}\ \text{mol/L}$. What is its pH, to two decimal places? At $25^\circ\text{C}$, $\text{pH} + \text{pOH} = 14.00$.
@step Find the pOH first: $\text{pOH} = -\log(2.5 \times 10^{-3}) = 2.60$
@step $\text{pH} = 14.00 - 2.60 = 11.40$
@answer $\text{pH} = 11.40$: a basic solution.
:::

:::check c20-ph 3
:::

:::example Hydroxide ion concentration from the pH
A solution has a pH of 9.30. What is its hydroxide ion concentration, $[\ce{OH-}]$, in mol/L, to two significant figures? At $25^\circ\text{C}$, $\text{pH} + \text{pOH} = 14.00$.
@step $\text{pOH} = 14.00 - 9.30 = 4.70$
@step $[\ce{OH-}] = 10^{-4.70}$
@answer $[\ce{OH-}] = 2.0 \times 10^{-5}\ \text{mol/L}$
:::

:::mistake Using the pH for the wrong ion
$10^{-9.30} = 5.0 \times 10^{-10}\ \text{mol/L}$ is the **hydronium** ion concentration of that solution, not the hydroxide. To find $[\ce{OH-}]$, change the pH into a pOH first.
:::

:::check c20-ph-conc 3
:::

:::note Why 25 °C?
The value of $K_w$ changes with temperature, so the sum $\text{pH} + \text{pOH} = 14.00$, and a neutral pH of exactly 7, hold at $25^\circ\text{C}$. Questions state the temperature, or the rule, that they use.
:::

## The pH scale

At $25^\circ\text{C}$, a pH below 7 is **acidic**, 7 is **neutral** and above 7 is **basic**. The lower the pH, the more acidic the solution. The number line shows the approximate pH of some everyday solutions.

:::figure

:::

| pH | $[\ce{H3O+}]$ (mol/L) | $[\ce{OH-}]$ (mol/L) | pOH | Solution |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 2.00 | $1.0 \times 10^{-2}$ | $1.0 \times 10^{-12}$ | 12.00 | acidic |
| 7.00 | $1.0 \times 10^{-7}$ | $1.0 \times 10^{-7}$ | 7.00 | neutral |
| 12.00 | $1.0 \times 10^{-12}$ | $1.0 \times 10^{-2}$ | 2.00 | basic |

:::check c20-ph-scale 1
:::

Because the scale is logarithmic, each step of 1 on the pH scale is a factor of 10 in $[\ce{H3O+}]$. A difference of 2 is a factor of $10^2 = 100$, and a difference of 3 is a factor of $10^3 = 1000$.

:::example Comparing two solutions
Solution A has a pH of 3, and solution B has a pH of 5. How many times greater is $[\ce{H3O+}]$ in solution A than in solution B?
@step The pH values differ by 2, so $[\ce{H3O+}]$ differs by a factor of $10^{2} = 100$.
@step The lower pH has the greater hydronium ion concentration, so A has more.
@answer 100 times greater
:::

:::mistake A difference of 2 is not twice as much
Two pH units apart does not mean twice the concentration. Each unit is a factor of ten, so a pH of 3 has $10 \times 10 = 100$ times the hydronium ion concentration of a pH of 5.
:::

:::check c20-ph-scale 2
:::

You can also classify a solution without finding its pH. In neutral water at $25^\circ\text{C}$, $[\ce{H3O+}] = 1.0 \times 10^{-7}\ \text{mol/L}$. A solution with more hydronium ions than that is acidic; one with fewer is basic.

:::example Acidic or basic?
At $25^\circ\text{C}$, a solution has $[\ce{H3O+}] = 4.6 \times 10^{-9}\ \text{mol/L}$. Is it acidic, basic or neutral?
@step Compare with $1.0 \times 10^{-7}$. A larger negative exponent means a smaller number, so $4.6 \times 10^{-9}$ is less than $1.0 \times 10^{-7}$.
@step There are fewer hydronium ions than in neutral water, so the pH is above 7. (It is 8.34.)
@answer Basic
:::

:::check c20-ph-scale 3
:::

:::summary
- $\text{pH} = -\log[\ce{H3O+}]$ and $[\ce{H3O+}] = 10^{-\text{pH}}$; $\text{pOH} = -\log[\ce{OH-}]$ and $[\ce{OH-}] = 10^{-\text{pOH}}$.
- At $25^\circ\text{C}$, $K_w = [\ce{H3O+}][\ce{OH-}] = 1.0 \times 10^{-14}$, so $\text{pH} + \text{pOH} = 14.00$.
- The decimal places in a pH equal the significant figures in the concentration.
- At $25^\circ\text{C}$, a pH below 7 is acidic, 7 is neutral and above 7 is basic.
- The pH scale is logarithmic: each step of 1 is a factor of 10 in $[\ce{H3O+}]$, and the lower pH has the greater concentration.
- Compare $[\ce{H3O+}]$ with $1.0 \times 10^{-7}\ \text{mol/L}$: greater is acidic, less is basic.
- Type scientific notation like `4.7e-5` or `4.7*10^-5`.
:::
