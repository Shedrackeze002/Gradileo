Why is water $\ce{H2O}$ and not $\ce{H3O}$? Why does carbon almost always form four bonds? The answers lie in the **valence electrons**, the outer electrons that atoms share when they bond. A **Lewis structure** is a simple diagram that keeps track of every one of them.

In this lesson you will count the valence electrons in a molecule or ion, sort them into bonds and lone pairs, and see when two atoms share two or even three pairs at once.

## Counting valence electrons

Every Lewis structure starts with a count. For a main-group element, the number of valence electrons comes from its group in the periodic table:

| Element | H | B | C, Si | N, P | O, S | F, Cl, Br, I |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Valence electrons | 1 | 3 | 4 | 5 | 6 | 7 |

:::definition Total valence electrons
Add up the valence electrons of every atom in the formula. For a polyatomic ion, **add one** electron for each negative charge and **subtract one** for each positive charge.
:::

:::example A molecule
How many valence electrons are in a molecule of chloromethane, $\ce{CH3Cl}$?
@step Carbon gives $1 \times 4 = 4$, hydrogen $3 \times 1 = 3$ and chlorine $1 \times 7 = 7$.
@answer $4 + 3 + 7 = 14$ valence electrons, which make 7 pairs.
:::

An ion has gained or lost electrons, so its charge changes the count. A negative ion carries extra electrons; a positive ion has lost some.

:::example A negative ion
How many valence electrons are in the carbonate ion, $\ce{CO3^2-}$?
@step Atoms: $1 \times 4 + 3 \times 6 = 4 + 18 = 22$
@step The charge is $2-$, so the ion has two extra electrons: add 2.
@answer $22 + 2 = 24$ valence electrons.
:::

:::example A positive ion
How many valence electrons are in the ammonium ion, $\ce{NH4+}$?
@step Atoms: $1 \times 5 + 4 \times 1 = 9$
@step The charge is $1+$, so one electron has been removed: subtract 1.
@answer $9 - 1 = 8$ valence electrons.
:::

:::mistake Getting the charge backwards
A **negative** charge means **more** electrons, so you add: $\ce{OH-}$ has $6 + 1 + 1 = 8$. A **positive** charge means **fewer** electrons, so you subtract: $\ce{H3O+}$ has $3 \times 1 + 6 - 1 = 8$.
:::

:::

## Bonding pairs and lone pairs

Valence electrons go into a Lewis structure in pairs, and every pair has one of two jobs.

:::definition Lewis structure
A diagram of a molecule or ion that shows all of its valence electrons. Each shared pair is drawn as a line between two atoms, and each unshared pair as two dots beside one atom.
:::

:::definition Bonding pair and lone pair
A **bonding pair** is a pair of electrons shared between two atoms: one covalent bond. A **lone pair** belongs to one atom only and is not part of a bond.
:::

:::definition Octet rule
Atoms tend to share electrons until each is surrounded by eight valence electrons (an **octet**), like a noble gas. Both electrons of a bond count for both of the atoms it joins. Hydrogen is the exception: it needs only two.
:::

Water is the simplest example. In $\ce{H-O-H}$ the oxygen atom is joined to each hydrogen atom by one bonding pair, and it also carries two lone pairs:

| Atom | Bonds | Lone pairs | Electrons around it |
|---|---|---|---|
| O | 2 | 2 | $2 \times 2 + 2 \times 2 = 8$, an octet |
| each H | 1 | 0 | 2 |

Altogether water has 2 bonding pairs and 2 lone pairs. That is 4 pairs, holding its 8 valence electrons.

## Drawing a Lewis structure

Follow these steps for any molecule or polyatomic ion:

1. **Count** the total valence electrons, and halve the count to find the number of pairs.
2. **Choose the central atom**: usually the least electronegative atom, which is often the one there is only one of. Hydrogen is never central, because it forms only one bond.
3. **Join** each outer atom to the central atom with a single bond (one bonding pair).
4. **Complete the octets of the outer atoms** with lone pairs. Hydrogen needs only 2 electrons, so it gets no lone pairs.
5. **Put any remaining electrons on the central atom** as lone pairs.
6. If the central atom **still lacks an octet**, turn lone pairs on outer atoms into double or triple bonds (see the next section).

:::example Phosphorus trichloride
Draw the Lewis structure of $\ce{PCl3}$ and count its bonding pairs and lone pairs.
@step Count: $5 + 3 \times 7 = 26$ valence electrons, or 13 pairs.
@step Phosphorus is less electronegative than chlorine, so P is central. Three P–Cl single bonds use 3 pairs.
@step Each Cl needs 3 lone pairs to complete its octet: $3 \times 3 = 9$ lone pairs. That makes $3 + 9 = 12$ pairs so far.
@step One pair is left. It goes on phosphorus as a lone pair, which gives P its octet: 3 bonds and 1 lone pair.
@answer 3 bonding pairs and 10 lone pairs in all: 9 on the chlorine atoms and 1 on the central phosphorus atom.
:::

:::tip Check your structure
Bonding pairs + lone pairs must equal the total valence electrons ÷ 2. For $\ce{PCl3}$: $3 + 10 = 13 = 26 \div 2$. If the numbers disagree, an electron has gone missing or been counted twice.
:::

The same steps give these molecules, which all have single bonds only:

| Molecule | Valence electrons | Bonding pairs | Lone pairs (whole molecule) | Lone pairs on the central atom |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| $\ce{CH4}$ | 8 | 4 | 0 | 0 |
| $\ce{NH3}$ | 8 | 3 | 1 | 1 |
| $\ce{H2O}$ | 8 | 2 | 2 | 2 |
| $\ce{CH3Cl}$ | 14 | 4 | 3 | 0 |
| $\ce{OF2}$ | 20 | 2 | 8 | 2 |
| $\ce{PCl3}$ | 26 | 3 | 10 | 1 |
| $\ce{CCl4}$ | 32 | 4 | 12 | 0 |

Notice the pattern on the central atom: carbon forms 4 bonds and keeps no lone pairs; nitrogen and phosphorus form 3 bonds and keep 1 lone pair; oxygen forms 2 bonds and keeps 2 lone pairs. Each one ends with an octet.

Ions follow the same steps; draw the finished ion in square brackets with its charge outside. The ammonium ion, $\ce{[NH4]+}$, has 8 electrons: four N–H bonds and no lone pairs. The hydroxide ion, $\ce{[OH]-}$, also has 8: one O–H bond and three lone pairs on oxygen.

## Double and triple bonds

Sometimes single bonds leave the central atom short of an octet, because there are not enough electrons to go round. Then two atoms share more than one pair.

:::definition Double bond and triple bond
A **double bond** is two bonding pairs shared by the same two atoms, drawn as $=$. A **triple bond** is three bonding pairs shared by the same two atoms, drawn as $\equiv$.
:::

:::example Carbon dioxide
Draw the Lewis structure of $\ce{CO2}$.
@step Count: $4 + 2 \times 6 = 16$ valence electrons, or 8 pairs. Carbon is central.
@step Two C–O single bonds use 2 pairs, and 3 lone pairs on each O use 6 more. All 8 pairs are placed.
@step Carbon has only 2 bonds, or 4 electrons. Move one lone pair from each oxygen into its bond, making two double bonds. Now carbon has $2 \times 4 = 8$ electrons, and each oxygen has 4 bonding and 4 lone-pair electrons.
@answer $\ce{O=C=O}$: 4 bonding pairs and 4 lone pairs (2 on each O), with no lone pairs on carbon.
:::

:::example Hydrogen cyanide
Draw the Lewis structure of $\ce{HCN}$.
@step Count: $1 + 4 + 5 = 10$ valence electrons, or 5 pairs. Hydrogen cannot be central, and carbon is less electronegative than nitrogen, so C is central.
@step The H–C and C–N bonds use 2 pairs. Nitrogen takes 3 lone pairs, and all 5 pairs are placed.
@step Carbon has only 4 electrons. Turn two of nitrogen's lone pairs into bonds, making a triple bond. Now carbon has 8 electrons, and nitrogen has 6 bonding electrons and 1 lone pair.
@answer $\ce{H-C#N}$: 4 bonding pairs (1 + 3) and 1 lone pair, on nitrogen. Carbon has no lone pairs.
:::

:::mistake A double bond is two pairs
When you count bonding pairs, a double bond counts as **two** and a triple bond as **three**. $\ce{CO2}$ shows two bonds, but it has 4 bonding pairs. The check still works: $4 + 4 = 8 = 16 \div 2$.
:::

| Molecule | Valence electrons | Structure | Bonding pairs | Lone pairs | Lone pairs on the central atom |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| $\ce{CO2}$ | 16 | $\ce{O=C=O}$ | 4 | 4 | 0 |
| $\ce{HCN}$ | 10 | $\ce{H-C#N}$ | 4 | 1 | 0 |
| $\ce{CH2O}$ | 12 | $\ce{H2C=O}$: two C–H bonds and a C=O double bond | 4 | 2 | 0 |

A molecule of two atoms has no central atom: the two atoms simply share enough pairs to give each an octet. $\ce{Cl2}$ (14 electrons) has a single bond and 3 lone pairs on each atom: 1 bonding pair and 6 lone pairs. $\ce{HF}$ (8 electrons) has 1 bonding pair and 3 lone pairs, all on fluorine. $\ce{N2}$ (10 electrons) needs a triple bond, $\ce{N#N}$, with one lone pair on each nitrogen: 3 bonding pairs and 2 lone pairs.

:::note An exception: boron trifluoride
$\ce{BF3}$ has $3 + 3 \times 7 = 24$ valence electrons. Three B–F bonds and three lone pairs on each F use all 12 pairs, which leaves boron with only **six** electrons. Boron is stable with six, so do not add a double bond: $\ce{BF3}$ has 3 bonding pairs, 9 lone pairs and no lone pairs on boron. It is an exception to the octet rule.
:::

:::summary
- Total valence electrons: add every atom's valence electrons, then add 1 for each negative charge or subtract 1 for each positive charge.
- A bonding pair is shared by two atoms; a lone pair belongs to one atom.
- Octet rule: atoms share electrons until each has 8 valence electrons (hydrogen needs 2).
- Steps: choose the central atom (never H), add single bonds, complete the outer octets, put leftovers on the central atom, then make double or triple bonds if it still lacks an octet.
- A double bond is 2 bonding pairs; a triple bond is 3.
- Check: bonding pairs + lone pairs = total valence electrons ÷ 2.
- Boron in $\ce{BF3}$ is an exception, with only six electrons.
:::
