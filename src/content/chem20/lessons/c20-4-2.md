In lesson 3.2, mixing silver nitrate and sodium chloride solutions made a white precipitate of silver chloride. Use potassium chloride or calcium chloride instead of sodium chloride, and exactly the same white solid appears. The sodium, potassium, calcium and nitrate ions make no difference, because they never take part: only the silver ions and the chloride ions react.

In this lesson you will write the **total ionic equation** for a reaction in solution, find the **spectator ions** that take no part, and write the **net ionic equation** that shows only the change that really happens.

## Three ways to write a reaction

An equation with whole formulas, like those in lesson 4.1, is a **formula equation** (some books call it a molecular equation). It is easy to balance, but it hides what the solutions really hold. In lesson 3.1 you saw that an ionic compound dissociates when it dissolves: a solution of potassium bromide holds separate $\ce{K+}$ and $\ce{Br-}$ ions, not $\ce{KBr}$ units.

:::definition Total ionic equation
A **total ionic equation** writes every dissolved ionic compound, (aq), as its separate ions. Solids, such as a precipitate, and molecular substances, such as water, stay as whole formulas.
:::

:::definition Spectator ion
A **spectator ion** is an ion that is in the solution before and after the reaction, unchanged. It appears in exactly the same form on both sides of the total ionic equation.
:::

:::definition Net ionic equation
A **net ionic equation** shows only the particles that react: the total ionic equation with the spectator ions crossed out, written with the smallest whole-number coefficients.
:::

To write a net ionic equation:

1. **Write the balanced formula equation**, with state symbols.
2. **Split** each dissolved ionic compound into its ions. This is the total ionic equation.
3. **Cross out** the ions that appear unchanged on both sides: the spectators.
4. **Write what is left**, in lowest terms, and check that the atoms and the charges balance.

:::example Silver nitrate and potassium bromide
Solutions of silver nitrate and potassium bromide are mixed: $\ce{AgNO3(aq) + KBr(aq) -> AgBr(s) + KNO3(aq)}$. Write the total ionic and the net ionic equations.
@step Split the three dissolved compounds; the solid $\ce{AgBr}$ stays whole. Total ionic equation: $\ce{Ag+(aq) + NO3-(aq) + K+(aq) + Br-(aq) -> AgBr(s) + K+(aq) + NO3-(aq)}$
@step $\ce{K+(aq)}$ and $\ce{NO3-(aq)}$ appear unchanged on both sides, so they are spectators. Cross them out.
@step What is left: $\ce{Ag+(aq) + Br-(aq) -> AgBr(s)}$. Atoms: 1 Ag and 1 Br on each side. Charge: $(1+) + (1-) = 0$ on the left, and the solid has no charge.
@answer $\ce{Ag+(aq) + Br-(aq) -> AgBr(s)}$
:::

The net ionic equation says that whenever silver ions meet bromide ions in water, silver bromide precipitates, whichever compounds they came from.

:::mistake Keep the solid whole
The precipitate is a solid, not dissolved ions, so it is never split: write $\ce{AgBr(s)}$, not $\ce{Ag+ + Br-}$, on the product side. Split only the substances marked (aq) that are ionic.
:::

## Spotting the spectators

In a precipitation reaction the ions swap partners, as in lesson 3.2. One new pair forms the solid. The other pair has high solubility, so its ions simply stay dissolved: those are the spectators.

:::example Barium chloride and sodium sulfate
Solutions of barium chloride and sodium sulfate are mixed: $\ce{BaCl2(aq) + Na2SO4(aq) -> BaSO4(s) + 2NaCl(aq)}$. Which ions are spectators?
@step Total ionic equation: $\ce{Ba^2+(aq) + 2Cl-(aq) + 2Na+(aq) + SO4^2-(aq) -> BaSO4(s) + 2Na+(aq) + 2Cl-(aq)}$
@step $\ce{Ba^2+}$ and $\ce{SO4^2-}$ end up in the solid $\ce{BaSO4}$, so they react. $\ce{Na+}$ and $\ce{Cl-}$ are dissolved before and after.
@answer The spectator ions are $\ce{Na+}$ and $\ce{Cl-}$. The net ionic equation is $\ce{Ba^2+(aq) + SO4^2-(aq) -> BaSO4(s)}$.
:::

:::tip The other pair
Of the four ions, the two that make the precipitate react; the other two are the spectators. They are the ions of the dissolved product: $\ce{NaCl(aq)}$ in the last example.
:::

## Counting the ions

A formula equation counts formula units, so the coefficients of the ions come from two numbers: the coefficient in front of the compound, times the number of each ion in one formula unit. $\ce{2KI(aq)}$ gives $\ce{2K+(aq) + 2I-(aq)}$, and $\ce{3CaCl2(aq)}$ gives $3$ calcium ions and $3 \times 2 = 6$ chloride ions: $\ce{3Ca^2+(aq) + 6Cl-(aq)}$.

:::example Lead(II) nitrate and potassium iodide
Solutions of lead(II) nitrate and potassium iodide are mixed: $\ce{Pb(NO3)2(aq) + 2KI(aq) -> PbI2(s) + 2KNO3(aq)}$. Write the net ionic equation.
@step $\ce{Pb(NO3)2}$ holds one $\ce{Pb^2+}$ ion and two $\ce{NO3-}$ ions. Total ionic equation: $\ce{Pb^2+(aq) + 2NO3-(aq) + 2K+(aq) + 2I-(aq) -> PbI2(s) + 2K+(aq) + 2NO3-(aq)}$
@step The spectators are $\ce{K+}$ and $\ce{NO3-}$. Cross them out.
@answer $\ce{Pb^2+(aq) + 2I-(aq) -> PbI2(s)}$. Check: 1 Pb and 2 I on each side; charge $(2+) + 2(1-) = 0$.
:::

:::example Calcium chloride and sodium phosphate
Solutions of calcium chloride and sodium phosphate are mixed: $\ce{3CaCl2(aq) + 2Na3PO4(aq) -> Ca3(PO4)2(s) + 6NaCl(aq)}$. Write the net ionic equation.
@step Total ionic equation: $\ce{3Ca^2+(aq) + 6Cl-(aq) + 6Na+(aq) + 2PO4^3-(aq) -> Ca3(PO4)2(s) + 6Na+(aq) + 6Cl-(aq)}$
@step The spectators are $\ce{Na+}$ and $\ce{Cl-}$.
@answer $\ce{3Ca^2+(aq) + 2PO4^3-(aq) -> Ca3(PO4)2(s)}$. Check: charge $3(2+) + 2(3-) = 0$.
:::

:::mistake A polyatomic ion stays whole
$\ce{Na3PO4}$ releases $\ce{3Na+}$ and one $\ce{PO4^3-}$ ion. The phosphate ion is never split into phosphorus and oxygen. Write each polyatomic ion whole, with its charge, as in lesson 1.2.
:::

Sometimes every coefficient left after crossing out shares a factor. Divide it out to reach lowest terms.

:::example Lowest terms
Solutions of aluminium sulfate and barium nitrate are mixed: $\ce{Al2(SO4)3(aq) + 3Ba(NO3)2(aq) -> 3BaSO4(s) + 2Al(NO3)3(aq)}$. Write the net ionic equation.
@step Total ionic equation: $\ce{2Al^3+(aq) + 3SO4^2-(aq) + 3Ba^2+(aq) + 6NO3-(aq) -> 3BaSO4(s) + 2Al^3+(aq) + 6NO3-(aq)}$
@step The spectators are $\ce{Al^3+}$ and $\ce{NO3-}$. What is left is $\ce{3Ba^2+(aq) + 3SO4^2-(aq) -> 3BaSO4(s)}$.
@step Every coefficient divides by 3.
@answer $\ce{Ba^2+(aq) + SO4^2-(aq) -> BaSO4(s)}$
:::

:::mistake Balance the net ionic equation
$\ce{Fe^3+(aq) + OH-(aq) -> Fe(OH)3(s)}$ is not balanced: one hydroxide ion cannot make a solid with three. Check the atoms and the charge. One $\ce{Fe^3+}$ needs three $\ce{OH-}$ to make a neutral solid: $\ce{Fe^3+(aq) + 3OH-(aq) -> Fe(OH)3(s)}$, with charge $(3+) + 3(1-) = 0$ on the left.
:::

## From names to a net ionic equation

Often you are told only the names of the solutions and of the precipitate. You do not need the whole formula equation: the net ionic equation of a precipitation is the precipitate's own ions coming together, in the ratio its formula shows.

:::example Aluminium hydroxide
Solutions of aluminium nitrate and sodium hydroxide are mixed, and aluminium hydroxide precipitates. Write the net ionic equation, and name the spectator ions.
@step The precipitate is aluminium hydroxide. $\ce{Al^3+}$ and $\ce{OH-}$ ions make $\ce{Al(OH)3}$: three $\ce{OH-}$ for each $\ce{Al^3+}$.
@step The ions that react are $\ce{Al^3+}$ and $\ce{OH-}$. The other ions in the two solutions, $\ce{Na+}$ and $\ce{NO3-}$, stay dissolved as sodium nitrate.
@answer $\ce{Al^3+(aq) + 3OH-(aq) -> Al(OH)3(s)}$. The spectator ions are $\ce{Na+}$ and $\ce{NO3-}$.
:::

:::example Silver chromate
Solutions of silver nitrate and potassium chromate are mixed, and silver chromate precipitates. Write the net ionic equation.
@step The chromate ion is $\ce{CrO4^2-}$, so silver chromate is $\ce{Ag2CrO4}$: two $\ce{Ag+}$ for each $\ce{CrO4^2-}$.
@answer $\ce{2Ag+(aq) + CrO4^2-(aq) -> Ag2CrO4(s)}$. The spectators are $\ce{K+}$ and $\ce{NO3-}$.
:::

| Solutions mixed | Precipitate | Net ionic equation |
|---|---|---|
| copper(II) sulfate and sodium hydroxide | $\ce{Cu(OH)2}$ | $\ce{Cu^2+(aq) + 2OH-(aq) -> Cu(OH)2(s)}$ |
| calcium chloride and sodium carbonate | $\ce{CaCO3}$ | $\ce{Ca^2+(aq) + CO3^2-(aq) -> CaCO3(s)}$ |
| lead(II) nitrate and sodium chloride | $\ce{PbCl2}$ | $\ce{Pb^2+(aq) + 2Cl-(aq) -> PbCl2(s)}$ |

## Neutralisation: a strong acid and a strong base

Lesson 3.8 showed that a strong acid ionises completely, so its solution holds no acid molecules at all, only hydronium ions and the acid's anion. In a total ionic equation, write a strong acid as $\ce{H3O+}$ and its anion. A strong base such as $\ce{NaOH}$ or $\ce{KOH}$ dissociates into its metal ion and $\ce{OH-}$.

:::example Nitric acid and potassium hydroxide
Nitric acid, $\ce{HNO3(aq)}$, is neutralised by potassium hydroxide, $\ce{KOH(aq)}$. Write the total ionic equation, name the spectator ions and write the net ionic equation.
@step Formula equation: $\ce{HNO3(aq) + KOH(aq) -> KNO3(aq) + H2O(l)}$
@step Total ionic equation: $\ce{H3O+(aq) + NO3-(aq) + K+(aq) + OH-(aq) -> 2H2O(l) + K+(aq) + NO3-(aq)}$
@step The hydronium and hydroxide ions react to form water. $\ce{K+}$ and $\ce{NO3-}$ are spectators.
@answer $\ce{H3O+(aq) + OH-(aq) -> 2H2O(l)}$. The spectator ions are $\ce{K+}$ and $\ce{NO3-}$.
:::

Why two water molecules, when the formula equation shows one? A hydronium ion is a water molecule carrying an extra hydrogen ion, $\ce{H+}$. It hands that $\ce{H+}$ to the hydroxide ion, which becomes a water molecule, and is left as a water molecule itself. The atoms balance: 4 H and 2 O on each side.

:::example Hydrochloric acid and barium hydroxide
Hydrochloric acid neutralises barium hydroxide solution: $\ce{2HCl(aq) + Ba(OH)2(aq) -> BaCl2(aq) + 2H2O(l)}$. Write the net ionic equation.
@step Each $\ce{HCl}$ gives one $\ce{H3O+}$ and one $\ce{Cl-}$; $\ce{Ba(OH)2}$ gives one $\ce{Ba^2+}$ and two $\ce{OH-}$.
@step Total ionic equation: $\ce{2H3O+(aq) + 2Cl-(aq) + Ba^2+(aq) + 2OH-(aq) -> 4H2O(l) + Ba^2+(aq) + 2Cl-(aq)}$
@step Cross out $\ce{Ba^2+}$ and $\ce{Cl-}$: $\ce{2H3O+(aq) + 2OH-(aq) -> 4H2O(l)}$. Every coefficient divides by 2.
@answer $\ce{H3O+(aq) + OH-(aq) -> 2H2O(l)}$
:::

Every neutralisation of a strong acid by a strong base has this same net ionic equation. The spectators change from one reaction to the next; the reaction itself never does.

:::mistake Hydronium and hydroxide are not spectators
$\ce{H3O+}$ and $\ce{OH-}$ are on the left of the total ionic equation but not on the right: they have become water. They are the ions that react. The spectators are the acid's anion and the base's metal ion.
:::

:::summary
- A **formula equation** shows whole formulas. A **total ionic equation** splits every dissolved ionic compound, and every strong acid, into its ions; solids and water stay whole.
- **Spectator ions** appear unchanged on both sides of the total ionic equation. Crossing them out leaves the **net ionic equation**, written in lowest terms.
- The number of each ion is the compound's coefficient times the number of that ion in one formula unit. Polyatomic ions stay whole.
- Check every net ionic equation for atoms and for charge: $\ce{Fe^3+(aq) + 3OH-(aq) -> Fe(OH)3(s)}$.
- A precipitation's net ionic equation is the precipitate's ions forming the solid, such as $\ce{Pb^2+(aq) + 2I-(aq) -> PbI2(s)}$.
- A strong acid is written as $\ce{H3O+}$ and its anion. Any strong acid with a strong base: $\ce{H3O+(aq) + OH-(aq) -> 2H2O(l)}$.
:::
