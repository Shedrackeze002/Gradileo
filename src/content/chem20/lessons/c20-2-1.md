Open a bottle of perfume in one corner of a room, and before long someone in the far corner can smell it. Pump air into a bicycle tyre and the tyre grows firm; leave a balloon in a cold car and it goes soft. Gases behave very differently from liquids and solids, yet one simple picture explains all of it.

In this lesson you will describe the properties of gases and meet the **kinetic molecular theory**, which pictures a gas as tiny particles in constant motion. Then you will use it to explain how gases behave, and see when real gases follow it closely and when they do not.

## The properties of gases

A solid keeps its own shape, and a liquid keeps its own volume. A gas keeps neither. Every gas, whatever it is made of, shares four properties:

- **It fills its container.** A gas has no fixed shape or volume: it spreads out to fill whatever space it is given, and takes the shape of its container.
- **It is easily compressed.** Pushing on a gas squeezes it into a much smaller space, as a syringe with its tip blocked shows. Liquids and solids hardly compress at all.
- **It has a low density.** A litre of air has a mass of only about $1.2\ \text{g}$; a litre of liquid water has a mass of about $1000\ \text{g}$.
- **It mixes and diffuses.** Two gases mix completely and evenly without being stirred, which is how the smell of cooking spreads from the kitchen through a whole house.

Tiny helium atoms and much larger carbon dioxide molecules share these properties alike. To see why, we need a picture of what the particles of a gas are doing.

## The kinetic molecular theory

The word *kinetic* comes from the Greek word for motion. The theory pictures a gas as a huge number of tiny particles, atoms or molecules, that never stop moving.

:::definition Kinetic molecular theory
The **kinetic molecular theory** is a model that explains the behaviour of gases by the motion of their particles. It describes an ideal gas with five ideas.
:::

1. Gas particles move constantly and randomly, in straight lines between collisions.
2. Gas particles are very small compared with the distances between them.
3. Collisions between gas particles, and with the walls, lose no kinetic energy overall.
4. In an ideal gas, the particles do not attract or repel each other.
5. The average kinetic energy of the particles is proportional to the Kelvin temperature.

The Kelvin scale, which starts at the coldest temperature possible, is the subject of the next lesson. For now, a higher temperature means faster particles.

:::definition Kinetic energy
**Kinetic energy** is the energy a particle has because it is moving. A particle of mass $m$ moving at speed $v$ has kinetic energy $E_\text{k} = \tfrac{1}{2}mv^2$, so a particle has more kinetic energy the faster it moves and the heavier it is.
:::

:::definition Elastic collision
In an **elastic collision**, no kinetic energy is lost overall. One particle may speed up and the other slow down, but their total kinetic energy is the same after the collision as before. The theory treats every collision in a gas, between particles or with the walls, as elastic.
:::

:::definition Ideal gas
An **ideal gas** is an imaginary gas that follows the kinetic molecular theory exactly: its particles take up no space of their own and neither attract nor repel each other. Many real gases come close to it under everyday conditions.
:::

The second idea says that a gas is mostly empty space. About $18\ \text{g}$ of liquid water fills about $18\ \text{mL}$. Boiled to steam at $100^\circ\text{C}$ and normal air pressure, the same molecules fill about $30\ \text{L}$, well over a thousand times as much space. The molecules have not grown; the gaps between them have.

The particles are fast, too: at room temperature the molecules in air move at hundreds of metres per second. But each one collides with others billions of times a second, so its path is a zigzag of very short straight lines.

:::mistake Misreading the theory
Gas particles do not stand still until the gas is heated, and they do not slow down a little each time they hit the walls until they stop: their collisions lose no kinetic energy overall, so they keep moving. Nor is most of the volume of a gas taken up by the particles themselves; it is empty space. And the particles of an ideal gas do not attract each other at all.
:::

## Explaining how gases behave

Each property of gases follows from the five ideas. Start with pressure, the push a gas exerts on the walls of its container.

:::example Why a gas exerts pressure
Use the kinetic molecular theory to explain why a gas exerts pressure on the walls of its container.
@step The particles move constantly and randomly, so they keep running into the walls.
@step Each collision gives the wall a tiny push. An enormous number of collisions every second add up to a steady push over the whole wall.
@answer Its particles keep colliding with the walls, and each collision pushes on them.
:::

A gas can be compressed much more easily than a liquid or a solid because its particles are far apart, with mostly empty space between them. Squeezing a gas only shrinks the empty space. In a liquid or a solid the particles are already touching, so there is almost no space to remove. The same empty space explains why a gas has such a low density: a litre of gas holds far fewer particles than a litre of liquid.

A gas fills its container because nothing holds its particles together. They move in straight lines until they hit something, and they do not attract each other, so they spread out until they meet the walls.

:::example Heating a sealed can
A sealed, rigid can of gas is heated. Use the kinetic molecular theory to explain why the pressure inside it rises.
@step A higher temperature means a greater average kinetic energy, so the particles move faster.
@step The can is rigid, so the particles still have the same space to move in. Moving faster, they reach the walls more often, and each collision is harder.
@answer The particles move faster, so they hit the walls more often and harder. The pressure rises.
:::

:::example A balloon in the cold
A balloon is taken from a warm room into the cold outdoors. Use the kinetic molecular theory to explain why it shrinks.
@step In the cold, the particles inside slow down, so they hit the rubber less often and less hard.
@step The gas inside now pushes out less than the outside air and the stretched rubber push in, so the balloon is squeezed smaller. As it shrinks, the particles reach the rubber more often again, until the pushes balance.
@answer The particles slow down, so at the same pressure they need less room. The balloon shrinks.
:::

:::definition Diffusion
**Diffusion** is the spreading of the particles of one substance through another by their own random motion, until the two are evenly mixed.
:::

Why does the smell of perfume spread across a room even when the air seems still? The particles move constantly and randomly, so they spread out on their own; no draught is needed. Diffusion is much slower than the particles' speeds suggest, because each perfume molecule keeps colliding with air molecules and changing direction.

| What we observe | The explanation from the theory |
|---|---|
| A gas exerts pressure on its container | Its particles keep colliding with the walls, and each collision pushes on them. |
| A gas is easily compressed | Its particles are far apart, with mostly empty space between them. |
| Heating a rigid container raises the pressure | The particles move faster, so they hit the walls more often and harder. |
| A balloon shrinks in the cold | The particles slow down, so at the same pressure they need less room. |
| A smell spreads through still air | The particles move constantly and randomly, so they spread out on their own. |

:::mistake Particles do not change size
Gas particles never grow when they are heated or shrink when they are cooled. A balloon shrinks in the cold because its particles slow down, not because they get smaller, and a gas is compressed by removing empty space, not by squashing its particles. What changes is how fast the particles move and how often they hit things.
:::

## Kinetic energy, mass and speed

The particles in a gas do not all move at the same speed. Collisions keep speeding some up and slowing others down, so at any moment there is a spread of speeds. That is why the theory speaks of the **average** kinetic energy, and that average depends only on the Kelvin temperature. It does not depend on which gas it is, on its pressure, or on how much gas there is.

So at the same temperature, molecules of hydrogen and of carbon dioxide have the same average kinetic energy. Since $E_\text{k} = \tfrac{1}{2}mv^2$, the lighter molecules must move faster to carry the same energy: a smaller $m$ needs a larger $v$.

:::example Energy and speed
Samples of hydrogen, $\ce{H2}$, and oxygen, $\ce{O2}$, are at the same temperature. Compare the average kinetic energies of their molecules, and say which molecules move faster on average.
@step At the same temperature, every gas has the same average kinetic energy, $\tfrac{1}{2}mv^2$.
@step The atomic masses are about 1 for hydrogen and 16 for oxygen, so an $\ce{O2}$ molecule (about 32) has about 16 times the mass of an $\ce{H2}$ molecule (about 2).
@step For the same $\tfrac{1}{2}mv^2$, a mass 16 times smaller needs a $v^2$ 16 times larger, so a speed about $\sqrt{16} = 4$ times larger.
@answer Their average kinetic energies are the same, but the $\ce{H2}$ molecules move faster: about 4 times as fast as the $\ce{O2}$ molecules, on average.
:::

A classic demonstration shows the difference. Cotton wool soaked in ammonia solution is placed in one end of a long glass tube, and cotton wool soaked in hydrochloric acid in the other. Ammonia gas and hydrogen chloride gas diffuse towards each other, and where they meet they react to form a white ring of solid ammonium chloride: $\ce{NH3 + HCl -> NH4Cl}$. The ring forms nearer the hydrochloric acid end, because the lighter $\ce{NH3}$ molecules (about 17) travel farther in the same time than the heavier $\ce{HCl}$ molecules (about 36.5).

:::mistake "Heavier particles have more energy"
At the same temperature, heavier gas particles do **not** have a greater average kinetic energy. Every gas at the same temperature has the same average kinetic energy; heavier particles simply move more slowly.
:::

## Ideal gases and real gases

No real gas is perfectly ideal. Its particles do take up some space, and they do attract each other a little, through the intermolecular forces of the previous unit. Under everyday conditions these effects are small, but two things make them matter more:

- **High pressure.** Squeezed together, the particles are closer, so their own volume becomes a noticeable part of the space, and they are near enough to attract each other.
- **Low temperature.** Slow particles linger near one another, so their attractions can pull them together. Cool a gas enough and the attractions win: it condenses to a liquid.

:::tip When is a real gas most ideal?
A real gas behaves most like an ideal gas at **high temperature and low pressure**. At low pressure its particles are far apart, so their own volume hardly matters; at high temperature they move fast, so their attractions hardly matter.
:::

:::example Which sample is closer to ideal?
One sample of nitrogen is at $-100^\circ\text{C}$ and a high pressure. Another is at $200^\circ\text{C}$ and a low pressure. Which behaves more like an ideal gas?
@step The second sample is hotter, so its particles move faster and their attractions matter less.
@step It is also at a lower pressure, so its particles are farther apart and their own volume matters less.
@answer The sample at $200^\circ\text{C}$ and low pressure behaves more like an ideal gas.
:::

:::summary
- Gases fill their containers, are easily compressed, have low densities, and mix and diffuse.
- The kinetic molecular theory: gas particles move constantly and randomly in straight lines; they are tiny compared with the distances between them; their collisions are elastic; in an ideal gas they neither attract nor repel; and their average kinetic energy is proportional to the Kelvin temperature.
- Pressure comes from particles colliding with the walls. Heating a rigid container makes them hit the walls more often and harder, so the pressure rises.
- A gas is easily compressed because it is mostly empty space; gas particles never change size.
- Diffusion is the spreading of particles by their own random motion.
- At the same temperature, all gases have the same average kinetic energy, $\tfrac{1}{2}mv^2$, so lighter particles move faster.
- Real gases behave most like an ideal gas at high temperature and low pressure.
:::
