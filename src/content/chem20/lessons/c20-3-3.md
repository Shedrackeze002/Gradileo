Read the label on a bottle of vinegar, a tube of ointment or a bag of plant food and you will usually find a percentage. A water-testing report gives its results in parts per million instead. Both numbers answer the same question: how much of one substance is mixed into another?

In this lesson you will describe the **concentration** of a solution as three kinds of percentage, find the mass of solute that a solution needs, and use **parts per million** for solutions so dilute that a percentage would be an awkwardly small number.

## Concentration

Stir a spoonful of sugar into a cup of tea and the tea tastes sweet. Stir the same spoonful into a large jug of water and you can hardly taste it. The amount of solute is the same; what differs is how much solution it is spread through.

:::definition Concentration
The **concentration** of a solution is the amount of solute in a given quantity of solution. A **concentrated** solution has a large amount of solute in that quantity; a **dilute** solution has a small amount.
:::

Concentration does not depend on how much of the solution you take. Pour a cup from a well-stirred jug and the cup has the same concentration as the jug: it holds less solute, but in less solution. That is why every concentration is a ratio, an amount of solute divided by a quantity of solution.

Solutes and solutions can be measured by mass or by volume, and the easiest choice depends on what the solution is and what it is for. So chemists use several units of concentration. This lesson uses percentages and parts per million; lesson 3.4 counts the solute in moles.

## Percent concentration

A percent concentration gives the amount of solute in 100 units of solution. There are three kinds, which differ in how the solute and the solution are measured. The letter w stands for weight, an everyday word for mass, and V stands for volume.

| Concentration | Solute measured by | Solution measured by | Meaning |
|---|---|---|---|
| percent by mass per volume, % (w/V) | mass | volume | grams of solute per $100\ \text{mL}$ of solution |
| percent by volume, % (V/V) | volume | volume | millilitres of solute per $100\ \text{mL}$ of solution |
| percent by mass, % (w/w) | mass | mass | grams of solute per $100\ \text{g}$ of solution |

:::note Other names
Some books write % (m/V) for % (w/V), and % (m/m) for % (w/w). They mean the same thing.
:::

A solid dissolved in water is most easily measured as a mass of solute and a volume of solution, so % (w/V) is common for solutions made from solids.

:::definition Percent by mass per volume
Percent by mass per volume, % (w/V), is grams of solute per $100\ \text{mL}$ of solution: % (w/V) $= \dfrac{\text{mass of solute (g)}}{\text{volume of solution (mL)}} \times 100\%$.
:::

:::example A sugar solution
$18.4\ \text{g}$ of sugar is dissolved in water to make $350\ \text{mL}$ of solution. What is the concentration in % (w/V), to the nearest hundredth?
@step % (w/V) $= \dfrac{\text{mass of solute (g)}}{\text{volume of solution (mL)}} \times 100\%$
@step $\dfrac{18.4\ \text{g}}{350\ \text{mL}} \times 100\% = 5.257\%$
@answer $5.26\%$ (w/V). Every $100\ \text{mL}$ of the solution holds $5.26\ \text{g}$ of sugar.
:::

:::

When the solute is a liquid, such as ethanol mixed with water, it is easier to measure both by volume.

:::definition Percent by volume
Percent by volume, % (V/V), is millilitres of solute per $100\ \text{mL}$ of solution: % (V/V) $= \dfrac{\text{volume of solute}}{\text{volume of solution}} \times 100\%$.
:::

:::example Ethanol in water
A solution contains $90\ \text{mL}$ of ethanol in $650\ \text{mL}$ of solution. What is its concentration in % (V/V), to the nearest tenth?
@step $\dfrac{90\ \text{mL}}{650\ \text{mL}} \times 100\% = 13.846\%$
@answer $13.8\%$ (V/V)
:::

:::note Volumes do not always add
Mixing $50\ \text{mL}$ of ethanol with $50\ \text{mL}$ of water gives slightly **less** than $100\ \text{mL}$ of solution, because the two kinds of molecule pack together more closely than either does alone. That is why % (V/V) uses the volume of the **solution**, measured after mixing, not the sum of the volumes poured in.
:::

:::

Percent by mass compares two masses. Masses are easy to measure precisely on a balance, and unlike a volume, a mass does not change when the solution warms up or cools down.

:::definition Percent by mass
Percent by mass, % (w/w), is grams of solute per $100\ \text{g}$ of solution: % (w/w) $= \dfrac{\text{mass of solute}}{\text{mass of solution}} \times 100\%$.
:::

Questions often give the mass of the water rather than the mass of the solution. The solute is part of the solution, so add the two masses first.

:::example Salt in water
$24.5\ \text{g}$ of salt is dissolved in $160\ \text{g}$ of water. What is the concentration in % (w/w), to the nearest tenth?
@step Mass of solution: $24.5 + 160 = 184.5\ \text{g}$
@step $\dfrac{24.5}{184.5} \times 100\% = 13.28\%$
@answer $13.3\%$ (w/w)
:::

:::mistake The mass of the solution
The solution is the solute **plus** the water. Dividing by the mass of the water alone, $\dfrac{24.5}{160} \times 100\% = 15.3\%$, gives too large a concentration. Read whether a question gives you the mass of the water or the mass of the solution.
:::

You can also turn a percentage around to plan a solution. A 6.2% (w/V) solution has $6.2\ \text{g}$ of solute in every $100\ \text{mL}$, so a larger volume needs proportionally more solute.

:::example Making a solution
What mass of solute is needed to make $350\ \text{mL}$ of a 6.2% (w/V) solution, to the nearest tenth?
@step A 6.2% (w/V) solution has $6.2\ \text{g}$ of solute in every $100\ \text{mL}$.
@step $m = \dfrac{6.2\ \text{g}}{100\ \text{mL}} \times 350\ \text{mL} = 21.7\ \text{g}$
@answer $21.7\ \text{g}$ of solute, dissolved in water and made up to $350\ \text{mL}$ of solution.
:::

:::tip Check the size
$350\ \text{mL}$ is $3.5$ lots of $100\ \text{mL}$, so the mass must be $3.5 \times 6.2\ \text{g}$, a little over $20\ \text{g}$. A quick estimate like this catches a misplaced decimal point.
:::

## Parts per million

Swimming-pool water may hold only a few milligrams of chlorine in each litre, and a drinking-water sample even less of an impurity such as lead. As percentages these concentrations are tiny numbers: $2\ \text{mg}$ in a litre of water is only $0.0002\%$. Chemists use **parts per million** (ppm) instead, which turns them into everyday numbers.

:::definition Parts per million
Parts per million are grams of solute per million grams of solution: ppm $= \dfrac{\text{mass of solute}}{\text{mass of solution}} \times 10^6$, with both masses in the same unit.
:::

One part per million is the same ratio as $1\ \text{g}$ in $1\,000\,000\ \text{g}$, or $1\ \text{mg}$ in $1\ \text{kg}$. For even smaller amounts chemists use parts per billion (ppb), and $1\ \text{ppm} = 1000\ \text{ppb}$.

A dilute solution in water is almost all water, and a litre of water has a mass of about $1\ \text{kg}$, which is $1\,000\,000\ \text{mg}$. So one milligram of solute in a litre of such a solution is one milligram in a million milligrams: one part per million. That gives a much quicker rule for water samples.

:::definition Parts per million in water
For a dilute solution in water, $1\ \text{ppm} = 1\ \text{mg}$ of solute per litre of solution ($1\ \text{mg/kg}$).
:::

:::example A water sample
A $2.4\ \text{L}$ water sample contains $7.35\ \text{mg}$ of nitrate. What is the concentration of nitrate in parts per million, to the nearest hundredth?
@step For a dilute solution in water, parts per million is milligrams per litre.
@step $\dfrac{7.35\ \text{mg}}{2.4\ \text{L}} = 3.0625\ \text{ppm}$
@answer $3.06\ \text{ppm}$
:::

When a sample is weighed rather than measured by volume, use the definition with the two masses.

:::example A weighed sample
A $180\ \text{g}$ sample of soil water contains $0.0021\ \text{g}$ of lead. What is the concentration in parts per million, to the nearest tenth?
@step ppm $= \dfrac{\text{mass of solute}}{\text{mass of solution}} \times 10^6$
@step $\dfrac{0.0021\ \text{g}}{180\ \text{g}} \times 10^6 = 11.67\ \text{ppm}$
@answer $11.7\ \text{ppm}$
:::

:::mistake Same units, and the factor of a million
Both masses must be in the same unit before you divide: $2.1\ \text{mg}$ in $180\ \text{g}$ is $0.0021\ \text{g}$ in $180\ \text{g}$. Then remember to multiply by $10^6$. The fraction $\dfrac{0.0021}{180} = 0.0000117$ is the concentration as a plain fraction, not in parts per million.
:::

:::check c20-ppm 2
:::

The rule for water also works backwards. Milligrams per litre times litres gives milligrams, so you can find the mass of a solute in a large volume of water, such as a swimming pool.

:::example Chlorine in a pool
A pool holds $35000\ \text{L}$ of water with 1.8 ppm of chlorine. What mass of chlorine, in grams, does it contain, to the nearest tenth?
@step $1.8\ \text{ppm}$ is $1.8\ \text{mg}$ in every litre: $1.8\ \text{mg/L} \times 35000\ \text{L} = 63000\ \text{mg}$
@step Change milligrams to grams, using $1000\ \text{mg} = 1\ \text{g}$: $63000\ \text{mg} = 63.0\ \text{g}$
@answer $63.0\ \text{g}$ of chlorine
:::

:::mistake Milligrams are not grams
Multiplying ppm by litres gives **milligrams**. Stopping there would report $63000\ \text{g}$ of chlorine, a thousand times too much. Divide by 1000 to change milligrams to grams.
:::

:::note Limits are stated in the question
Health authorities set the highest concentrations they allow for substances such as lead or nitrate in drinking water. These limits differ from place to place and change over time, so this course does not quote them. If a question compares a sample with a limit, it states the limit to use.
:::

:::summary
- The **concentration** of a solution is the amount of solute in a given quantity of solution.
- **Percent by mass per volume**, % (w/V), is grams of solute per $100\ \text{mL}$ of solution.
- **Percent by volume**, % (V/V), is millilitres of solute per $100\ \text{mL}$ of solution.
- **Percent by mass**, % (w/w), is grams of solute per $100\ \text{g}$ of solution. The mass of a solution is the mass of the solute plus the mass of the water.
- Each percentage is $\dfrac{\text{solute}}{\text{solution}} \times 100\%$. To find the mass of solute for a % (w/V) solution, scale the grams per $100\ \text{mL}$ up to the volume you need.
- **Parts per million**: ppm $= \dfrac{\text{mass of solute}}{\text{mass of solution}} \times 10^6$, with both masses in the same unit.
- For a dilute solution in water, $1\ \text{ppm} = 1\ \text{mg}$ of solute per litre ($1\ \text{mg/kg}$). Milligrams per litre times litres gives milligrams.
:::
