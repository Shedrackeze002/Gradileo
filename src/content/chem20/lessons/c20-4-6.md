A bicycle factory has 50 frames and 80 wheels in stock. Each bicycle needs one frame and two wheels, so the factory can build only 40 bicycles before the wheels run out, and 10 frames are left over. It does not matter how many frames there are: the wheels decide how many bicycles get built.

In this lesson you will find which reactant runs out first in a reaction, use it to find how much product forms, and work out how much of the other reactant is left over.

## Limiting and excess reagents

In lesson 4.5 one reactant was always "in excess", so the other one decided how much product formed. When a question gives you amounts of **both** reactants, you have to find out which one runs out first.

:::definition Limiting reagent
The **limiting reagent** is the reactant that is used up first in a reaction. When it is gone the reaction stops, so it decides how much product forms.
:::

:::definition Excess reagent
An **excess reagent** is a reactant that is not used up completely. Some of it is left over when the reaction stops.
:::

In the bicycle factory, the wheels are the limiting reagent and the frames are in excess, even though there are fewer frames than wheels. What matters is how many of each one bicycle **needs**, and in a reaction the coefficients of the balanced equation say that. The word *reagent* simply means a substance used in a reaction; you will also see *limiting reactant*.

## Finding the limiting reagent

Compare what one reactant needs with what is there:

1. Find the amount in moles of each reactant.
2. Use the mole ratio to find how much of the second reactant the first one needs.
3. If the second reactant is short of that amount, it runs out first and is limiting. If there is more than enough of it, the first reactant is limiting and the second is in excess.

:::example Making ammonia
A mixture for the reaction $\ce{N2 + 3H2 -> 2NH3}$ holds $2.00\ \text{mol}$ of $\ce{N2}$ and $4.50\ \text{mol}$ of $\ce{H2}$. Which reactant is the limiting reagent?
@step The $\ce{N2}$ needs $2.00 \times \dfrac{3}{1} = 6.00\ \text{mol}$ of $\ce{H2}$.
@step There is only $4.50\ \text{mol}$ of $\ce{H2}$, which is not enough, so the hydrogen runs out first.
@answer $\ce{H2}$ is the limiting reagent, and $\ce{N2}$ is in excess.
:::

:::example The thermite reaction
A mixture for the reaction $\ce{2Al + Fe2O3 -> Al2O3 + 2Fe}$ holds $1.50\ \text{mol}$ of $\ce{Al}$ and $1.00\ \text{mol}$ of $\ce{Fe2O3}$. Which reactant is the limiting reagent?
@step The $\ce{Al}$ needs $1.50 \times \dfrac{1}{2} = 0.750\ \text{mol}$ of $\ce{Fe2O3}$.
@step There is $1.00\ \text{mol}$ of $\ce{Fe2O3}$, which is more than enough, so the aluminium runs out first.
@answer $\ce{Al}$ is the limiting reagent, and $\ce{Fe2O3}$ is in excess.
:::

:::mistake The smaller amount is not always limiting
In the ammonia example there is less nitrogen ($2.00\ \text{mol}$) than hydrogen ($4.50\ \text{mol}$), yet the hydrogen runs out first, because each $\ce{N2}$ needs three $\ce{H2}$. Never pick the limiting reagent by comparing the amounts, or the masses, directly. Use the mole ratio.
:::

:::

:::tip A shortcut: divide by the coefficients
Divide the amount of each reactant by its coefficient in the balanced equation. The reactant with the **smaller** result runs out first. For the ammonia example: $\dfrac{2.00}{1} = 2.00$ for $\ce{N2}$ and $\dfrac{4.50}{3} = 1.50$ for $\ce{H2}$, so $\ce{H2}$ is limiting. Each result counts how many "rounds" of the reaction that reactant can supply.
:::

When the question gives masses, change both masses to moles first. Masses alone cannot be compared, because the substances have different molar masses.

:::example Aluminium in copper(II) chloride
A mixture for the reaction $\ce{2Al + 3CuCl2 -> 2AlCl3 + 3Cu}$ holds $5.40\ \text{g}$ of $\ce{Al}$ and $30.0\ \text{g}$ of $\ce{CuCl2}$. Which reactant is the limiting reagent? Use these molar masses: Al $26.98\ \text{g/mol}$, Cu $63.55\ \text{g/mol}$, Cl $35.45\ \text{g/mol}$.
@step $n(\ce{Al}) = \dfrac{5.40}{26.98} = 0.2001\ \text{mol}$
@step $M(\ce{CuCl2}) = 63.55 + 2(35.45) = 134.45\ \text{g/mol}$, so $n(\ce{CuCl2}) = \dfrac{30.0}{134.45} = 0.2231\ \text{mol}$
@step The $\ce{Al}$ needs $0.2001 \times \dfrac{3}{2} = 0.3002\ \text{mol}$ of $\ce{CuCl2}$, and there is only $0.2231\ \text{mol}$.
@step Shortcut check: $\dfrac{0.2001}{2} = 0.1001$ for $\ce{Al}$ and $\dfrac{0.2231}{3} = 0.07437$ for $\ce{CuCl2}$; the smaller one is $\ce{CuCl2}$.
@answer $\ce{CuCl2}$ is the limiting reagent, and $\ce{Al}$ is in excess.
:::

:::

## How much product forms?

The reaction stops when the limiting reagent is gone, however much of the excess reagent is left. So the amount of product comes from the **limiting reagent only**: find it first, then use its amount in the mole ratio.

:::example Zinc chloride
A mixture for the reaction $\ce{Zn + 2HCl -> ZnCl2 + H2}$ holds $0.500\ \text{mol}$ of $\ce{Zn}$ and $0.800\ \text{mol}$ of $\ce{HCl}$. How many moles of $\ce{ZnCl2}$ can form, to the nearest thousandth?
@step The $\ce{Zn}$ needs $0.500 \times \dfrac{2}{1} = 1.000\ \text{mol}$ of $\ce{HCl}$, and there is only $0.800\ \text{mol}$, so $\ce{HCl}$ is limiting.
@step Shortcut: $\dfrac{0.500}{1} = 0.500$ for $\ce{Zn}$ and $\dfrac{0.800}{2} = 0.400$ for $\ce{HCl}$; the smaller is $\ce{HCl}$.
@step $n(\ce{ZnCl2}) = 0.800 \times \dfrac{1}{2} = 0.400\ \text{mol}$
@answer $0.400\ \text{mol}$ of zinc chloride
:::

:::mistake Using the excess reagent
Starting from the zinc in the last example gives $0.500\ \text{mol}$ of $\ce{ZnCl2}$, which is more than can form: the acid would run out after $0.400\ \text{mol}$. Working from the excess reagent always gives too much product.
:::

:::

With masses, the path is: both masses to moles, find the limiting reagent, use the mole ratio from the limiting reagent to the product, and change the product's moles to a mass.

:::example Burning methane
A mixture for the reaction $\ce{CH4 + 2O2 -> CO2 + 2H2O}$ holds $16.0\ \text{g}$ of $\ce{CH4}$ and $48.0\ \text{g}$ of $\ce{O2}$. What mass of $\ce{CO2}$ can form, to the nearest tenth? Use these molar masses: C $12.01\ \text{g/mol}$, H $1.01\ \text{g/mol}$, O $16.00\ \text{g/mol}$.
@step $M(\ce{CH4}) = 12.01 + 4(1.01) = 16.05\ \text{g/mol}$, so $n(\ce{CH4}) = \dfrac{16.0}{16.05} = 0.9969\ \text{mol}$
@step $M(\ce{O2}) = 2(16.00) = 32.00\ \text{g/mol}$, so $n(\ce{O2}) = \dfrac{48.0}{32.00} = 1.500\ \text{mol}$
@step The $\ce{CH4}$ needs $0.9969 \times \dfrac{2}{1} = 1.994\ \text{mol}$ of $\ce{O2}$, but there is only $1.500\ \text{mol}$, so $\ce{O2}$ is limiting.
@step $n(\ce{CO2}) = 1.500 \times \dfrac{1}{2} = 0.7500\ \text{mol}$
@step $M(\ce{CO2}) = 12.01 + 2(16.00) = 44.01\ \text{g/mol}$, so $m = 0.7500 \times 44.01 = 33.01\ \text{g}$
@answer $33.0\ \text{g}$ of carbon dioxide
:::

## What is left over?

The excess reagent does not all react. To find how much is left, work out how much of it the limiting reagent uses up, and subtract that from what was there at the start:

$$\text{left over} = \text{amount at the start} - \text{amount used}$$

:::example Methane left over
In the methane example, what mass of the excess reagent is left over when the reaction stops? Give the answer to the nearest hundredth.
@step $\ce{O2}$ is limiting, so $\ce{CH4}$ is the excess reagent.
@step $\ce{CH4}$ used: $1.500 \times \dfrac{1}{2} = 0.7500\ \text{mol}$
@step Left: $0.9969 - 0.7500 = 0.2469\ \text{mol}$
@step $m = 0.2469 \times 16.05 = 3.96\ \text{g}$
@answer $3.96\ \text{g}$ of methane is left over.
:::

:::tip Check with conservation of mass
Mass is conserved, so the masses at the end must add up to the $16.0 + 48.0 = 64.0\ \text{g}$ at the start. The $1.500\ \text{mol}$ of $\ce{O2}$ also makes $1.500 \times \dfrac{2}{2} = 1.500\ \text{mol}$ of water, which is $1.500 \times 18.02 = 27.03\ \text{g}$. At the end: $33.01 + 27.03 + 3.96 = 64.00\ \text{g}$.
:::

:::example Magnesium in hydrochloric acid
A mixture for the reaction $\ce{Mg + 2HCl -> MgCl2 + H2}$ holds $3.00\ \text{g}$ of $\ce{Mg}$ and $4.90\ \text{g}$ of $\ce{HCl}$. When the reaction stops, what mass of the excess reagent is left over, to the nearest hundredth? Use these molar masses: Mg $24.31\ \text{g/mol}$, H $1.01\ \text{g/mol}$, Cl $35.45\ \text{g/mol}$.
@step $n(\ce{Mg}) = \dfrac{3.00}{24.31} = 0.1234\ \text{mol}$
@step $M(\ce{HCl}) = 1.01 + 35.45 = 36.46\ \text{g/mol}$, so $n(\ce{HCl}) = \dfrac{4.90}{36.46} = 0.1344\ \text{mol}$
@step $\dfrac{n}{\text{coefficient}}$ is $0.1234$ for $\ce{Mg}$ and $\dfrac{0.1344}{2} = 0.06720$ for $\ce{HCl}$, so $\ce{HCl}$ is limiting and $\ce{Mg}$ is in excess.
@step $\ce{Mg}$ used: $0.1344 \times \dfrac{1}{2} = 0.06720\ \text{mol}$
@step Left: $(0.1234 - 0.06720) \times 24.31 = 1.37\ \text{g}$
@answer $1.37\ \text{g}$ of magnesium is left over.
:::

:::mistake The amount used is not the amount left
$0.06720\ \text{mol}$ in the last example is the magnesium that **reacts**. The question asks what is left, so subtract it from the $0.1234\ \text{mol}$ there at the start. Also make sure you subtract amounts of the **same** substance: magnesium used from magnesium at the start.
:::

:::note Excess on purpose
Chemists often add an excess of one reactant on purpose. A cheap reactant, such as oxygen from the air or an inexpensive acid, is added in excess to make sure that every bit of a costly or important reactant reacts. A furnace burns its fuel with extra air for the same reason: with too little oxygen, the fuel burns incompletely and forms poisonous carbon monoxide.
:::

:::summary
- The **limiting reagent** is used up first and decides how much product forms. An **excess reagent** is left over when the reaction stops.
- To find the limiting reagent, change both quantities to moles, use the mole ratio to find how much of the second reactant the first one needs, and compare it with what is there.
- Shortcut: divide each amount by its coefficient. The smaller result belongs to the limiting reagent.
- Never compare the amounts or masses directly: the coefficients decide what each reactant needs.
- The amount of product comes from the limiting reagent only.
- Excess left over = amount at the start − amount used, both for the excess reagent.
:::
