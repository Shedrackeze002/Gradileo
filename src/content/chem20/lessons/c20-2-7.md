Each gas law so far compares a gas before and after a change, while two of its quantities stay fixed. The **ideal gas law** is different. It links all four quantities, pressure, volume, amount and temperature, for a gas in **one** state, so you can find any one of them from the other three.

In this lesson you will use $PV = nRT$ to find a pressure, volume, amount or temperature, then a mass and a molar mass, and use the molar mass to identify an unknown gas. Finally you will see when real gases stop behaving like the ideal gas that the law describes.

## One law for four quantities

Three of the gas laws describe how the volume of a gas depends on one quantity at a time:

| Law | Held constant | The volume of a gas is |
|---|---|---|
| Boyle's law | $n$ and $T$ | inversely proportional to pressure: $V \propto \dfrac{1}{P}$ |
| Charles's law | $n$ and $P$ | directly proportional to Kelvin temperature: $V \propto T$ |
| Avogadro's law | $T$ and $P$ | directly proportional to amount: $V \propto n$ |

Put together, $V \propto \dfrac{nT}{P}$. A constant, $R$, turns the proportion into an equation, $V = \dfrac{nRT}{P}$, and multiplying both sides by $P$ gives the ideal gas law.

:::definition Ideal gas law
For a gas in one state, $PV = nRT$, where $P$ is the pressure, $V$ the volume, $n$ the amount of gas, $T$ the Kelvin temperature and $R$ the gas constant.
:::

:::definition Gas constant
The **gas constant** is $R = 8.314\ \text{kPa·L/(mol·K)}$. It is the same for every gas. Its units decide the units of everything else in the law.
:::

| Symbol | Quantity | Unit |
|---|---|---|
| $P$ | pressure | kilopascals, $\text{kPa}$ |
| $V$ | volume | litres, $\text{L}$ |
| $n$ | amount of gas | moles, $\text{mol}$ |
| $T$ | temperature | kelvins, $\text{K}$ |
| $R$ | gas constant | $8.314\ \text{kPa·L/(mol·K)}$ |

:::note The molar volume again
For one mole at STP, $V = \dfrac{nRT}{P} = \dfrac{1 \times 8.314 \times 273.15}{101.325} = 22.4\ \text{L}$: the molar volume from lesson 2.6. At SATP, $\dfrac{1 \times 8.314 \times 298.15}{100} = 24.8\ \text{L}$. The ideal gas law contains both.
:::

## Finding pressure, volume, amount or temperature

Rearrange $PV = nRT$ for the quantity you want, then substitute the other four:

| To find | Use |
|---|---|
| pressure | $P = \dfrac{nRT}{V}$ |
| volume | $V = \dfrac{nRT}{P}$ |
| amount | $n = \dfrac{PV}{RT}$ |
| temperature | $T = \dfrac{PV}{nR}$ |

:::example Finding the pressure
A $15.0\ \text{L}$ container holds $1.20\ \text{mol}$ of gas at $350\ \text{K}$. What is the pressure of the gas, to the nearest whole number? Use $PV = nRT$ with $R = 8.314\ \text{kPa·L/(mol·K)}$.
@step Solve for the pressure: $P = \dfrac{nRT}{V}$.
@step $P = \dfrac{1.20 \times 8.314 \times 350}{15.0} = 232.8\ \text{kPa}$
@answer $233\ \text{kPa}$
:::

:::check c20-ideal-gas 1
:::

Most questions give the temperature in degrees Celsius. The ideal gas law needs kelvins, so convert first with $T(\text{K}) = T(^\circ\text{C}) + 273.15$.

:::example Finding the volume
What volume does $0.45\ \text{mol}$ of gas occupy at $120\ \text{kPa}$ and $35^\circ\text{C}$, to the nearest hundredth? Use $PV = nRT$ with $R = 8.314\ \text{kPa·L/(mol·K)}$.
@step $T = 35 + 273.15 = 308.15\ \text{K}$
@step $V = \dfrac{nRT}{P} = \dfrac{0.45 \times 8.314 \times 308.15}{120} = 9.607\ \text{L}$
@answer $9.61\ \text{L}$
:::

:::mistake Temperature in degrees Celsius
Using $35$ instead of $308.15$ in the last example gives $1.09\ \text{L}$, nearly nine times too small. At $0^\circ\text{C}$ it would even give a volume of zero. Always change the temperature to kelvins before you use $PV = nRT$.
:::

:::example Finding the amount
How many moles of gas are in a $20.0\ \text{L}$ tank at $250\ \text{kPa}$ and $18^\circ\text{C}$? Give the answer to the nearest hundredth. Use $PV = nRT$ with $R = 8.314\ \text{kPa·L/(mol·K)}$.
@step $T = 18 + 273.15 = 291.15\ \text{K}$
@step $n = \dfrac{PV}{RT} = \dfrac{250 \times 20.0}{8.314 \times 291.15} = 2.066\ \text{mol}$
@answer $2.07\ \text{mol}$
:::

:::check c20-ideal-gas 2
:::

Solving for $T$ always gives kelvins. If the question asks for degrees Celsius, subtract $273.15$ at the end.

:::example Finding the temperature
$0.85\ \text{mol}$ of gas in a $15.0\ \text{L}$ container has a pressure of $180\ \text{kPa}$. What is its temperature in degrees Celsius? Round to the nearest degree.
@step $T = \dfrac{PV}{nR} = \dfrac{180 \times 15.0}{0.85 \times 8.314} = 382.1\ \text{K}$
@step $T = 382.1 - 273.15 = 109^\circ\text{C}$, to the nearest degree
@answer $109^\circ\text{C}$
:::

## Mass and molar mass

The ideal gas law gives an amount in moles, and the molar mass turns moles into grams: $m = n \times M$.

:::example Finding a mass
What mass of carbon dioxide, $\ce{CO2}$, is in a $10.0\ \text{L}$ container at $150\ \text{kPa}$ and $25^\circ\text{C}$, to the nearest tenth? Use $PV = nRT$ with $R = 8.314\ \text{kPa·L/(mol·K)}$. Use these molar masses: C $12.01\ \text{g/mol}$, O $16.00\ \text{g/mol}$.
@step $T = 25 + 273.15 = 298.15\ \text{K}$
@step $n = \dfrac{PV}{RT} = \dfrac{150 \times 10.0}{8.314 \times 298.15} = 0.6051\ \text{mol}$
@step $M = 12.01 + 2 \times 16.00 = 44.01\ \text{g/mol}$, so $m = 0.6051 \times 44.01 = 26.63\ \text{g}$
@answer $26.6\ \text{g}$
:::

:::check c20-ideal-gas 3
:::

It works the other way too. If you know the mass of a gas sample as well as its pressure, volume and temperature, you can find its molar mass: first the amount, $n = \dfrac{PV}{RT}$, then the mass per mole, $M = \dfrac{m}{n}$.

:::example Finding a molar mass
A $2.308\ \text{g}$ sample of a gas occupies $1.50\ \text{L}$ at $120\ \text{kPa}$ and $27^\circ\text{C}$. What is the molar mass of the gas, to the nearest tenth? Use $PV = nRT$ with $R = 8.314\ \text{kPa·L/(mol·K)}$.
@step $T = 27 + 273.15 = 300.15\ \text{K}$
@step $n = \dfrac{PV}{RT} = \dfrac{120 \times 1.50}{8.314 \times 300.15} = 0.07213\ \text{mol}$
@step $M = \dfrac{m}{n} = \dfrac{2.308}{0.07213} = 32.0\ \text{g/mol}$
@answer $32.0\ \text{g/mol}$
:::

:::

:::check c20-gas-molar-mass 2
:::

Every gas has its own molar mass, so a measured molar mass can identify an unknown gas. Compare it with the molar masses of the possible gases and choose the closest:

| Gas | Molar mass | Gas | Molar mass |
|---|---|---|---|
| hydrogen, $\ce{H2}$ | $2.02\ \text{g/mol}$ | nitrogen, $\ce{N2}$ | $28.02\ \text{g/mol}$ |
| helium, $\ce{He}$ | $4.00\ \text{g/mol}$ | oxygen, $\ce{O2}$ | $32.00\ \text{g/mol}$ |
| methane, $\ce{CH4}$ | $16.05\ \text{g/mol}$ | argon, $\ce{Ar}$ | $39.95\ \text{g/mol}$ |
| ammonia, $\ce{NH3}$ | $17.04\ \text{g/mol}$ | carbon dioxide, $\ce{CO2}$ | $44.01\ \text{g/mol}$ |
| sulfur dioxide, $\ce{SO2}$ | $64.06\ \text{g/mol}$ | chlorine, $\ce{Cl2}$ | $70.90\ \text{g/mol}$ |

:::example Identifying a gas
A $2.746\ \text{g}$ sample of a gas occupies $1.20\ \text{L}$ at $80\ \text{kPa}$ and $25^\circ\text{C}$. What is the molar mass of the gas, to the nearest tenth? Is it argon, carbon dioxide, sulfur dioxide or chlorine? Use $PV = nRT$ with $R = 8.314\ \text{kPa·L/(mol·K)}$.
@step $n = \dfrac{PV}{RT} = \dfrac{80 \times 1.20}{8.314 \times 298.15} = 0.03873\ \text{mol}$
@step $M = \dfrac{m}{n} = \dfrac{2.746}{0.03873} = 70.9\ \text{g/mol}$
@step Compare: argon $39.95$, carbon dioxide $44.01$, sulfur dioxide $64.06$ and chlorine $70.90\ \text{g/mol}$.
@answer $70.9\ \text{g/mol}$. The gas is chlorine, $\ce{Cl2}$, the closest match.
:::

:::tip Round the amount with care
Keep at least four significant figures in $n$. Rounding $0.03873\ \text{mol}$ to $0.039\ \text{mol}$ in the last example would give $70.4\ \text{g/mol}$ instead of $70.9\ \text{g/mol}$.
:::

## Real gases and the ideal gas

The kinetic molecular theory in lesson 2.1 pictured particles with no volume of their own and no attractions between them. That picture is the ideal gas, and real gases only come close to it.

:::definition Ideal gas and real gas
An **ideal gas** obeys $PV = nRT$ exactly: its particles take up no space and do not attract each other. A **real gas** is any actual gas. Its particles do take up a little space and do attract each other a little, so it follows the ideal gas law only approximately.
:::

At everyday temperatures and pressures most gases follow $PV = nRT$ to within a few per cent, which is why the law is so useful. The match depends on how far apart the particles are and how fast they move:

| Conditions | What the particles do | Behaviour |
|---|---|---|
| low pressure | far apart, so their own volume is a tiny part of the container | close to ideal |
| high temperature | move fast, so attractions barely slow them as they pass | close to ideal |
| high pressure | crowded together, so their own volume takes up a noticeable part of the container | less ideal |
| low temperature | move slowly, so attractions pull them towards each other | less ideal |

Real gases behave **most like an ideal gas at high temperature and low pressure**. At high pressure and low temperature, the volume of the particles and the attractions between them both matter. Cool a real gas enough, or squeeze it hard enough, and the attractions win: it condenses to a liquid. An ideal gas would never condense.

:::example Which conditions are more ideal?
A sample of nitrogen, $\ce{N2}$, is studied at $50\ \text{kPa}$ and $150^\circ\text{C}$, and again at $4000\ \text{kPa}$ and $-50^\circ\text{C}$. Under which conditions does it behave more like an ideal gas?
@step At $50\ \text{kPa}$ the particles are far apart, so their own volume hardly matters.
@step At $150^\circ\text{C}$ they move fast, so their attractions hardly matter.
@answer At $50\ \text{kPa}$ and $150^\circ\text{C}$: low pressure and high temperature.
:::

The intermolecular forces from lesson 1.8 matter too. Helium and hydrogen, with few electrons and weak London dispersion forces, behave very nearly ideally. Polar ammonia and sulfur dioxide, and chlorine with its many electrons, attract more strongly and are less ideal. Squeezed hard enough at room temperature, all three condense, so they are stored as liquids in pressurised cylinders.

:::summary
- The **ideal gas law**, $PV = nRT$, links pressure, volume, amount and temperature for a gas in one state.
- The **gas constant** is $R = 8.314\ \text{kPa·L/(mol·K)}$, so use $P$ in $\text{kPa}$, $V$ in $\text{L}$, $n$ in $\text{mol}$ and $T$ in $\text{K}$.
- Rearrange for the quantity you want, and change degrees Celsius to kelvins first: $T(\text{K}) = T(^\circ\text{C}) + 273.15$.
- Mass: $n = \dfrac{PV}{RT}$, then $m = n \times M$. Molar mass: $n = \dfrac{PV}{RT}$, then $M = \dfrac{m}{n}$.
- Identify a gas by choosing the one whose molar mass is closest to the value you found.
- A **real gas** is most like an ideal gas at **high temperature and low pressure**. At high pressure and low temperature, its particles' volume and attractions matter, and it can condense.
:::
