A Lewis structure shows which atoms are joined and where the lone pairs are, but it is drawn flat on a page. Real molecules are three-dimensional, and their shapes matter: a molecule's shape helps decide whether it is polar, how strongly it attracts its neighbours, and even how a medicine fits the molecule it acts on in the body.

Chemists predict shapes from one simple idea: electrons repel each other.

## Electron groups repel

:::definition VSEPR theory
**Valence-shell electron-pair repulsion** theory, or **VSEPR** (often said "vesper"): the electron groups around a central atom repel each other, so they spread out as far apart as possible.
:::

:::definition Electron group
A region of electrons around the central atom. **Each bonded atom counts as one group**, whether the bond is single, double or triple, and **each lone pair on the central atom counts as one group**.
:::

To count the groups, draw the Lewis structure first, then look only at the central atom:

| Molecule | Lewis structure | Bonded atoms | Lone pairs on the central atom | Electron groups |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| $\ce{CO2}$ | $\ce{O=C=O}$ | 2 | 0 | 2 |
| $\ce{CH2O}$ | $\ce{H2C=O}$ | 3 | 0 | 3 |
| $\ce{CH4}$ | four C–H bonds | 4 | 0 | 4 |
| $\ce{NH3}$ | three N–H bonds | 3 | 1 | 4 |
| $\ce{H2O}$ | $\ce{H-O-H}$ | 2 | 2 | 4 |

:::mistake A double bond is one group
For counting groups, a double or triple bond is **one** group, because all of its electrons lie between the same two atoms. $\ce{CO2}$ has 4 bonding pairs but only 2 electron groups. Lone pairs on the **outer** atoms do not count either: only those on the central atom.
:::

## Shapes with no lone pairs

When every group around the central atom is a bonded atom, the shape follows straight from the number of groups:

| Electron groups | Shape | Bond angle | Examples |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2 | linear | 180° | $\ce{CO2}$, $\ce{HCN}$, $\ce{CS2}$ |
| 3 | trigonal planar | 120° | $\ce{BF3}$, $\ce{CH2O}$ |
| 4 | tetrahedral | 109.5° | $\ce{CH4}$, $\ce{CCl4}$, $\ce{SiH4}$ |

:::definition Linear, trigonal planar and tetrahedral
- **Linear**: the atoms lie in a straight line; the bond angle is 180°.
- **Trigonal planar**: three atoms surround the central atom in a flat triangle, 120° apart.
- **Tetrahedral**: four atoms sit at the corners of a tetrahedron (a pyramid with four triangular faces) around the central atom, 109.5° apart.
:::

:::mistake Tetrahedral is not flat
On paper, $\ce{CH4}$ is often drawn as a cross with 90° angles. In space, four groups can get further apart than that: they point to the corners of a tetrahedron, at 109.5° to each other.
:::

:::example Methanal (formaldehyde)
Predict the shape and bond angle of $\ce{CH2O}$.
@step Lewis structure: carbon is central, with two C–H single bonds, a C=O double bond and no lone pairs.
@step Groups on carbon: 3 bonded atoms (the double bond is one group) + 0 lone pairs = 3 groups.
@step Three groups spread out in a flat triangle.
@answer Trigonal planar, with bond angles of about 120°.
:::

:::note Different outer atoms
In $\ce{CH3Cl}$ or $\ce{CHCl3}$, carbon still has 4 bonded atoms and no lone pairs, so these molecules are tetrahedral too. Because the outer atoms are not all the same, the angles are not exactly 109.5°, but they are close to it.
:::

:::tip Two atoms make a line
A molecule of only two atoms, such as $\ce{HCl}$, $\ce{N2}$ or $\ce{Cl2}$, is always linear: any two points lie on a straight line. It has no central atom and no bond angle to predict.
:::

## Lone pairs change the shape

Lone pairs take up space around the central atom, so they help decide where the atoms go. But the shape is **named from the positions of the atoms only**; the lone pairs are not part of the name.

Ammonia, $\ce{NH3}$, has 4 electron groups (3 bonded H atoms and 1 lone pair) pointing roughly to the corners of a tetrahedron. Leave out the lone pair, and nitrogen sits at the top of a low pyramid with the three hydrogen atoms as its base. Water, $\ce{H2O}$, also has 4 groups, but two of them are lone pairs, so its three atoms form a V.

:::definition Trigonal pyramidal and V-shaped
- **Trigonal pyramidal**: a central atom with 3 bonded atoms and 1 lone pair; the atoms form a pyramid on a triangular base.
- **V-shaped (bent)**: a central atom with 2 bonded atoms and 2 lone pairs; the three atoms form a V.
:::

Lone pairs change the angles too. A lone pair is held by only one nucleus, so it spreads out more widely around the central atom than a bonding pair does. **Lone pairs repel more strongly than bonding pairs**, so they squeeze the bond angles below 109.5°, and two lone pairs squeeze harder than one:

| Electron groups | Bonded atoms | Lone pairs | Shape | Bond angle | Examples |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 4 | 4 | 0 | tetrahedral | 109.5° | $\ce{CH4}$, $\ce{CCl4}$, $\ce{SiH4}$ |
| 4 | 3 | 1 | trigonal pyramidal | about 107° (in $\ce{NH3}$) | $\ce{NH3}$, $\ce{PCl3}$, $\ce{NF3}$ |
| 4 | 2 | 2 | V-shaped (bent) | about 104.5° (in $\ce{H2O}$) | $\ce{H2O}$, $\ce{OF2}$, $\ce{H2S}$ |

:::note Where 107° and 104.5° come from
These are the measured angles in ammonia and water, and they are the usual values given for the two shapes. Other molecules with the same shape have somewhat different angles, but always less than 109.5°.
:::

:::example Ammonia
Predict the shape and bond angle of $\ce{NH3}$.
@step Lewis structure: three N–H single bonds and 1 lone pair on nitrogen.
@step Groups on nitrogen: 3 bonded atoms + 1 lone pair = 4 groups, spread out like a tetrahedron.
@step Name the shape from the atoms only: N on top of a pyramid with three H atoms as its base.
@step The lone pair pushes the N–H bonds a little closer together.
@answer Trigonal pyramidal, with a bond angle of about 107°.
:::

:::example Water
Predict the shape and bond angle of $\ce{H2O}$.
@step Lewis structure: $\ce{H-O-H}$, with 2 lone pairs on oxygen.
@step Groups on oxygen: 2 bonded atoms + 2 lone pairs = 4 groups.
@step Name the shape from the atoms only: H, O and H form a V.
@step Two lone pairs squeeze the H–O–H angle even more than one would.
@answer V-shaped (bent), with a bond angle of about 104.5°.
:::

## Predicting a shape, step by step

1. Draw the Lewis structure and find the central atom.
2. Count the electron groups on the central atom: bonded atoms + lone pairs.
3. Use the number of groups and lone pairs to name the shape from the tables.
4. Read off the bond angle: lone pairs make it smaller than the angle with no lone pairs.

:::example Phosphorus trichloride
Predict the shape of $\ce{PCl3}$.
@step Lewis structure: P is central, with 3 P–Cl single bonds and 1 lone pair on phosphorus.
@step Groups: 3 bonded atoms + 1 lone pair = 4 groups.
@answer Trigonal pyramidal, like ammonia, with bond angles less than 109.5°.
:::

:::example Hydrogen cyanide
Predict the shape and bond angle of $\ce{HCN}$.
@step Lewis structure: $\ce{H-C#N}$, with no lone pairs on carbon.
@step Groups on carbon: 2 bonded atoms (the triple bond is one group) + 0 lone pairs = 2 groups.
@answer Linear, with a bond angle of 180°.
:::

:::summary
- VSEPR theory: electron groups around the central atom repel and spread as far apart as possible.
- Each bonded atom is one group (single, double or triple bond); each lone pair on the central atom is one group.
- No lone pairs: 2 groups linear (180°), 3 groups trigonal planar (120°), 4 groups tetrahedral (109.5°).
- 4 groups with 1 lone pair: trigonal pyramidal (about 107°); with 2 lone pairs: V-shaped (bent) (about 104.5°).
- The shape is named from the atoms only, and lone pairs repel more strongly, squeezing the bond angles.
- A molecule of two atoms is always linear.
:::
