A percentage tells you the mass of solute in a solution, but chemical reactions happen particle by particle. To know how many particles of solute are in each portion you pour out, chemists count the solute in **moles** and give the concentration in moles per litre.

In this lesson you will use molar concentration to find a concentration, an amount or a volume, work out the mass of solid needed to make a solution, prepare a solution of known concentration, and find the concentration of each ion that a dissolved ionic compound releases.

## Molar concentration

Lesson 2.6 described the mole as the chemist's way of counting particles. Dividing the amount of solute by the volume of the solution tells you how many moles each litre holds.

:::definition Molar concentration
Molar concentration is $c = \dfrac{n}{V}$, in moles per litre: the amount of solute, $n$, in moles, divided by the volume of the **solution**, $V$, in litres. Its unit is $\text{mol/L}$.
:::

You will meet two shorthand forms. Many books write $\text{mol/L}$ as $\text{M}$, read "molar", so a $0.25\ \text{M}$ solution has a concentration of $0.25\ \text{mol/L}$. Square brackets around a formula mean "the molar concentration of": $[\ce{NaCl}] = 0.25\ \text{mol/L}$ says that each litre of the solution holds $0.25\ \text{mol}$ of sodium chloride.

:::example From amount and volume
$0.45\ \text{mol}$ of solute is dissolved to make $1.20\ \text{L}$ of solution. What is the molar concentration, to the nearest thousandth?
@step $c = \dfrac{n}{V} = \dfrac{0.45\ \text{mol}}{1.20\ \text{L}}$
@answer $0.375\ \text{mol/L}$
:::

:::mistake Litres, not millilitres
The volume in $c = \dfrac{n}{V}$ must be in **litres**. Divide millilitres by 1000 first: $250\ \text{mL} = 0.250\ \text{L}$. Dividing by $1200\ \text{mL}$ instead of $1.20\ \text{L}$ in the last example would give a concentration a thousand times too small.
:::

Rearranging $c = \dfrac{n}{V}$ gives the amount or the volume when you know the other two quantities.

| To find | Use | Unit |
|---|---|---|
| the concentration | $c = \dfrac{n}{V}$ | $\text{mol/L}$ |
| the amount of solute | $n = cV$ | $\text{mol}$ |
| the volume of solution | $V = \dfrac{n}{c}$ | $\text{L}$ |

:::example Finding the amount
How many moles of solute are in $250\ \text{mL}$ of a $0.150\ \text{mol/L}$ solution?
@step $V = 250\ \text{mL} = 0.250\ \text{L}$
@step $n = cV = 0.150\ \text{mol/L} \times 0.250\ \text{L}$
@answer $0.0375\ \text{mol}$
:::

:::example Finding the volume
What volume of a $0.350\ \text{mol/L}$ solution contains $0.0840\ \text{mol}$ of solute? Give the answer in millilitres.
@step $V = \dfrac{n}{c} = \dfrac{0.0840\ \text{mol}}{0.350\ \text{mol/L}} = 0.240\ \text{L}$
@answer $0.240\ \text{L}$, which is $240\ \text{mL}$.
:::

:::tip Let the units decide
$\text{mol/L} \times \text{L} = \text{mol}$, and $\text{mol} \div \text{mol/L} = \text{L}$. If the unit of your answer comes out wrong, the formula is upside down.
:::

## From mass to concentration

In the laboratory you weigh a solid; you cannot count its moles. Change the mass to an amount with the molar mass, $n = \dfrac{m}{M}$, as in lesson 2.6, and then divide by the volume in litres:

$$m \xrightarrow{\ \div M\ } n \xrightarrow{\ \div V\ } c$$

:::example Sodium chloride
$8.50\ \text{g}$ of sodium chloride, $\ce{NaCl}$, is dissolved to make $250\ \text{mL}$ of solution. What is the molar concentration, to the nearest thousandth? Use these molar masses: Na $22.99\ \text{g/mol}$, Cl $35.45\ \text{g/mol}$.
@step $M = 22.99 + 35.45 = 58.44\ \text{g/mol}$
@step $n = \dfrac{m}{M} = \dfrac{8.50}{58.44} = 0.1454\ \text{mol}$
@step $V = 250\ \text{mL} = 0.250\ \text{L}$, so $c = \dfrac{0.1454\ \text{mol}}{0.250\ \text{L}} = 0.5818\ \text{mol/L}$
@answer $0.582\ \text{mol/L}$
:::

## Preparing a standard solution

Many experiments need a solution whose concentration is known precisely, for example to find the unknown concentration of another solution by reacting the two.

:::definition Standard solution
A **standard solution** is a solution whose concentration is known precisely.
:::

:::definition Volumetric flask
A **volumetric flask** is a flask with a long, narrow neck marked with a single line. Filled until the bottom of the meniscus sits on the line, it holds one exact volume, such as $100.0\ \text{mL}$ or $250.0\ \text{mL}$, at the temperature marked on it.
:::

To make a standard solution from a solid, first find the mass you need. Combine $n = cV$ with $m = nM$:

$$m = cVM$$

:::example The mass needed
What mass of sodium carbonate, $\ce{Na2CO3}$, is needed to make $250\ \text{mL}$ of a $0.120\ \text{mol/L}$ solution, to the nearest hundredth? Use these molar masses: Na $22.99\ \text{g/mol}$, C $12.01\ \text{g/mol}$, O $16.00\ \text{g/mol}$.
@step $M = 2 \times 22.99 + 12.01 + 3 \times 16.00 = 105.99\ \text{g/mol}$
@step $n = cV = 0.120\ \text{mol/L} \times 0.250\ \text{L} = 0.0300\ \text{mol}$
@step $m = nM = 0.0300 \times 105.99 = 3.1797\ \text{g}$
@answer $3.18\ \text{g}$
:::

Then make up the solution:

1. **Weigh** the calculated mass of solid, $3.18\ \text{g}$ in the example, into a clean, dry beaker on a balance.
2. **Dissolve** it in a small volume of distilled water, much less than $250\ \text{mL}$, stirring until no solid remains.
3. **Transfer** the solution through a funnel into a $250\ \text{mL}$ volumetric flask. Rinse the beaker, the stirring rod and the funnel with small portions of distilled water, and add every rinse to the flask so that no solute is left behind.
4. **Fill** the flask with distilled water to just below the line, then add the last drops with a dropper until the bottom of the meniscus sits on the line, viewed at eye level.
5. **Mix**: stopper the flask and turn it upside down several times, so that the concentration is the same throughout.
6. **Label** the flask with the name of the solute, its concentration and the date.

:::mistake Make up to the volume; do not add to it
Dissolving $3.18\ \text{g}$ of solid in $250\ \text{mL}$ of water does **not** make $250\ \text{mL}$ of solution, because the dissolved solid changes the volume. The volume in $c = \dfrac{n}{V}$ is the volume of the solution, which is why you dissolve the solid first and then add water up to the line.
:::

## Concentrations of ions

Lesson 3.1 showed that an ionic compound **dissociates** when it dissolves: its ions separate and spread through the water. The dissociation equation shows how many of each ion one formula unit releases, and the concentrations of the ions follow the same ratio.

:::definition Concentration of an ion
In a solution of an ionic compound, the concentration of each ion is its coefficient in the dissociation equation times the concentration of the compound.
:::

:::example Calcium chloride
What is the concentration of each ion in a $0.35\ \text{mol/L}$ solution of calcium chloride, $\ce{CaCl2}$?
@step $\ce{CaCl2(s) -> Ca^2+(aq) + 2Cl^-(aq)}$
@step Each formula unit releases one $\ce{Ca^2+}$ ion and two $\ce{Cl^-}$ ions.
@step $[\ce{Ca^2+}] = 1 \times 0.35 = 0.35\ \text{mol/L}$ and $[\ce{Cl^-}] = 2 \times 0.35 = 0.70\ \text{mol/L}$
@answer $[\ce{Ca^2+}] = 0.35\ \text{mol/L}$ and $[\ce{Cl^-}] = 0.70\ \text{mol/L}$
:::

:::example Aluminium sulfate
What is the concentration of each ion in a $0.15\ \text{mol/L}$ solution of aluminium sulfate, $\ce{Al2(SO4)3}$?
@step $\ce{Al2(SO4)3(s) -> 2Al^3+(aq) + 3SO4^2-(aq)}$
@step $[\ce{Al^3+}] = 2 \times 0.15 = 0.30\ \text{mol/L}$
@step $[\ce{SO4^2-}] = 3 \times 0.15 = 0.45\ \text{mol/L}$
@answer $[\ce{Al^3+}] = 0.30\ \text{mol/L}$ and $[\ce{SO4^2-}] = 0.45\ \text{mol/L}$
:::

:::mistake A polyatomic ion stays together
The sulfate ion, $\ce{SO4^2-}$, is a single ion. The 4 inside it counts oxygen atoms, not ions; the 3 outside the brackets in $\ce{Al2(SO4)3}$ counts the sulfate ions. Do not split a polyatomic ion into atoms when you write the dissociation equation.
:::

:::check c20-ion-conc 1
:::

To go the other way, from the concentration of one ion to the concentration of the compound, divide by that ion's coefficient.

:::example From an ion to the compound
A solution of sodium phosphate, $\ce{Na3PO4}$, has $[\ce{Na^+}] = 0.84\ \text{mol/L}$. What is the concentration of $\ce{Na3PO4}$, to the nearest thousandth?
@step $\ce{Na3PO4(s) -> 3Na^+(aq) + PO4^3-(aq)}$
@step Each formula unit releases three $\ce{Na^+}$ ions, so the compound's concentration is a third of the sodium ion concentration.
@step $c = \dfrac{0.84}{3} = 0.280\ \text{mol/L}$
@answer $0.280\ \text{mol/L}$
:::

:::tip Multiply or divide?
An ion's concentration is never smaller than the compound's, because each formula unit releases at least one of every ion. Multiply to go from the compound to an ion; divide to go back.
:::

:::check c20-ion-conc 2
:::

When the solution is made from a weighed solid, find the concentration of the compound first, then use the dissociation equation.

:::example From a mass to an ion
$5.00\ \text{g}$ of magnesium nitrate, $\ce{Mg(NO3)2}$, is dissolved to make $200\ \text{mL}$ of solution. What is the concentration of $\ce{NO3^-}$ ions, to the nearest thousandth? Use these molar masses: Mg $24.31\ \text{g/mol}$, N $14.01\ \text{g/mol}$, O $16.00\ \text{g/mol}$.
@step $M = 24.31 + 2 \times (14.01 + 3 \times 16.00) = 148.33\ \text{g/mol}$
@step $c = \dfrac{5.00 / 148.33}{0.200} = 0.1685\ \text{mol/L}$
@step $\ce{Mg(NO3)2(s) -> Mg^2+(aq) + 2NO3^-(aq)}$
@step $[\ce{NO3^-}] = 2 \times 0.1685 = 0.337\ \text{mol/L}$
@answer $0.337\ \text{mol/L}$
:::

:::note Molecular compounds do not dissociate
A molecular solute such as glucose, $\ce{C6H12O6}$, or sucrose, $\ce{C12H22O11}$, dissolves as whole molecules, not ions. A $0.10\ \text{mol/L}$ glucose solution holds $0.10\ \text{mol}$ of glucose molecules in each litre and no ions from the glucose at all.
:::

:::

:::summary
- **Molar concentration** is $c = \dfrac{n}{V}$, in moles per litre ($\text{mol/L}$, also written $\text{M}$). Square brackets mean "the molar concentration of": $[\ce{NaCl}]$.
- Rearranged: $n = cV$ and $V = \dfrac{n}{c}$. The volume is always the volume of the solution, in litres.
- From a mass, find the amount first with $n = \dfrac{m}{M}$, then $c = \dfrac{n}{V}$.
- A **standard solution** has a precisely known concentration. It needs a mass $m = cVM$ of solute: dissolve it, transfer it with every rinse to a **volumetric flask**, add water up to the line and mix.
- The concentration of an ion is its coefficient in the dissociation equation times the concentration of the compound. Divide by the coefficient to go back.
:::
