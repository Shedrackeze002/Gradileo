A small sip of water, $5.00\ \text{g}$ of it, holds about $1.67 \times 10^{23}$ water molecules. Counting them at one a second would take far longer than the universe has existed. Yet a chemist can count them in a moment, with a balance and a calculator, because a mole of any substance has a known mass.

In this lesson you will calculate the molar mass of any compound, including a hydrate, convert between mass and amount in moles, and use Avogadro's number to count molecules and atoms.

## Molar mass

Lesson 2.6 introduced the **mole** as the chemist's counting unit. One mole of anything holds $6.02 \times 10^{23}$ particles, a number called **Avogadro's number**, and the molar mass is the mass of that many particles.

:::definition Molar mass
The **molar mass**, $M$, of a substance is the mass of one mole of it, in grams per mole ($\text{g/mol}$). For a compound, add the molar masses of all the atoms in one formula unit.
:::

The molar mass of each element comes from the periodic table. The questions in this course state the ones they use, to two decimal places, such as H $1.01\ \text{g/mol}$ and O $16.00\ \text{g/mol}$. Add those values and keep the compound's molar mass to two decimal places too, and your answer will match exactly.

:::example Water
What is the molar mass of water, $\ce{H2O}$, to the nearest hundredth? Use these molar masses: H $1.01\ \text{g/mol}$, O $16.00\ \text{g/mol}$.
@step One molecule holds 2 hydrogen atoms and 1 oxygen atom.
@step $M(\ce{H2O}) = 2(1.01) + 16.00$
@answer $18.02\ \text{g/mol}$
:::

:::example Glucose
What is the molar mass of glucose, $\ce{C6H12O6}$, to the nearest hundredth? Use these molar masses: C $12.01\ \text{g/mol}$, H $1.01\ \text{g/mol}$, O $16.00\ \text{g/mol}$.
@step $M = 6(12.01) + 12(1.01) + 6(16.00) = 72.06 + 12.12 + 96.00$
@answer $180.18\ \text{g/mol}$
:::

A subscript after a bracket multiplies everything inside the bracket. Count the atoms of each element first, then add.

:::example Calcium hydroxide
What is the molar mass of calcium hydroxide, $\ce{Ca(OH)2}$, to the nearest hundredth? Use these molar masses: Ca $40.08\ \text{g/mol}$, O $16.00\ \text{g/mol}$, H $1.01\ \text{g/mol}$.
@step One formula unit holds 1 Ca, $2 \times 1 = 2$ O and $2 \times 1 = 2$ H.
@step $M = 40.08 + 2(16.00) + 2(1.01) = 40.08 + 32.00 + 2.02$
@answer $74.10\ \text{g/mol}$
:::

:::example Aluminium sulfate
What is the molar mass of aluminium sulfate, $\ce{Al2(SO4)3}$, to the nearest hundredth? Use these molar masses: Al $26.98\ \text{g/mol}$, S $32.06\ \text{g/mol}$, O $16.00\ \text{g/mol}$.
@step One formula unit holds 2 Al, $3 \times 1 = 3$ S and $3 \times 4 = 12$ O.
@step $M = 2(26.98) + 3(32.06) + 12(16.00) = 53.96 + 96.18 + 192.00$
@answer $342.14\ \text{g/mol}$
:::

:::mistake The bracket multiplies everything inside
In $\ce{Al2(SO4)3}$ the 3 multiplies both the sulfur and the oxygen. Forgetting it, and counting only 1 S and 4 O, gives $2(26.98) + 32.06 + 4(16.00) = 150.02\ \text{g/mol}$, far too small.
:::

## Hydrates

Some ionic compounds hold water molecules inside their crystals.

:::definition Hydrate
A **hydrate** is an ionic compound with a fixed number of water molecules built into its crystals for each formula unit. The formula shows the water after a raised dot: copper(II) sulfate pentahydrate, $\ce{CuSO4*5H2O}$, has five water molecules for each $\ce{CuSO4}$.
:::

Heat blue crystals of $\ce{CuSO4*5H2O}$ and the water is driven off as steam, leaving a white powder of **anhydrous** ("without water") copper(II) sulfate, $\ce{CuSO4}$. The water is part of the hydrate, so it counts in the hydrate's molar mass.

:::example Copper(II) sulfate pentahydrate
What is the molar mass of $\ce{CuSO4*5H2O}$, to the nearest hundredth? Use these molar masses: Cu $63.55\ \text{g/mol}$, S $32.06\ \text{g/mol}$, O $16.00\ \text{g/mol}$, H $1.01\ \text{g/mol}$.
@step $M(\ce{CuSO4}) = 63.55 + 32.06 + 4(16.00) = 159.61\ \text{g/mol}$
@step $M(\ce{H2O}) = 2(1.01) + 16.00 = 18.02\ \text{g/mol}$, and there are 5 of them: $5(18.02) = 90.10\ \text{g/mol}$.
@step $M = 159.61 + 90.10$. Or count every atom: 1 Cu, 1 S, $4 + 5 = 9$ O and 10 H, so $63.55 + 32.06 + 9(16.00) + 10(1.01)$.
@answer $249.71\ \text{g/mol}$
:::

:::example Washing soda
Washing soda is sodium carbonate decahydrate, $\ce{Na2CO3*10H2O}$. What is its molar mass, to the nearest hundredth? Use these molar masses: Na $22.99\ \text{g/mol}$, C $12.01\ \text{g/mol}$, O $16.00\ \text{g/mol}$, H $1.01\ \text{g/mol}$.
@step $M(\ce{Na2CO3}) = 2(22.99) + 12.01 + 3(16.00) = 105.99\ \text{g/mol}$
@step $10(18.02) = 180.20\ \text{g/mol}$ of water.
@answer $105.99 + 180.20 = 286.19\ \text{g/mol}$
:::

:::mistake The dot means add
The dot in $\ce{CuSO4*5H2O}$ is not a multiplication sign. The 5 counts only the water molecules: add $5(18.02)$ to the molar mass of $\ce{CuSO4}$. Multiplying $159.61$ by anything would be wrong.
:::

## Mass and amount

The molar mass connects the mass you can weigh with the amount in moles. Divide a mass by the molar mass to get moles; multiply moles by the molar mass to get grams:

$$n = \dfrac{m}{M} \qquad m = nM$$

:::example From mass to moles
How many moles are in $25.0\ \text{g}$ of sodium chloride, $\ce{NaCl}$? Give the answer to the nearest thousandth. The molar mass of sodium chloride is $58.44\ \text{g/mol}$.
@step $n = \dfrac{m}{M} = \dfrac{25.0\ \text{g}}{58.44\ \text{g/mol}} = 0.4278\ \text{mol}$
@answer $0.428\ \text{mol}$
:::

:::tip Let the units decide
$\text{g} \div \text{g/mol} = \text{mol}$, and $\text{mol} \times \text{g/mol} = \text{g}$. If the unit of your answer comes out as $\text{mol/g}$ or $\text{g}^2/\text{mol}$, the fraction is upside down.
:::

:::check c20-mole-mass 1
:::

:::example From moles to mass
What is the mass of $2.50\ \text{mol}$ of carbon dioxide, $\ce{CO2}$, to the nearest whole number? The molar mass of carbon dioxide is $44.01\ \text{g/mol}$.
@step $m = nM = 2.50\ \text{mol} \times 44.01\ \text{g/mol} = 110.025\ \text{g}$
@answer $110\ \text{g}$
:::

:::check c20-mole-mass 2
:::

When the molar mass is not given, work it out first, keeping two decimal places, and only then divide.

:::example Molar mass first
A sample of sodium hydrogen carbonate, $\ce{NaHCO3}$, has a mass of $12.5\ \text{g}$. Find the molar mass to the nearest hundredth, then the amount in moles to the nearest thousandth. Use these molar masses: Na $22.99\ \text{g/mol}$, H $1.01\ \text{g/mol}$, C $12.01\ \text{g/mol}$, O $16.00\ \text{g/mol}$.
@step $M = 22.99 + 1.01 + 12.01 + 3(16.00) = 84.01\ \text{g/mol}$
@step $n = \dfrac{12.5}{84.01} = 0.1488\ \text{mol}$
@answer $M = 84.01\ \text{g/mol}$ and $n = 0.149\ \text{mol}$
:::

## Counting particles

Avogadro's number turns moles into particles and back again.

:::definition Avogadro's number
**Avogadro's number**, $N_A = 6.02 \times 10^{23}\ \text{mol}^{-1}$, is the number of particles in one mole. The number of particles is $N = nN_A$, and the amount is $n = \dfrac{N}{N_A}$.
:::

Answers this large are written in scientific notation. To enter $2.05 \times 10^{24}$, type `2.05e24` or `2.05*10^24`.

:::example Moles to molecules
How many molecules are in $3.40\ \text{mol}$ of water, $\ce{H2O}$? Give the answer in scientific notation to three significant figures. Use $N_A = 6.02 \times 10^{23}\ \text{mol}^{-1}$.
@step $N = nN_A = 3.40 \times 6.02 \times 10^{23} = 2.0468 \times 10^{24}$
@answer $2.05 \times 10^{24}$ molecules
:::

:::example Molecules to moles
A sample of water contains $1.20 \times 10^{25}$ molecules. How many moles is that, to the nearest tenth? Use $N_A = 6.02 \times 10^{23}\ \text{mol}^{-1}$.
@step $n = \dfrac{N}{N_A} = \dfrac{1.20 \times 10^{25}}{6.02 \times 10^{23}} = 19.93\ \text{mol}$
@answer $19.9\ \text{mol}$
:::

:::tip Powers of ten on a calculator
Enter $6.02 \times 10^{23}$ with the calculator's exponent key, often marked EE or EXP, as `6.02E23`. When you divide by it, typing `÷ 6.02 × 10^23` divides by 6.02 and then multiplies by $10^{23}$, which is wrong. Use the exponent key, or put brackets around the whole number.
:::

A molecule is made of atoms, so a count of molecules is not a count of atoms. Each molecule of methane, $\ce{CH4}$, holds 1 carbon atom and 4 hydrogen atoms, so a sample holds four times as many hydrogen atoms as molecules. The full path from a mass to a number of atoms is:

$$m \xrightarrow{\ \div M\ } n \xrightarrow{\ \times N_A\ } \text{molecules} \xrightarrow{\ \times \text{atoms per molecule}\ } \text{atoms}$$

:::example Atoms in a mass
How many hydrogen atoms are in $10.0\ \text{g}$ of methane, $\ce{CH4}$? Give the answer in scientific notation to three significant figures. Use these molar masses: C $12.01\ \text{g/mol}$, H $1.01\ \text{g/mol}$. Use $N_A = 6.02 \times 10^{23}\ \text{mol}^{-1}$.
@step $M(\ce{CH4}) = 12.01 + 4(1.01) = 16.05\ \text{g/mol}$
@step $n = \dfrac{10.0}{16.05} = 0.6231\ \text{mol}$
@step Molecules: $0.6231 \times 6.02 \times 10^{23} = 3.751 \times 10^{23}$
@step Each molecule has 4 hydrogen atoms: $4 \times 3.751 \times 10^{23} = 1.500 \times 10^{24}$
@answer $1.50 \times 10^{24}$ hydrogen atoms
:::

:::mistake Molecules or atoms?
One mole of oxygen gas, $\ce{O2}$, is $6.02 \times 10^{23}$ molecules but $2 \times 6.02 \times 10^{23} = 1.20 \times 10^{24}$ oxygen atoms. Read the question: if it asks for atoms, multiply by the number of those atoms in one molecule.
:::

:::summary
- The **molar mass**, $M$, is the mass of one mole, in $\text{g/mol}$: add the molar masses of all the atoms in the formula, to two decimal places.
- A subscript after a bracket multiplies every atom inside it: $\ce{Al2(SO4)3}$ holds 3 S and 12 O.
- A **hydrate** holds water in its crystals. Add the water to the molar mass: $M(\ce{CuSO4*5H2O}) = 159.61 + 5(18.02) = 249.71\ \text{g/mol}$.
- Mass and amount: $n = \dfrac{m}{M}$ and $m = nM$.
- **Avogadro's number**, $N_A = 6.02 \times 10^{23}\ \text{mol}^{-1}$: $N = nN_A$ and $n = \dfrac{N}{N_A}$. Type scientific notation as `1.51e24` or `1.51*10^24`.
- To count atoms, multiply the number of molecules by the number of those atoms in one molecule.
:::
