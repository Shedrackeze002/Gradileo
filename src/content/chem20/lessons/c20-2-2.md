Squeeze a sealed plastic bottle and you can feel the air inside pushing back. That push is **pressure**, and it comes from the particles of the gas striking the walls. Temperature is just as closely tied to the particles: it tells you how fast they are moving.

In this lesson you will explain gas pressure, measure it with a barometer, and convert pressures between the three units chemists use. Then you will meet absolute zero, the coldest temperature possible, and convert temperatures to the Kelvin scale that starts there.

## What is pressure?

Stand in deep snow in ordinary boots and you sink; strap on snowshoes and you stay on top. Your weight is the same either way, but the snowshoes spread it over a much larger area, so the push on each square centimetre of snow is much smaller.

:::definition Pressure
**Pressure** is the force acting on each unit of area: $P = \dfrac{F}{A}$. With the force in newtons and the area in square metres, the pressure is in **pascals**: $1\ \text{Pa} = 1\ \text{N/m}^2$.
:::

:::definition Kilopascal
A pascal is a very small pressure, so gas pressures are usually given in **kilopascals**: $1\ \text{kPa} = 1000\ \text{Pa}$.
:::

:::example Force and area
A box pushes down on the floor with a force of $120\ \text{N}$, spread over an area of $0.040\ \text{m}^2$. What pressure does it exert on the floor, in kilopascals?
@step $P = \dfrac{F}{A} = \dfrac{120\ \text{N}}{0.040\ \text{m}^2} = 3000\ \text{Pa}$
@answer $3000\ \text{Pa} = 3.0\ \text{kPa}$
:::

In a gas, the force comes from collisions. Every second, an enormous number of particles hit each square centimetre of the wall, and each one gives it a tiny push. Together the pushes make a steady force, and that force divided by the area of the wall is the gas pressure. The particles move in every direction, so a gas pushes on the top and sides of its container as hard as on the bottom.

The pressure rises when the particles hit the walls **more often**, because there are more particles or less room, or **harder**, because they move faster at a higher temperature. The gas laws in the coming lessons put numbers to these ideas.

## Atmospheric pressure and the barometer

We live at the bottom of an ocean of air. Gravity pulls the atmosphere down, and its weight presses on everything beneath it. At sea level, the pressure of the atmosphere is about $101\ \text{kPa}$: a force of about $101\,000\ \text{N}$ on every square metre, roughly the weight of a 10-tonne mass. We do not feel it, because the air pushes equally from every side and the fluids in our bodies push back out just as hard.

Higher up there is less air above, so atmospheric pressure falls: near the tops of the highest mountains it is only about a third of its value at sea level. It also rises and falls a little with the weather.

:::definition Barometer
A **barometer** is an instrument that measures the pressure of the atmosphere.
:::

The first barometer, made by Evangelista Torricelli in the 1640s, used mercury. A glass tube about a metre long, closed at one end, is filled with mercury and turned upside down in a dish of mercury. Some mercury runs out, but not all of it: the atmosphere pressing down on the mercury in the dish holds up a column in the tube, with a nearly empty space above it. At sea level the column stands about $760\ \text{mm}$ high. When the air pressure rises, it pushes the column higher; when the pressure falls, the column drops.

Mercury is used because it is so dense, about 13.6 times as dense as water. A barometer filled with water would need a tube more than $10\ \text{m}$ tall.

## Units of pressure

The barometer gives one of the three units of pressure used in chemistry:

| Unit | Symbol | Where it comes from |
|---|---|---|
| kilopascal | kPa | 1000 pascals; the pascal is the SI unit of pressure |
| atmosphere | atm | close to the average pressure of the air at sea level |
| millimetre of mercury | mm Hg | the pressure that holds up a column of mercury 1 mm high |

:::definition Atmosphere
The **atmosphere**, atm, is a unit of pressure close to the average pressure of the air at sea level. The three units are related by $1\ \text{atm} = 101.325\ \text{kPa} = 760\ \text{mm Hg}$.
:::

To convert a pressure, multiply it by a **conversion factor**: a fraction whose top and bottom are the same pressure in two units, so it is equal to 1. Put the unit you want on top and the unit you have on the bottom, so the unit you have cancels.

:::example Atmospheres to kilopascals
Convert $1.75\ \text{atm}$ to kilopascals, to the nearest whole number. Use $1\ \text{atm} = 101.325\ \text{kPa} = 760\ \text{mm Hg}$.
@step You want kPa and have atm, so use the factor with kPa on top: $\dfrac{101.325\ \text{kPa}}{1\ \text{atm}}$.
@step $1.75\ \text{atm} \times \dfrac{101.325\ \text{kPa}}{1\ \text{atm}} = 177.318\ldots\ \text{kPa}$
@answer $177\ \text{kPa}$
:::

:::example Kilopascals to atmospheres
Convert $85\ \text{kPa}$ to atmospheres, to the nearest thousandth. Use $1\ \text{atm} = 101.325\ \text{kPa} = 760\ \text{mm Hg}$.
@step Now kPa must cancel, so it goes on the bottom: $85\ \text{kPa} \times \dfrac{1\ \text{atm}}{101.325\ \text{kPa}} = 0.8388\ldots\ \text{atm}$
@answer $0.839\ \text{atm}$
:::

:::example A barometer reading
A barometer reads $735\ \text{mm Hg}$. Convert this pressure to kilopascals, to the nearest tenth. Use $1\ \text{atm} = 101.325\ \text{kPa} = 760\ \text{mm Hg}$.
@step $735\ \text{mm Hg} \times \dfrac{101.325\ \text{kPa}}{760\ \text{mm Hg}} = 97.991\ldots\ \text{kPa}$
@answer $98.0\ \text{kPa}$
:::

Going the other way, from kilopascals, the factor turns over: $250\ \text{kPa} \times \dfrac{760\ \text{mm Hg}}{101.325\ \text{kPa}} = 1875.15\ldots\ \text{mm Hg}$, or $1875\ \text{mm Hg}$ to the nearest whole number.

:::check c20-pressure-units 2
:::

:::example Millimetres of mercury to atmospheres
Convert $2050\ \text{mm Hg}$ to atmospheres, to the nearest hundredth. Use $1\ \text{atm} = 101.325\ \text{kPa} = 760\ \text{mm Hg}$.
@step $2050\ \text{mm Hg} \times \dfrac{1\ \text{atm}}{760\ \text{mm Hg}} = 2.697\ldots\ \text{atm}$
@answer $2.70\ \text{atm}$
:::

:::tip Is the answer sensible?
The same pressure is a large number in mm Hg, a middling one in kPa and a small one in atm: $760\ \text{mm Hg}$, $101.325\ \text{kPa}$ and $1\ \text{atm}$ are all the same pressure. Converting to mm Hg should make the number bigger; converting to atm should make it smaller.
:::

:::mistake A factor upside down
To convert $0.85\ \text{atm}$ to mm Hg, multiply by $\dfrac{760\ \text{mm Hg}}{1\ \text{atm}}$ to get $646\ \text{mm Hg}$. Multiplying by $\dfrac{1\ \text{atm}}{760\ \text{mm Hg}}$ instead leaves the units $\text{atm}^2/\text{mm Hg}$ and a tiny number. Write the units in every step, and check that the unit you started with cancels.
:::

## Temperature and absolute zero

Temperature measures the average kinetic energy of the particles: the hotter a gas, the faster its particles move on average. Cool a gas and its particles slow down. Keep cooling it, and you approach a temperature at which the particles would have the least kinetic energy they can have. Nothing can be colder than that.

:::definition Absolute zero
**Absolute zero** is the lowest temperature possible, $-273.15^\circ\text{C}$. At absolute zero the particles would have the least kinetic energy possible. It can be approached very closely, but never quite reached.
:::

The Celsius scale puts its zero where water freezes: a convenient choice, but not a fundamental one, which is why Celsius temperatures can be negative. The Kelvin scale puts its zero at absolute zero instead.

:::definition Kelvin scale
The **Kelvin scale** starts at absolute zero, $0\ \text{K}$. A change of one kelvin is the same as a change of one degree Celsius, so $T(\text{K}) = T(^\circ\text{C}) + 273.15$. Kelvin temperatures are written without a degree sign, as in $300\ \text{K}$ ("300 kelvins"), and are never negative.
:::

The graph shows the two scales side by side. Each temperature in kelvins is the Celsius temperature plus 273.15, so the line has a slope of 1 and meets $0\ \text{K}$ at $-273.15^\circ\text{C}$.

At normal air pressure, water freezes at about $0^\circ\text{C}$ and boils at about $100^\circ\text{C}$:

| | Celsius | Kelvin |
|---|---|---|
| Absolute zero | $-273.15^\circ\text{C}$ | $0\ \text{K}$ |
| Water freezes | $0^\circ\text{C}$ | $273.15\ \text{K}$ |
| A warm room | $25^\circ\text{C}$ | $298.15\ \text{K}$ |
| Water boils | $100^\circ\text{C}$ | $373.15\ \text{K}$ |

:::example Celsius to kelvins
Convert $-40^\circ\text{C}$ to kelvins. Use $T(\text{K}) = T(^\circ\text{C}) + 273.15$.
@step $T = -40 + 273.15 = 233.15\ \text{K}$
@answer $233.15\ \text{K}$
:::

:::check c20-kelvin 1
:::

:::example Kelvins to Celsius
Convert $350\ \text{K}$ to degrees Celsius. Use $T(\text{K}) = T(^\circ\text{C}) + 273.15$.
@step Going from kelvins to Celsius, subtract: $T = 350 - 273.15 = 76.85^\circ\text{C}$
@answer $76.85^\circ\text{C}$
:::

:::mistake Adding when you should subtract
The Kelvin number is always 273.15 **larger** than the Celsius number. Add 273.15 to go from Celsius to kelvins, and subtract it to go back. If you ever get a negative Kelvin temperature, you have gone the wrong way.
:::

## Why gas calculations use kelvins

The kinetic molecular theory says that the average kinetic energy of the particles is proportional to the **Kelvin** temperature. Double the Kelvin temperature and the average kinetic energy doubles too. The Celsius temperature is not proportional to the average kinetic energy, because $0^\circ\text{C}$ is simply where water freezes, not where the particles have the least energy. On the graph, the line does not pass through the point where both temperatures are zero.

:::example Doubling a Kelvin temperature
A gas is at $20^\circ\text{C}$. Its Kelvin temperature is doubled. What is its new temperature in degrees Celsius? Use $T(\text{K}) = T(^\circ\text{C}) + 273.15$.
@step Change to kelvins: $T_1 = 20 + 273.15 = 293.15\ \text{K}$
@step Double it: $T_2 = 2 \times 293.15 = 586.30\ \text{K}$
@step Change back: $T_2 = 586.30 - 273.15 = 313.15^\circ\text{C}$
@answer $313.15^\circ\text{C}$, not $40^\circ\text{C}$.
:::

:::mistake Doubling the Celsius temperature
Warming a gas from $20^\circ\text{C}$ to $40^\circ\text{C}$ doubles the Celsius number, but the Kelvin temperature only goes from $293.15\ \text{K}$ to $313.15\ \text{K}$, an increase of under 7%. The particles' average kinetic energy rises by the same small fraction. Whenever you multiply or divide temperatures, as every gas law does, use kelvins.
:::

:::summary
- Pressure is force per unit area, $P = \dfrac{F}{A}$, measured in pascals; $1\ \text{kPa} = 1000\ \text{Pa}$.
- Gas pressure comes from particles colliding with the walls. More frequent or harder collisions mean a higher pressure.
- Atmospheric pressure is about $101\ \text{kPa}$ at sea level, and a mercury barometer holds up a column of about $760\ \text{mm}$ there.
- $1\ \text{atm} = 101.325\ \text{kPa} = 760\ \text{mm Hg}$. Convert with a factor that has the unit you want on top.
- Temperature measures the average kinetic energy of the particles. Absolute zero, $0\ \text{K}$ or $-273.15^\circ\text{C}$, is the lowest temperature possible.
- $T(\text{K}) = T(^\circ\text{C}) + 273.15$. Kelvin temperatures have no degree sign and are never negative.
- Doubling a Kelvin temperature doubles the average kinetic energy; doubling a Celsius temperature does not double the Kelvin temperature.
:::
