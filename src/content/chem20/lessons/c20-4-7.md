A recipe that promises 24 cookies rarely gives exactly 24. Some dough sticks to the bowl and the spoon, one cookie burns, and another breaks on the way to the plate. Chemical reactions are the same: the mass of product a chemist actually collects is almost always less than the stoichiometry predicts.

In this lesson you will compare the mass of product collected with the mass predicted, calculate the **percent yield**, explain why it is usually below 100%, and plan how much reactant to use when the percent yield is known.

## Theoretical, actual and percent yield

Every stoichiometry calculation in lessons 4.4 to 4.6 predicted the most product that could possibly form, if every particle of the limiting reagent reacted and every bit of product were collected. That prediction has a name.

:::definition Theoretical yield
The **theoretical yield** is the mass of product that the stoichiometry predicts from the amount of limiting reagent: the most that could form.
:::

:::definition Actual yield
The **actual yield** is the mass of product actually collected and measured in an experiment.
:::

:::definition Percent yield
The **percent yield** compares the two:
$$\text{percent yield} = \dfrac{\text{actual yield}}{\text{theoretical yield}} \times 100\%$$
:::

:::example Lead(II) iodide
Lead(II) nitrate reacts with excess potassium iodide, and the precipitate is filtered and dried: $\ce{Pb(NO3)2 + 2KI -> PbI2 + 2KNO3}$. The theoretical yield is $4.61\ \text{g}$ of $\ce{PbI2}$, and $4.02\ \text{g}$ is collected. What is the percent yield, to the nearest tenth?
@step $\text{percent yield} = \dfrac{\text{actual}}{\text{theoretical}} \times 100\% = \dfrac{4.02}{4.61} \times 100\%$
@answer $87.2\%$
:::

:::mistake Upside down
The actual yield goes on **top**. Dividing the other way, $\dfrac{4.61}{4.02} \times 100\% = 114.7\%$, would claim that more product was collected than could possibly form. A percent yield above 100% is a warning sign, as the next section explains.
:::

## Why the actual yield is lower

A percent yield of 100% is very rare. Some of the reasons the actual yield falls short are:

- **The reaction does not go to completion.** Some reactant is still unreacted when the reaction stops, or the reaction reaches a balance with products changing back into reactants.
- **Side reactions** use up some of the reactants to make other, unwanted products. Magnesium burned in air, for example, forms some magnesium nitride as well as magnesium oxide.
- **Product is lost while it is collected.** A precipitate sticks to the beaker and the filter paper, a few crystals pass through the filter, and a little product is spilled or left behind with each transfer between containers.
- **The reactants are impure.** If a reactant is only 95% pure, the other 5% cannot form the product.

A percent yield can also come out **above** 100%. That never means the reaction made extra product, because mass is conserved; it means something else was weighed with the product.

:::example A yield that is too high
Barium chloride reacts with excess sodium sulfate, and the precipitate is filtered: $\ce{BaCl2 + Na2SO4 -> BaSO4 + 2NaCl}$. The theoretical yield is $4.85\ \text{g}$ of $\ce{BaSO4}$, but a student weighs $5.12\ \text{g}$ of precipitate straight off the filter paper. What is the percent yield, to the nearest tenth, and what probably went wrong?
@step $\dfrac{5.12}{4.85} \times 100\% = 105.6\%$
@step No reaction can make more than its theoretical yield, so something other than barium sulfate was weighed.
@answer $105.6\%$. The precipitate was most likely still wet, so water was weighed with it. Impurities in the solid would also add mass.
:::

:::tip Dry to a constant mass
Dry a precipitate, weigh it, dry it again and weigh it again. When two weighings agree, the water is gone and the mass is the actual yield.
:::

## Finding the theoretical yield first

Usually you are given the mass of the reactant used, not the theoretical yield. Find the theoretical yield with gravimetric stoichiometry, as in lesson 4.4, then calculate the percent yield.

:::example Lime from limestone
Calcium carbonate is heated to make lime, calcium oxide: $\ce{CaCO3 -> CaO + CO2}$. $40.0\ \text{g}$ of $\ce{CaCO3}$ is used, and $20.5\ \text{g}$ of $\ce{CaO}$ is collected. Find the theoretical yield to the nearest tenth, then the percent yield to the nearest tenth. Use these molar masses: Ca $40.08\ \text{g/mol}$, C $12.01\ \text{g/mol}$, O $16.00\ \text{g/mol}$.
@step $M(\ce{CaCO3}) = 40.08 + 12.01 + 3(16.00) = 100.09\ \text{g/mol}$, so $n(\ce{CaCO3}) = \dfrac{40.0}{100.09} = 0.3996\ \text{mol}$
@step $n(\ce{CaO}) = 0.3996 \times \dfrac{1}{1} = 0.3996\ \text{mol}$
@step $M(\ce{CaO}) = 40.08 + 16.00 = 56.08\ \text{g/mol}$, so the theoretical yield is $0.3996 \times 56.08 = 22.41\ \text{g}$, which is $22.4\ \text{g}$ to the nearest tenth.
@step Percent yield: $\dfrac{20.5}{22.41} \times 100\% = 91.5\%$
@answer Theoretical yield $22.4\ \text{g}$; percent yield $91.5\%$
:::

:::example Silver from copper
A copper wire is placed in excess silver nitrate solution, and the silver is collected: $\ce{Cu + 2AgNO3 -> Cu(NO3)2 + 2Ag}$. $2.50\ \text{g}$ of $\ce{Cu}$ is used, and $7.50\ \text{g}$ of $\ce{Ag}$ is collected. Find the theoretical yield to the nearest hundredth, then the percent yield to the nearest tenth. Use these molar masses: Cu $63.55\ \text{g/mol}$, Ag $107.87\ \text{g/mol}$.
@step $n(\ce{Cu}) = \dfrac{2.50}{63.55} = 0.03934\ \text{mol}$
@step $n(\ce{Ag}) = 0.03934 \times \dfrac{2}{1} = 0.07868\ \text{mol}$
@step Theoretical yield: $0.07868 \times 107.87 = 8.487\ \text{g}$, which is $8.49\ \text{g}$ to the nearest hundredth.
@step Percent yield: $\dfrac{7.50}{8.487} \times 100\% = 88.4\%$
@answer Theoretical yield $8.49\ \text{g}$; percent yield $88.4\%$
:::

:::tip Keep the unrounded theoretical yield
Round the theoretical yield only for the answer you write down, and divide by the unrounded value. In the silver example, dividing by the rounded $8.49\ \text{g}$ gives $88.3\%$ instead of $88.4\%$. Keep every digit in your calculator until the last step.
:::

## Planning for a known percent yield

A chemist who knows that a process has a yield of 80% has to plan for it. To collect $20.0\ \text{g}$ of product, the reaction must be set up to make **more** than that in theory, because only 80% of the theoretical yield ends up in the bottle:

$$\text{theoretical yield needed} = \dfrac{\text{mass wanted}}{\text{percent yield as a decimal}} = \dfrac{20.0\ \text{g}}{0.80} = 25.0\ \text{g}$$

Then work back through moles, from the product to the reactant, to find the mass of reactant to use.

:::example Collecting copper
Zinc is added to excess copper(II) sulfate solution, and the copper is collected: $\ce{Zn + CuSO4 -> ZnSO4 + Cu}$. The process has a percent yield of 75%. What mass of $\ce{Zn}$ is needed to collect $10.0\ \text{g}$ of $\ce{Cu}$? Give the answer to the nearest tenth. Use these molar masses: Zn $65.38\ \text{g/mol}$, Cu $63.55\ \text{g/mol}$.
@step Theoretical yield needed: $\dfrac{10.0}{0.75} = 13.33\ \text{g}$ of copper
@step $n(\ce{Cu}) = \dfrac{13.33}{63.55} = 0.2098\ \text{mol}$
@step $n(\ce{Zn}) = 0.2098 \times \dfrac{1}{1} = 0.2098\ \text{mol}$
@step $m = 0.2098 \times 65.38 = 13.72\ \text{g}$
@answer $13.7\ \text{g}$ of zinc
:::

:::mistake Multiply or divide by the yield?
Multiplying by the yield instead of dividing, $10.0 \times 0.75 = 7.50\ \text{g}$, plans for a **smaller** theoretical yield than the mass you want, and leads to only $7.72\ \text{g}$ of zinc. With a yield below 100% you always need more reactant than a perfect reaction would, so the theoretical yield must come out **larger** than the mass wanted. Divide.
:::

:::example Making ammonia
Nitrogen reacts with excess hydrogen to make ammonia: $\ce{N2 + 3H2 -> 2NH3}$. The process has a percent yield of 85%. What mass of $\ce{N2}$ is needed to collect $50.0\ \text{g}$ of $\ce{NH3}$? Give the answer to the nearest tenth. Use these molar masses: N $14.01\ \text{g/mol}$, H $1.01\ \text{g/mol}$.
@step Theoretical yield needed: $\dfrac{50.0}{0.85} = 58.82\ \text{g}$ of ammonia
@step $M(\ce{NH3}) = 14.01 + 3(1.01) = 17.04\ \text{g/mol}$, so $n(\ce{NH3}) = \dfrac{58.82}{17.04} = 3.452\ \text{mol}$
@step $n(\ce{N2}) = 3.452 \times \dfrac{1}{2} = 1.726\ \text{mol}$
@step $M(\ce{N2}) = 2(14.01) = 28.02\ \text{g/mol}$, so $m = 1.726 \times 28.02 = 48.36\ \text{g}$
@answer $48.4\ \text{g}$ of nitrogen
:::

:::summary
- The **theoretical yield** is the mass of product the stoichiometry predicts from the limiting reagent. The **actual yield** is the mass collected.
- $\text{percent yield} = \dfrac{\text{actual yield}}{\text{theoretical yield}} \times 100\%$, with the actual yield on top.
- The actual yield is usually lower: the reaction may not go to completion, side reactions make other products, product is lost while it is collected, and reactants may be impure.
- A percent yield above 100% means an error, most often a product that was still wet or impure when weighed.
- To find a percent yield from the mass of reactant, find the theoretical yield first, and divide by its unrounded value.
- To plan for a known yield, divide the mass wanted by the yield as a decimal to get the theoretical yield needed, then work back through moles to the reactant.
:::
