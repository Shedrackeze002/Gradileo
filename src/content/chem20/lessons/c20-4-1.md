A gas stove burns methane, $\ce{CH4}$, in oxygen from the air, and the flame gives off carbon dioxide and water vapour. Not a single atom is made or destroyed along the way: the carbon, hydrogen and oxygen atoms only change partners. A balanced chemical equation is the bookkeeping that shows it.

In this lesson you will balance chemical equations with the smallest whole-number coefficients, sort reactions into five types, and predict the type of a reaction from its reactants alone.

## Reading a chemical equation

A chemical equation describes a chemical change with formulas. The substances on the left of the arrow react; the substances on the right are formed.

:::definition Reactants and products
The **reactants** are the substances that react, written on the left of the arrow. The **products** are the new substances that form, written on the right. The arrow reads "react to form".
:::

In a reaction, atoms are rearranged into new substances, but none are created or destroyed. That is why the total mass of the products always equals the total mass of the reactants, the **law of conservation of mass**. An equation must show it: every element needs the same number of atoms on both sides.

:::definition Coefficient
A **coefficient** is the whole number in front of a formula. It counts the molecules or formula units of that substance, and a coefficient of 1 is not written. $\ce{2H2O}$ means two water molecules: 4 hydrogen atoms and 2 oxygen atoms.
:::

:::definition Balanced equation
An equation is **balanced** when each element has the same number of atoms on both sides. Chemists write it with the smallest whole-number coefficients that do this.
:::

Written with formulas only, methane burning is $\ce{CH4 + O2 -> CO2 + H2O}$. Count the atoms on each side:

| Element | Reactants | Products |
|---|---|---|
| C | 1 | 1 |
| H | 4 | 2 |
| O | 2 | $2 + 1 = 3$ |

Carbon matches, but hydrogen and oxygen do not. A 2 in front of $\ce{H2O}$ gives 4 hydrogen atoms on each side, and the right side then has $2 + 2 = 4$ oxygen atoms, so a 2 in front of $\ce{O2}$ finishes the job: $\ce{CH4 + 2O2 -> CO2 + 2H2O}$. Check: C 1 = 1, H 4 = 4, O 4 = 4.

:::mistake Change coefficients, never subscripts
To balance $\ce{H2 + O2 -> H2O}$ you might be tempted to write $\ce{H2O2}$ on the right so that the oxygen matches. But $\ce{H2O2}$ is hydrogen peroxide, a different substance. The subscripts say what a substance is, so they never change; only the coefficients do. The balanced equation is $\ce{2H2 + O2 -> 2H2O}$.
:::

## Balancing step by step

Balancing is organised trial and error. A few habits keep it short:

1. **Count** the atoms of each element on both sides.
2. **Start with the largest formula.** Leave its coefficient at 1 for now and balance its elements one at a time, metals first.
3. **Keep polyatomic ions together.** When a group such as $\ce{SO4}$, $\ce{NO3}$ or $\ce{PO4}$ appears unchanged on both sides, balance it as one unit.
4. **Even out pairs.** A diatomic element such as $\ce{O2}$, $\ce{N2}$ or $\ce{Br2}$ brings its atoms in pairs, so the other side often needs an even number. When the counts are 2 and 3, make both 6.
5. **Leave lone elements until last.** Hydrogen and oxygen usually come last, and so does any element written on its own, such as $\ce{Cu}$, $\ce{H2}$ or $\ce{O2}$: changing its coefficient changes nothing else.
6. **Check** every element, and make sure the coefficients are the smallest whole numbers. If they can all be divided by 2, divide.

In the practice questions you type each coefficient into the box in front of its formula. Leave a box empty when the coefficient is 1.

:::example A formation
Balance $\ce{Al + Br2 -> AlBr3}$.
@step Start with $\ce{AlBr3}$, which holds 3 bromine atoms. But $\ce{Br2}$ brings bromine in pairs, so the number of bromine atoms must be even. The smallest number that both 3 and 2 divide into is 6: $\ce{2AlBr3}$ and $\ce{3Br2}$ each hold 6.
@step $\ce{2AlBr3}$ holds 2 aluminium atoms, so write $\ce{2Al}$.
@answer $\ce{2Al + 3Br2 -> 2AlBr3}$. Check: Al 2 = 2, Br 6 = 6.
:::

:::example A decomposition
In a car's airbag, sodium azide decomposes into sodium and nitrogen gas. Balance $\ce{NaN3 -> Na + N2}$.
@step $\ce{NaN3}$ holds 3 nitrogen atoms, but $\ce{N2}$ brings them in pairs. Both 3 and 2 divide into 6, so write $\ce{2NaN3}$ and $\ce{3N2}$.
@step $\ce{2NaN3}$ holds 2 sodium atoms, so write $\ce{2Na}$.
@answer $\ce{2NaN3 -> 2Na + 3N2}$. Check: Na 2 = 2, N 6 = 6.
:::

:::example Keep the sulfate together
Balance $\ce{Al + H2SO4 -> Al2(SO4)3 + H2}$.
@step Start with the largest formula, $\ce{Al2(SO4)3}$, with a coefficient of 1. It holds 2 aluminium atoms, so write $\ce{2Al}$.
@step The sulfate group stays whole, so count it as one unit: 3 on the right needs $\ce{3H2SO4}$ on the left.
@step $\ce{3H2SO4}$ holds 6 hydrogen atoms, which is 3 molecules of $\ce{H2}$.
@answer $\ce{2Al + 3H2SO4 -> Al2(SO4)3 + 3H2}$. Check: Al 2 = 2, H 6 = 6, S 3 = 3, O 12 = 12.
:::

:::example Two compounds
Balance $\ce{CaCl2 + Na3PO4 -> Ca3(PO4)2 + NaCl}$.
@step Start with $\ce{Ca3(PO4)2}$. Its 3 calcium atoms need $\ce{3CaCl2}$; its 2 phosphate groups need $\ce{2Na3PO4}$.
@step The left side now has $2 \times 3 = 6$ sodium atoms and $3 \times 2 = 6$ chlorine atoms, so write $\ce{6NaCl}$.
@answer $\ce{3CaCl2 + 2Na3PO4 -> Ca3(PO4)2 + 6NaCl}$. Check: Ca 3 = 3, Cl 6 = 6, Na 6 = 6, P 2 = 2, O 8 = 8.
:::

:::tip Water as H–OH
When a metal reacts with water to make a hydroxide, think of water as H–OH. In $\ce{Ca + H2O -> Ca(OH)2 + H2}$, the two OH groups of $\ce{Ca(OH)2}$ need two water molecules, and the two H atoms left over make one $\ce{H2}$: $\ce{Ca + 2H2O -> Ca(OH)2 + H2}$. Check: Ca 1 = 1, H 4 = 4, O 2 = 2.
:::

:::

When a fuel burns, oxygen turns up in both products, so it goes last. Balance carbon first, then hydrogen, then oxygen.

:::example Propane
Balance $\ce{C3H8 + O2 -> CO2 + H2O}$.
@step Carbon: 3 atoms, so $\ce{3CO2}$.
@step Hydrogen: 8 atoms, so $\ce{4H2O}$.
@step Oxygen last: the right side has $3 \times 2 + 4 \times 1 = 10$ oxygen atoms, which is $\ce{5O2}$.
@answer $\ce{C3H8 + 5O2 -> 3CO2 + 4H2O}$. Check: C 3 = 3, H 8 = 8, O 10 = 10.
:::

:::example When oxygen needs a half
Balance $\ce{C2H6 + O2 -> CO2 + H2O}$.
@step Carbon: $\ce{2CO2}$. Hydrogen: 6 atoms, so $\ce{3H2O}$.
@step Oxygen: the right side has $2 \times 2 + 3 = 7$ oxygen atoms, which would be $3\tfrac{1}{2}$ molecules of $\ce{O2}$.
@step Coefficients must be whole numbers, so double every one: $\ce{2C2H6}$, $\ce{7O2}$, $\ce{4CO2}$ and $\ce{6H2O}$.
@answer $\ce{2C2H6 + 7O2 -> 4CO2 + 6H2O}$. Check: C 4 = 4, H 12 = 12, O 14 = 14.
:::

:::mistake Oxygen in the fuel
An alcohol or a sugar already contains oxygen. In $\ce{C2H5OH + O2 -> CO2 + H2O}$, carbon gives $\ce{2CO2}$ and the 6 hydrogen atoms give $\ce{3H2O}$, so the right side has $4 + 3 = 7$ oxygen atoms. One of them comes from the ethanol, so $\ce{O2}$ supplies only 6: $\ce{C2H5OH + 3O2 -> 2CO2 + 3H2O}$. Check: C 2 = 2, H 6 = 6, O 7 = 7.
:::

## Five types of reactions

Many reactions follow one of a few patterns. Recognising the pattern tells you what to expect from a reaction before you balance it.

| Type | Pattern | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Formation** | element + element → compound | $\ce{2Na + Cl2 -> 2NaCl}$ |
| **Simple decomposition** | compound → element + element | $\ce{2H2O -> 2H2 + O2}$ |
| **Single replacement** | element + compound → element + compound | $\ce{Zn + CuSO4 -> ZnSO4 + Cu}$ |
| **Double replacement** | compound + compound → compound + compound | $\ce{AgNO3 + NaCl -> AgCl + NaNO3}$ |
| **Complete combustion** | fuel + oxygen → carbon dioxide + water | $\ce{CH4 + 2O2 -> CO2 + 2H2O}$ |

In a **formation**, elements combine to make one compound. A **simple decomposition** is the reverse: a compound breaks down into its elements, usually when it is heated or when electricity passes through it.

In a **single replacement**, an element takes the place of a similar element in a compound. A metal replaces a metal ion, or the hydrogen of an acid or of water, as in $\ce{2Na + 2H2O -> 2NaOH + H2}$. A non-metal replaces a non-metal: $\ce{Cl2 + 2NaBr -> 2NaCl + Br2}$.

In a **double replacement**, two compounds, usually in solution, swap ions. It is the kind of reaction that forms a precipitate, as in lesson 3.2. A neutralisation from lesson 3.6 is a double replacement too: the hydrogen of the acid and the metal of the base swap places, as in $\ce{2HCl + Ca(OH)2 -> CaCl2 + 2H2O}$.

In a **complete combustion**, a fuel burns in plenty of oxygen and forms only carbon dioxide and water. The fuel is a hydrocarbon, a compound of carbon and hydrogen only, such as methane, $\ce{CH4}$, or octane, $\ce{C8H18}$; an alcohol, such as ethanol, $\ce{C2H5OH}$; or a sugar, such as glucose, $\ce{C6H12O6}$. With too little oxygen the burning is incomplete, and carbon monoxide or soot forms as well.

:::tip Element or compound?
An element's formula has one symbol: $\ce{Na}$, $\ce{O2}$, $\ce{P4}$. A compound's formula has two or more: $\ce{NaCl}$, $\ce{H2O}$, $\ce{CH4}$. Sort every reactant and product this way, then match the pattern.
:::

:::example Name the type
What type of reaction is $\ce{2Al + 3CuCl2 -> 2AlCl3 + 3Cu}$?
@step Reactants: $\ce{Al}$ is an element and $\ce{CuCl2}$ a compound. Products: $\ce{AlCl3}$ is a compound and $\ce{Cu}$ an element.
@step Element + compound → element + compound: aluminium has taken the place of copper.
@answer Single replacement.
:::

:::example Heating mercury(II) oxide
What type of reaction is $\ce{2HgO -> 2Hg + O2}$?
@step One compound reacts, and both products are elements.
@answer Simple decomposition.
:::

:::note Burning a metal or hydrogen
When magnesium burns in air, $\ce{2Mg + O2 -> 2MgO}$, two elements form one compound, so it is a formation. But it is also burning in oxygen, a combustion. Hydrogen burning, $\ce{2H2 + O2 -> 2H2O}$, is the same. Because these reactions fit two types at once, the practice questions that ask for a type leave them out. The combustions they ask about are fuels containing carbon burning to carbon dioxide and water.
:::

## Predicting the type from the reactants

You do not need the products to name the type. The reactants alone decide it:

1. **One reactant**, a compound: simple decomposition.
2. **Two elements**: formation.
3. **A hydrocarbon, alcohol or sugar with oxygen**, $\ce{O2}$: complete combustion.
4. **An element and a compound**, when it is not a fuel with oxygen: single replacement.
5. **Two compounds**: double replacement.

:::example Chlorine and sodium bromide
A reaction has the reactants $\ce{Cl2}$ and $\ce{NaBr}$. What type of reaction is it?
@step $\ce{Cl2}$ is an element; $\ce{NaBr}$ is a compound, and not a fuel.
@step Element + compound: chlorine takes the place of bromine. The whole equation is $\ce{Cl2 + 2NaBr -> 2NaCl + Br2}$.
@answer Single replacement.
:::

:::example Pentane and oxygen
A reaction has the reactants $\ce{C5H12}$ and $\ce{O2}$. What type of reaction is it?
@step $\ce{C5H12}$ contains only carbon and hydrogen, so it is a hydrocarbon, and it reacts with oxygen.
@step It burns to carbon dioxide and water: $\ce{C5H12 + 8O2 -> 5CO2 + 6H2O}$. Check: C 5 = 5, H 12 = 12, O 16 = 16.
@answer Complete combustion.
:::

:::mistake A fuel with oxygen is not a single replacement
$\ce{O2}$ is an element and $\ce{C5H12}$ is a compound, so pentane and oxygen look like element + compound. But nothing is replaced: the fuel burns, and all its carbon and hydrogen end up in carbon dioxide and water. Look for a fuel with oxygen before you call a reaction a single replacement.
:::

| Reactants | What they are | Type |
|---|---|---|
| $\ce{Mg}$ and $\ce{N2}$ | two elements | formation: $\ce{3Mg + N2 -> Mg3N2}$ |
| $\ce{Al2O3}$ | one compound | simple decomposition: $\ce{2Al2O3 -> 4Al + 3O2}$ |
| $\ce{Zn}$ and $\ce{HCl}$ | element and compound | single replacement: $\ce{Zn + 2HCl -> ZnCl2 + H2}$ |
| $\ce{BaCl2}$ and $\ce{Na2SO4}$ | two compounds | double replacement: $\ce{BaCl2 + Na2SO4 -> BaSO4 + 2NaCl}$ |
| $\ce{C2H5OH}$ and $\ce{O2}$ | an alcohol and oxygen | complete combustion: $\ce{C2H5OH + 3O2 -> 2CO2 + 3H2O}$ |

:::check c20-reaction-type 3
:::

:::summary
- Atoms are rearranged in a reaction, never created or destroyed, so a balanced equation has the same number of atoms of each element on both sides.
- Balance with **coefficients**, never by changing subscripts, and use the smallest whole numbers. Always finish with an atom check.
- Start with the largest formula, keep polyatomic ions together as one unit, and leave lone elements, hydrogen and oxygen until last.
- Combustion: balance carbon, then hydrogen, then oxygen. If oxygen needs a half, double every coefficient.
- **Formation**: element + element → compound. **Simple decomposition**: compound → elements. **Single replacement**: element + compound → element + compound. **Double replacement**: compound + compound → compound + compound, including neutralisation. **Complete combustion**: a hydrocarbon, alcohol or sugar burns in oxygen to carbon dioxide and water.
- The reactants alone decide the type; a fuel with oxygen is a combustion, not a single replacement.
:::
