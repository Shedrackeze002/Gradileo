Pour a clear, colourless solution of silver nitrate into a clear, colourless solution of sodium chloride, and a white cloud appears at once. Within minutes a white solid settles to the bottom of the beaker. Each solution held its own ions with no trouble, but mixing them brought together two ions that cannot stay dissolved side by side.

In this lesson you will see how much solute a solvent can hold, use **solubility rules** to decide whether an ionic compound has high or low solubility in water, and predict the **precipitate** that forms when two solutions are mixed.

## How much can dissolve?

Keep adding salt to a glass of water, stirring all the time, and eventually no more dissolves: the extra crystals just sit at the bottom. The water is holding as much salt as it can.

:::definition Solubility
The **solubility** of a substance is the greatest amount of it that dissolves in a given amount of solvent at a given temperature. It is often given in grams of solute per $100\ \text{g}$ of water, or in moles per litre of solution.
:::

:::definition Saturated and unsaturated solutions
A **saturated** solution holds as much solute as it can at its temperature, so any extra solid stays undissolved. An **unsaturated** solution holds less than that, so more solute can still dissolve.
:::

Dissolving does not stop in a saturated solution with solid at the bottom. Particles keep leaving the solid while dissolved particles join it again, at the same rate, so the amount dissolved stays the same.

:::example Saturated or unsaturated?
The solubility of potassium nitrate, $\ce{KNO3}$, is $32\ \text{g}$ per $100\ \text{g}$ of water at $20^\circ\text{C}$. A student stirs $25\ \text{g}$ of it into $100\ \text{g}$ of water at $20^\circ\text{C}$. Is the solution saturated or unsaturated? What happens when the student adds another $15\ \text{g}$?
@step $25\ \text{g}$ is less than $32\ \text{g}$, so all of it dissolves. The solution is unsaturated: $32 - 25 = 7\ \text{g}$ more could still dissolve.
@step After the second portion there is $25 + 15 = 40\ \text{g}$ in all, but only $32\ \text{g}$ can dissolve.
@answer At first the solution is unsaturated. After the second portion it is saturated, and $40 - 32 = 8\ \text{g}$ of solid stays at the bottom.
:::

Solubility depends on temperature, so a solubility value always comes with one. Most solids dissolve better in hot water: the solubility of potassium nitrate rises to about $110\ \text{g}$ per $100\ \text{g}$ of water at $60^\circ\text{C}$. Gases do the opposite. They are less soluble in warm water, which is why a warm fizzy drink goes flat sooner than a cold one.

:::mistake Saturated is not the same as concentrated
"Saturated" says the solution holds all it can; "concentrated" says it holds a lot. A saturated solution of a compound with very low solubility, such as silver chloride, holds only a trace of it and is extremely dilute. An unsaturated sugar solution can be very concentrated.
:::

## High and low solubility

Ionic compounds differ enormously in how well they dissolve: a litre of water dissolves hundreds of grams of some, and only a tiny trace of others. In this course an ionic compound has either **high solubility**, when a lot of it dissolves, or **low solubility**, when only a tiny amount dissolves. Low solubility does not mean none at all, but it is so little that when its ions meet in a solution, nearly all of them come out as a solid.

The **solubility rules** sort ionic compounds by their anion. Each rule says whether compounds of that anion generally have high or low solubility, and lists the cations that are exceptions:

| Anion | High solubility | Low solubility |
|---|---|---|
| nitrate, $\ce{NO3-}$ | all | none |
| acetate, $\ce{CH3COO-}$ | all | none |
| chloride $\ce{Cl-}$, bromide $\ce{Br-}$, iodide $\ce{I-}$ | most | those of $\ce{Ag+}$, $\ce{Pb^2+}$ and $\ce{Cu+}$ |
| sulfate, $\ce{SO4^2-}$ | most | those of $\ce{Ca^2+}$, $\ce{Sr^2+}$, $\ce{Ba^2+}$, $\ce{Pb^2+}$ and $\ce{Ag+}$ |
| hydroxide, $\ce{OH-}$ | those of group 1 ions, $\ce{NH4+}$, $\ce{Sr^2+}$ and $\ce{Ba^2+}$ | most |
| carbonate $\ce{CO3^2-}$, phosphate $\ce{PO4^3-}$ | those of group 1 ions and $\ce{NH4+}$ | most |
| sulfide, $\ce{S^2-}$ | those of group 1 and group 2 ions and $\ce{NH4+}$ | most |

Each question states the rule it uses as a sentence, which you can read straight from a row of the table. The sulfate row says: sulfates have high solubility, except those of $\ce{Ca^2+}$, $\ce{Sr^2+}$, $\ce{Ba^2+}$, $\ce{Pb^2+}$ and $\ce{Ag+}$, which have low solubility. The carbonate row says: carbonates have low solubility, except those of group 1 ions and $\ce{NH4+}$, which have high solubility.

:::note Always high solubility
In every row of the table, compounds of the **group 1 ions** ($\ce{Li+}$, $\ce{Na+}$, $\ce{K+}$, $\ce{Rb+}$ and $\ce{Cs+}$) and of the **ammonium ion**, $\ce{NH4+}$, have high solubility. So do all **nitrates** and all **acetates**. A compound that contains any of these ions has high solubility.
:::

To use the rules, find the row for the compound's anion first. Then check whether its cation is one of the exceptions in that row.

:::example A chloride
Does lead(II) chloride, $\ce{PbCl2}$, have high or low solubility in water?
@step The anion is chloride. Chlorides have high solubility, except those of $\ce{Ag+}$, $\ce{Pb^2+}$ and $\ce{Cu+}$, which have low solubility.
@step The cation is $\ce{Pb^2+}$, one of the exceptions.
@answer Lead(II) chloride has low solubility.
:::

:::example A hydroxide
Does barium hydroxide, $\ce{Ba(OH)2}$, have high or low solubility in water?
@step The anion is hydroxide. Hydroxides have low solubility, except those of group 1 ions, $\ce{NH4+}$, $\ce{Sr^2+}$ and $\ce{Ba^2+}$, which have high solubility.
@step The cation is $\ce{Ba^2+}$, one of the exceptions.
@answer Barium hydroxide has high solubility.
:::

:::mistake Start with the anion
Silver appears among the exceptions in several rows, but silver nitrate, $\ce{AgNO3}$, has high solubility: all nitrates do. Always find the anion's row first. And notice which way each row runs: for hydroxides, carbonates, phosphates and sulfides, the exceptions are the compounds with **high** solubility.
:::

## Predicting precipitates

:::definition Precipitate
A **precipitate** is a solid that forms when two solutions are mixed, because the mixture makes a compound with low solubility. It is written with the state symbol (s).
:::

Before mixing, each solution holds its own ions: silver nitrate solution holds $\ce{Ag+}$ and $\ce{NO3-}$ ions, and sodium chloride solution holds $\ce{Na+}$ and $\ce{Cl-}$ ions. Once the solutions are mixed, all four ions move about in the same water, and each cation meets the other anion. The ions **swap partners**: $\ce{Ag+}$ with $\ce{Cl-}$, and $\ce{Na+}$ with $\ce{NO3-}$. If one of the new pairs has low solubility, those ions come out of the solution together as a precipitate.

1. **List** the ions in each solution.
2. **Swap partners** and write the formulas of the two possible new compounds, balancing the charges.
3. **Check** each new compound against the solubility rules.
4. A new compound with **low solubility** forms a precipitate. If both new compounds have high solubility, no precipitate forms, and the ions simply stay dissolved.

:::example Silver nitrate and sodium chloride
Solutions of silver nitrate, $\ce{AgNO3}$, and sodium chloride, $\ce{NaCl}$, are mixed. Which precipitate forms, if any?
@step The ions are $\ce{Ag+}$ and $\ce{NO3-}$ from one solution, $\ce{Na+}$ and $\ce{Cl-}$ from the other.
@step The possible new compounds are $\ce{AgCl}$ and $\ce{NaNO3}$.
@step $\ce{NaNO3}$ has high solubility: it is a nitrate, and $\ce{Na+}$ is a group 1 ion. $\ce{AgCl}$ has low solubility: $\ce{Ag+}$ is an exception to the chloride rule.
@answer $\ce{AgCl(s)}$ precipitates, as a white solid: $\ce{AgNO3(aq) + NaCl(aq) -> AgCl(s) + NaNO3(aq)}$.
:::

:::example Lead(II) nitrate and potassium iodide
Solutions of lead(II) nitrate, $\ce{Pb(NO3)2}$, and potassium iodide, $\ce{KI}$, are mixed. Which precipitate forms, if any?
@step The ions are $\ce{Pb^2+}$ and $\ce{NO3-}$, and $\ce{K+}$ and $\ce{I-}$.
@step Swap partners: two $\ce{I-}$ ions balance one $\ce{Pb^2+}$ ion, giving $\ce{PbI2}$; $\ce{K+}$ and $\ce{NO3-}$ give $\ce{KNO3}$.
@step $\ce{KNO3}$ has high solubility. $\ce{PbI2}$ has low solubility: $\ce{Pb^2+}$ is an exception to the iodide rule.
@answer $\ce{PbI2(s)}$ precipitates, as a bright yellow solid: $\ce{Pb(NO3)2(aq) + 2KI(aq) -> PbI2(s) + 2KNO3(aq)}$.
:::

:::example No precipitate
Solutions of zinc nitrate, $\ce{Zn(NO3)2}$, and sodium sulfate, $\ce{Na2SO4}$, are mixed. Which precipitate forms, if any?
@step The possible new compounds are $\ce{ZnSO4}$ and $\ce{NaNO3}$.
@step $\ce{NaNO3}$ has high solubility. Sulfates have high solubility, except those of $\ce{Ca^2+}$, $\ce{Sr^2+}$, $\ce{Ba^2+}$, $\ce{Pb^2+}$ and $\ce{Ag+}$; $\ce{Zn^2+}$ is not one of them, so $\ce{ZnSO4}$ has high solubility too.
@answer No precipitate forms. All four ions stay dissolved in the mixture.
:::

:::tip A metal nitrate and a sodium or potassium salt
When one solution is a metal nitrate and the other is a sodium or potassium salt, one possible new compound is always sodium nitrate or potassium nitrate. All nitrates and all compounds of group 1 ions have high solubility, so that one never precipitates. Check only the other compound: the metal ion with the anion of the sodium or potassium salt.
:::

:::mistake The precipitate is a new compound
The compounds you started with were already dissolved, so neither of them can be the precipitate: look only at the new pairs. Balance the charges in each new formula, too: $\ce{Pb^2+}$ and $\ce{I-}$ make $\ce{PbI2}$, not $\ce{PbI}$.
:::

| Solutions mixed | Possible new compounds | Precipitate |
|---|---|---|
| $\ce{Ca(NO3)2}$ and $\ce{Na2CO3}$ | $\ce{CaCO3}$, $\ce{NaNO3}$ | $\ce{CaCO3(s)}$ |
| $\ce{Cu(NO3)2}$ and $\ce{NaOH}$ | $\ce{Cu(OH)2}$, $\ce{NaNO3}$ | $\ce{Cu(OH)2(s)}$ |
| $\ce{Ba(NO3)2}$ and $\ce{K2SO4}$ | $\ce{BaSO4}$, $\ce{KNO3}$ | $\ce{BaSO4(s)}$ |
| $\ce{Mg(NO3)2}$ and $\ce{KCl}$ | $\ce{MgCl2}$, $\ce{KNO3}$ | none |

:::note Net ionic equations
In the silver chloride example, the $\ce{Na+}$ and $\ce{NO3-}$ ions are still dissolved at the end, unchanged. Ions that take no part in a reaction are called **spectator ions**. Leaving them out gives the **net ionic equation**, which shows only the ions that form the precipitate: $\ce{Ag+(aq) + Cl-(aq) -> AgCl(s)}$. For lead(II) iodide it is $\ce{Pb^2+(aq) + 2I-(aq) -> PbI2(s)}$.
:::

:::summary
- **Solubility** is the greatest amount of a solute that dissolves in a given amount of solvent at a given temperature.
- A **saturated** solution holds all the solute it can; an **unsaturated** solution can dissolve more.
- The solubility rules sort ionic compounds by anion into **high** and **low** solubility. Find the anion's row, then check whether the cation is an exception.
- Compounds of group 1 ions and $\ce{NH4+}$, and all nitrates and acetates, have high solubility.
- When two solutions are mixed, the ions swap partners. A new compound with low solubility forms a **precipitate**, (s); if both new compounds have high solubility, no precipitate forms.
- Mixing a metal nitrate with a sodium or potassium salt: only the metal ion with the other anion can precipitate.
:::
