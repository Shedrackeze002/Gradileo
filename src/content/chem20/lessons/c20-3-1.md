Stir a spoonful of table salt into a glass of water and it disappears. Stir a spoonful of sugar into another glass and that disappears too. The two glasses look exactly the same, but dip a pair of wires from a battery and a light bulb into each, and only the salt water lights the bulb. Something in the salt water carries an electric current, and the sugar water has none of it.

In this lesson you will describe a solution by its **solute** and **solvent**, sort dissolved substances into **electrolytes** and **non-electrolytes**, and write **dissociation equations** that show the ions an ionic compound releases in water.

## Solutions, solutes and solvents

:::definition Solution, solute and solvent
A **solution** is a homogeneous mixture: it is the same all the way through, and its particles are too small to see or to settle out. The **solute** is the substance that dissolves, and the **solvent** is the substance it dissolves in. When both start as liquids, the solvent is the one present in the larger amount.
:::

In salt water, sodium chloride is the solute and water is the solvent. A solution can hold more than one solute: sea water contains many different salts, and a fizzy drink holds both sugar and carbon dioxide in the same water.

:::definition Aqueous solution
A solution in which water is the solvent is an **aqueous** solution. The state symbol (aq) marks a substance dissolved in water: $\ce{NaCl(aq)}$ is a solution of sodium chloride, while $\ce{NaCl(s)}$ is the solid. The other state symbols are (s) for solid, (l) for liquid and (g) for gas.
:::

Water is often called the **universal solvent**, because it dissolves more substances, and a wider range of them, than any other common liquid. Its molecules are polar (lesson 1.7) and form hydrogen bonds (lesson 1.8), so water attracts ions and other polar molecules strongly. The name is an exaggeration, though. As "like dissolves like" predicts, non-polar substances such as oil and wax hardly dissolve in water at all.

:::example Solute and solvent
A cleaning solution is made by mixing $50\ \text{mL}$ of ethanol, $\ce{C2H5OH}$, with $200\ \text{mL}$ of water. Name the solute and the solvent, and write the formula of the solute with its state symbol.
@step Both substances are liquids, so the solvent is the one present in the larger amount: water.
@step Ethanol is the solute. It is dissolved in water, so its state symbol is (aq).
@answer The solute is ethanol, $\ce{C2H5OH(aq)}$, and the solvent is water. The solution is aqueous.
:::

## Electrolytes and non-electrolytes

An electric current is a flow of charged particles. In a metal wire, electrons carry the current. In a solution there are no free electrons, so the current must be carried by **ions** moving through the liquid.

:::definition Electrolyte and non-electrolyte
An **electrolyte** is a substance whose solution conducts electricity, because the solution contains ions that can move. A **non-electrolyte** is a substance whose solution does not conduct, because it contains no ions to carry a current.
:::

The **conductivity test** tells them apart. A conductivity tester has two metal electrodes wired to a battery and a light bulb or a meter. Dip the electrodes into a solution: if it contains ions that can move, they carry charge from one electrode to the other, the circuit is complete and the bulb lights. With no ions, the bulb stays dark. Pure water conducts so poorly that the bulb stays dark in it, so any glow must come from the solute.

Whether a solute forms ions depends on what kind of substance it is. There are three cases, and each has its own reason:

| Kind of solute | Examples | In water | Electrolyte? |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ionic compound, including hydroxide bases | $\ce{NaCl}$, $\ce{CaCl2}$, $\ce{CuSO4}$, $\ce{NH4Cl}$, $\ce{NaOH}$, $\ce{KOH}$ | dissociates into ions that can move | yes |
| Acid | $\ce{HCl}$, $\ce{HNO3}$, $\ce{CH3COOH}$ | ionises, forming ions | yes |
| Molecular compound that is not an acid | sucrose $\ce{C12H22O11}$, glucose $\ce{C6H12O6}$, ethanol $\ce{C2H5OH}$, methanol $\ce{CH3OH}$ | its molecules stay whole | no |

To recognise an ionic compound, look for a metal ion or the ammonium ion, $\ce{NH4+}$, at the start of the formula. An acid usually has its hydrogen written first, as in $\ce{HCl}$ and $\ce{HNO3}$, or ends in COOH, as in acetic acid, $\ce{CH3COOH}$. Sugars and alcohols are molecular compounds made only of non-metals, and they are non-electrolytes.

:::example An ionic compound with no metal
Ammonium chloride, $\ce{NH4Cl}$, is dissolved in water. Is the solution an electrolyte or a non-electrolyte?
@step The compound contains no metal, but $\ce{NH4+}$ is the ammonium ion. It takes the place of a metal ion, so ammonium chloride is an ionic compound.
@step In water it dissociates: $\ce{NH4Cl(s) -> NH4+(aq) + Cl-(aq)}$.
@answer An electrolyte: the ionic compound dissociates into ions that can move.
:::

:::example A molecular compound that conducts
Hydrogen chloride, $\ce{HCl}$, is made of molecules of two non-metals. It is dissolved in water. Is the solution an electrolyte or a non-electrolyte?
@step Hydrogen chloride is molecular, so it has no ions of its own. But it is an acid.
@step In water, its molecules react with water molecules and form ions (see the last section).
@answer An electrolyte: the acid ionises in water, forming ions.
:::

:::example A sugar
Glucose, $\ce{C6H12O6}$, dissolves easily in water. Is the solution an electrolyte or a non-electrolyte?
@step Glucose is a molecular compound of non-metals, and it is not an acid.
@step Its molecules spread out through the water, but each one stays whole.
@answer A non-electrolyte: its molecules stay whole, so there are no ions to carry a current.
:::

:::mistake Dissolving is not conducting
Sugar dissolves just as completely as salt, yet sugar water does not conduct. Dissolving alone is not enough: the solution conducts only if it contains **ions**. And a solution needs no metal to conduct; salt water conducts because of its moving ions, not because sodium is a metal.
:::

:::check c20-electrolyte 2
:::

:::mistake The OH in an alcohol is not hydroxide
Ethanol, $\ce{C2H5OH}$, and methanol, $\ce{CH3OH}$, end in OH, but they are not bases. Their OH group is joined to carbon by a covalent bond, and there is no metal ion, so they do not release $\ce{OH-}$ ions. Hydroxide bases such as $\ce{NaOH}$ and $\ce{KOH}$ are ionic compounds of a metal ion and the hydroxide ion.
:::

Acetic acid, $\ce{CH3COOH}$, the acid in vinegar, is a weak acid: only a small fraction of its molecules ionise, so its solution conducts only weakly and the bulb glows dimly. It still contains ions, so it is still an electrolyte. Lesson 3.8 compares strong and weak acids.

## Dissociation of ionic compounds

Solid sodium chloride does not conduct: its ions are locked in place in the crystal lattice (lesson 1.2). In water, the lattice comes apart. Polar water molecules crowd round the ions at the surface of the crystal, the negative oxygen ends of the molecules facing the cations and the positive hydrogen ends facing the anions, and pull the ions away one by one. Each ion then moves through the solution surrounded by water molecules.

:::definition Dissociation
**Dissociation** is the separation of an ionic compound into its ions as it dissolves. A **dissociation equation** shows the solid compound on the left and the separate aqueous ions on the right: $\ce{NaCl(s) -> Na+(aq) + Cl-(aq)}$.
:::

One **formula unit** is the smallest group of ions the formula describes: one $\ce{Na+}$ and one $\ce{Cl-}$ for $\ce{NaCl}$. To write a dissociation equation:

1. **Identify** the cation and the anion, with their charges.
2. **Count** each ion in one formula unit and write the counts as coefficients. A subscript after a bracket counts the polyatomic ion inside it.
3. **Keep polyatomic ions whole.** The subscripts inside an ion, such as the 4 in $\ce{SO4^2-}$, belong to the ion and do not change.
4. **Check** that the charges on the right add up to zero.

:::example Calcium chloride
Write the dissociation equation for calcium chloride, $\ce{CaCl2}$. How many ions does each formula unit release?
@step The ions are $\ce{Ca^2+}$ and $\ce{Cl-}$. The subscript 2 means two chloride ions for each calcium ion.
@step $\ce{CaCl2(s) -> Ca^2+(aq) + 2Cl-(aq)}$
@step Check: $(+2) + 2 \times (-1) = 0$.
@answer $\ce{CaCl2(s) -> Ca^2+(aq) + 2Cl-(aq)}$: 1 cation and 2 anions, 3 ions in all.
:::

:::example Aluminium sulfate
Write the dissociation equation for aluminium sulfate, $\ce{Al2(SO4)3}$.
@step The ions are $\ce{Al^3+}$ and the polyatomic sulfate ion, $\ce{SO4^2-}$.
@step The subscript 2 gives two aluminium ions. The 3 after the bracket gives three sulfate ions, each kept whole.
@step Check: $2 \times (+3) + 3 \times (-2) = 6 - 6 = 0$.
@answer $\ce{Al2(SO4)3(s) -> 2Al^3+(aq) + 3SO4^2-(aq)}$: 2 cations and 3 anions, 5 ions in all.
:::

:::mistake Polyatomic ions stay whole
Do not split $\ce{SO4^2-}$ into sulfur and oxygen, and do not join the three sulfate ions into one: $\ce{(SO4)3^6-}$ and $\ce{Al2^6+}$ are not ions. Each ion keeps its own charge, and the number of ions becomes a coefficient. Metal ions are always positive. And $\ce{Al2(SO4)3(s) -> Al2(SO4)3(aq)}$ shows the compound dissolving, but not the ions it forms.
:::

The same steps give these equations:

| Compound | Dissociation equation | Ions per formula unit |
|---|---|---|
| magnesium nitrate | $\ce{Mg(NO3)2(s) -> Mg^2+(aq) + 2NO3-(aq)}$ | 3 |
| ammonium sulfate | $\ce{(NH4)2SO4(s) -> 2NH4+(aq) + SO4^2-(aq)}$ | 3 |
| potassium dichromate | $\ce{K2Cr2O7(s) -> 2K+(aq) + Cr2O7^2-(aq)}$ | 3 |
| potassium phosphate | $\ce{K3PO4(s) -> 3K+(aq) + PO4^3-(aq)}$ | 4 |

A dissociation equation works for moles as well as for single formula units. One mole of $\ce{CaCl2}$ releases one mole of $\ce{Ca^2+}$ ions and two moles of $\ce{Cl-}$ ions: three moles of ions in all.

:::example Moles of ions
$0.40\ \text{mol}$ of aluminium sulfate, $\ce{Al2(SO4)3}$, dissolves completely in water. How many moles of ions does it release in total?
@step $\ce{Al2(SO4)3(s) -> 2Al^3+(aq) + 3SO4^2-(aq)}$
@step Each mole releases $2 + 3 = 5$ moles of ions.
@step $0.40 \times 5 = 2.00\ \text{mol}$
@answer $2.00\ \text{mol}$ of ions: $0.80\ \text{mol}$ of $\ce{Al^3+}$ and $1.20\ \text{mol}$ of $\ce{SO4^2-}$.
:::

:::tip Count ions, not atoms
A formula unit of $\ce{Al2(SO4)3}$ holds 17 atoms but only 5 ions. Read the counts from the coefficients of the dissociation equation, never from the total number of atoms.
:::

## Ionisation of acids

An acid is a molecular compound, yet its solution conducts. Pure hydrogen chloride is a gas made of $\ce{HCl}$ molecules, with no ions at all. When it dissolves, each molecule reacts with a water molecule: it gives a hydrogen ion to the water molecule, forming a **hydronium ion**, $\ce{H3O+}$, and a chloride ion. The hydronium ions are what make the solution acidic; lesson 3.6 looks at acids and bases in depth.

$$\ce{HCl(aq) + H2O(l) -> H3O+(aq) + Cl-(aq)}$$

:::definition Ionisation
**Ionisation** is the formation of ions from a molecular substance. An acid ionises when its molecules react with water.
:::

Both processes give a solution full of ions, but the ions come from different places:

| | Dissociation | Ionisation |
|---|---|---|
| Happens to | ionic compounds, including hydroxide bases | acids, which are molecular |
| Before dissolving | ions already held in a crystal lattice | neutral molecules, with no ions |
| In water | the existing ions separate | molecules react with water to form new ions |
| Example | $\ce{NaOH(s) -> Na+(aq) + OH-(aq)}$ | $\ce{HNO3(aq) + H2O(l) -> H3O+(aq) + NO3-(aq)}$ |

:::summary
- A **solution** is a homogeneous mixture of a **solute** dissolved in a **solvent**. In an **aqueous** solution, marked (aq), the solvent is water.
- Water, the "universal solvent", dissolves many ionic and polar substances because its molecules are polar, but it does not dissolve non-polar substances such as oil.
- An **electrolyte** forms a solution that conducts, because the solution contains ions that can move. A **non-electrolyte** forms a solution with no ions.
- Ionic compounds (including hydroxide bases) are electrolytes: they dissociate into ions that can move. Acids are electrolytes: they ionise in water, forming ions. Other molecular compounds, such as sugars and alcohols, are non-electrolytes: their molecules stay whole.
- **Dissociation:** an ionic compound separates into its ions, for example $\ce{Al2(SO4)3(s) -> 2Al^3+(aq) + 3SO4^2-(aq)}$. Polyatomic ions stay whole, and the subscripts become coefficients.
- Moles of ions = moles of compound $\times$ ions per formula unit.
- **Ionisation:** an acid's molecules react with water to form ions, such as $\ce{H3O+}$.
:::
