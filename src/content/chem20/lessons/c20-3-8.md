Drop a strip of magnesium ribbon into a beaker of $0.10\ \text{mol/L}$ hydrochloric acid and it fizzes hard. Drop an identical strip into $0.10\ \text{mol/L}$ acetic acid and only a few bubbles rise. Both acids have the same concentration, yet one behaves far more like an acid than the other.

In this lesson you will see why some acids are **strong** and others **weak**, learn the six common strong acids, tell strength apart from concentration, and find the pH of a solution of a strong acid or a strong base.

## Strong and weak acids

In lesson 3.6 you saw that an acid gives a hydrogen ion to water, forming the hydronium ion, $\ce{H3O+}$. This reaction is called **ionisation**, because molecules of the acid become ions. Acids differ in how far it goes.

:::definition Ionisation
The reaction of a molecular substance with water to form ions. An acid ionises when its molecules give hydrogen ions to water molecules.
:::

:::definition Strong acid
An acid that ionises completely in water: every molecule gives its hydrogen ion to water, so no acid molecules are left. $\ce{HCl(aq) + H2O(l) -> H3O+(aq) + Cl-(aq)}$
:::

:::definition Weak acid
An acid that ionises only partly in water: only a small fraction of its molecules ionise, and most stay whole. The double arrow shows that the reaction goes both ways. $\ce{CH3COOH(aq) + H2O(l) <=> H3O+(aq) + CH3COO-(aq)}$
:::

Picture 100 molecules of each acid dropped into water at a concentration of $0.10\ \text{mol/L}$. The hydrochloric acid ionises completely, while only about 1 molecule in 100 of acetic acid does.

| Particles in solution | Hydrochloric acid, $\ce{HCl}$ | Acetic acid, $\ce{CH3COOH}$ |
|---|---|---|
| Acid molecules still whole | 0 | about 99 |
| Hydronium ions, $\ce{H3O+}$ | 100 | about 1 |
| Anions | 100 $\ce{Cl-}$ | about 1 $\ce{CH3COO-}$ |

Only a handful of common acids are strong, so it pays to learn them. Most other acids are weak.

| Strong acid | Formula | Weak acid | Formula |
|---|---|---|---|
| hydrochloric acid | $\ce{HCl}$ | hydrofluoric acid | $\ce{HF}$ |
| hydrobromic acid | $\ce{HBr}$ | nitrous acid | $\ce{HNO2}$ |
| hydroiodic acid | $\ce{HI}$ | sulfurous acid | $\ce{H2SO3}$ |
| nitric acid | $\ce{HNO3}$ | phosphoric acid | $\ce{H3PO4}$ |
| sulfuric acid | $\ce{H2SO4}$ | carbonic acid | $\ce{H2CO3}$ |
| perchloric acid | $\ce{HClO4}$ | acetic acid | $\ce{CH3COOH}$ |

:::mistake Look-alike acids
Hydrofluoric acid, $\ce{HF}$, looks like $\ce{HCl}$, $\ce{HBr}$ and $\ce{HI}$, but it is weak. The "-ous" acids, nitrous acid and sulfurous acid, are weak too, even though nitric acid and sulfuric acid are strong. Check the list rather than the look of the formula.
:::

## Strong is not the same as concentrated

In everyday speech, "strong" often means concentrated: a strong cup of coffee has plenty dissolved in it. In chemistry, **strong** and **concentrated** mean different things.

:::definition Strength and concentration
**Concentration** says how much acid was dissolved: the moles of acid per litre of solution. **Strength** says how much of it ionises: all of it for a strong acid, a small fraction for a weak one.
:::

Any acid can be made concentrated or dilute by changing how much of it is dissolved, but nothing you do to the solution changes whether the acid is strong or weak. So all four combinations exist:

| | Strong acid | Weak acid |
|---|---|---|
| Concentrated | $6.0\ \text{mol/L}$ $\ce{HCl}$ | $6.0\ \text{mol/L}$ $\ce{CH3COOH}$ |
| Dilute | $0.0010\ \text{mol/L}$ $\ce{HCl}$ | $0.0010\ \text{mol/L}$ $\ce{CH3COOH}$ |

A dilute solution of a strong acid can even hold fewer hydronium ions than a concentrated solution of a weak acid. To compare strengths fairly, compare solutions of the **same** concentration.

## Comparing acids of the same concentration

At the same concentration, a strong acid makes far more $\ce{H3O+}$ ions than a weak acid, because every one of its molecules ionises. That one fact explains every difference you can measure. The much higher $[\ce{H3O+}]$ gives the strong acid a **lower pH**. Its solution holds more ions to carry charge, so it **conducts electricity better**. And more hydronium ions collide with the metal each second, so it **reacts faster with magnesium ribbon**.

| $0.10\ \text{mol/L}$ solution | Hydrochloric acid | Acetic acid |
|---|---|---|
| $[\ce{H3O+}]$ | $0.10\ \text{mol/L}$ | about $1.3 \times 10^{-3}\ \text{mol/L}$ |
| pH | 1.00 | about 2.9 |
| Conductivity | high | low |
| With magnesium ribbon | fast fizzing | slow bubbling |

:::example Which has the higher pH?
Solutions of nitric acid and nitrous acid both have a concentration of $0.50\ \text{mol/L}$. Which solution has the higher pH?
@step Nitric acid is strong and nitrous acid is weak, so nitric acid has the higher $[\ce{H3O+}]$: a lower pH, better conductivity and a faster reaction.
@step The solution with fewer hydronium ions has the higher pH.
@answer Nitrous acid, $\ce{HNO2}$
:::

:::mistake Equal concentrations, equal pH?
"They are the same, because the concentrations are equal" is wrong. Concentration says how much acid was dissolved; strength says how much of it ionises. At the same concentration, the strong acid always has the higher $[\ce{H3O+}]$ and the lower pH.
:::

## Strong bases

Bases come in strong and weak kinds too. Metal hydroxides are ionic compounds, so they do not ionise: they **dissociate** into the ions they are already made of.

:::definition Strong base
A base that dissociates completely in water, releasing all of its hydroxide ions. The group 1 hydroxides, such as $\ce{LiOH}$, $\ce{NaOH}$ and $\ce{KOH}$, are strong bases, and so are barium hydroxide, $\ce{Ba(OH)2}$, and strontium hydroxide, $\ce{Sr(OH)2}$.
:::

Each formula unit of a group 1 hydroxide releases one $\ce{OH-}$ ion, but barium and strontium hydroxide release **two**:

$$\ce{NaOH(s) -> Na+(aq) + OH-(aq)} \qquad \ce{Ba(OH)2(s) -> Ba^2+(aq) + 2OH-(aq)}$$

Ammonia is a common weak base. Only a small fraction of its molecules take a hydrogen ion from water: $\ce{NH3(aq) + H2O(l) <=> NH4+(aq) + OH-(aq)}$.

## pH of a strong acid or base

Because a strong acid ionises completely, each mole of a strong acid such as $\ce{HCl}$ makes one mole of $\ce{H3O+}$. So $[\ce{H3O+}]$ equals the acid's concentration, and the pH follows from lesson 3.7.

:::example A strong acid
What is the pH of a $3.5 \times 10^{-3}\ \text{mol/L}$ solution of nitric acid, $\ce{HNO3}$, to two decimal places? It is a strong acid, so $[\ce{H3O+}]$ equals the acid's concentration.
@step $[\ce{H3O+}] = 3.5 \times 10^{-3}\ \text{mol/L}$
@step $\text{pH} = -\log(3.5 \times 10^{-3}) = 2.46$
@answer $\text{pH} = 2.46$
:::

:::check c20-strong-ph 1
:::

A group 1 hydroxide dissociates completely and releases one $\ce{OH-}$ ion per formula unit, so $[\ce{OH-}]$ equals its concentration. Find the pOH, then use $\text{pH} + \text{pOH} = 14.00$ at $25^\circ\text{C}$.

:::example A group 1 hydroxide
What is the pH of a $4.0 \times 10^{-2}\ \text{mol/L}$ solution of potassium hydroxide, $\ce{KOH}$, to two decimal places? It dissociates completely, so $[\ce{OH-}]$ equals its concentration. At $25^\circ\text{C}$, $\text{pH} + \text{pOH} = 14.00$.
@step $\text{pOH} = -\log(4.0 \times 10^{-2}) = 1.40$
@step $\text{pH} = 14.00 - 1.40 = 12.60$
@answer $\text{pH} = 12.60$
:::

:::check c20-strong-ph 2
:::

Barium hydroxide and strontium hydroxide release two $\ce{OH-}$ ions per formula unit, so for a solution of concentration $c$, $[\ce{OH-}] = 2c$.

:::example Barium hydroxide
What is the pH of a $2.5 \times 10^{-3}\ \text{mol/L}$ solution of barium hydroxide, $\ce{Ba(OH)2}$, to two decimal places? It dissociates completely. At $25^\circ\text{C}$, $\text{pH} + \text{pOH} = 14.00$.
@step $\ce{Ba(OH)2(s) -> Ba^2+(aq) + 2OH-(aq)}$
@step $[\ce{OH-}] = 2 \times 2.5 \times 10^{-3} = 5.00 \times 10^{-3}\ \text{mol/L}$
@step $\text{pOH} = -\log(5.00 \times 10^{-3}) = 2.30$
@answer $\text{pH} = 14.00 - 2.30 = 11.70$
:::

:::mistake Forgetting the second hydroxide ion
Using $2.5 \times 10^{-3}\ \text{mol/L}$ as $[\ce{OH-}]$ gives a pOH of 2.60 and a pH of 11.40, which is wrong. Write the dissociation equation first, and multiply by the number of $\ce{OH-}$ ions in the formula.
:::

:::check c20-strong-ph 3
:::

:::note Sulfuric acid
Sulfuric acid, $\ce{H2SO4}$, has two hydrogen ions to give. Only the first is given up completely, so its pH is not found as simply as for $\ce{HCl}$, $\ce{HBr}$, $\ce{HI}$, $\ce{HNO3}$ or $\ce{HClO4}$, which give up one hydrogen ion each.
:::

:::summary
- A **strong acid** ionises completely in water; a **weak acid** ionises only partly, and most of its molecules stay whole.
- The six common strong acids are $\ce{HCl}$, $\ce{HBr}$, $\ce{HI}$, $\ce{HNO3}$, $\ce{H2SO4}$ and $\ce{HClO4}$. Most other acids, including $\ce{HF}$ and $\ce{CH3COOH}$, are weak.
- Concentration says how much acid was dissolved; strength says how much of it ionises.
- At the same concentration, a strong acid has a much higher $[\ce{H3O+}]$: a lower pH, better conductivity and a faster reaction with magnesium.
- The group 1 hydroxides, $\ce{Ba(OH)2}$ and $\ce{Sr(OH)2}$ are strong bases: they dissociate completely. The last two release two $\ce{OH-}$ ions per formula unit.
- Strong acid: $[\ce{H3O+}] = c$. Group 1 hydroxide: $[\ce{OH-}] = c$. Barium or strontium hydroxide: $[\ce{OH-}] = 2c$. Then $\text{pOH} = -\log[\ce{OH-}]$ and $\text{pH} = 14.00 - \text{pOH}$.
:::
