Sodium is a soft, shiny metal that reacts violently with water, and chlorine is a poisonous yellow-green gas. Yet together they make sodium chloride, the ordinary table salt you sprinkle on food. What changes is the electrons: sodium atoms give electrons to chlorine atoms, and the ions that form hold on to each other tightly.

In this lesson you will see how ions combine into **ionic compounds**, learn to write their formulas and names, and explain why they are hard, brittle and so difficult to melt.

## Ionic bonds and crystal lattices

When a metal reacts with a non-metal, electrons move from the metal atoms to the non-metal atoms:
$$\ce{2Na + Cl2 -> 2NaCl}$$
Each sodium atom loses one electron to become $\ce{Na+}$, and each chlorine atom gains one to become $\ce{Cl-}$.

:::definition Ionic bond
An **ionic bond** is the attraction between oppositely charged ions — cations and anions — that forms after electrons transfer from a metal to a non-metal.
:::

:::definition Crystal lattice
A **crystal lattice** is a regular, repeating, three-dimensional arrangement of ions. In solid sodium chloride, each $\ce{Na+}$ ion is surrounded by six $\ce{Cl-}$ ions, and each $\ce{Cl-}$ ion by six $\ce{Na+}$ ions.
:::

An ionic compound is not made of molecules. Every ion is attracted to all the oppositely charged ions around it, so a grain of salt is one huge lattice of ions. The formula $\ce{NaCl}$ gives the **ratio** of the ions, one sodium ion for every chloride ion, not a molecule.

## Writing formulas

A compound has no overall charge, so the positive charges of its cations must cancel the negative charges of its anions. Use the **fewest ions whose charges add up to zero**:

1. Write the cation and the anion with their charges.
2. Find the lowest common multiple (LCM) of the sizes of the two charges.
3. Divide the LCM by each charge to get the number of each ion.
4. Write the cation first, with the numbers as subscripts. A 1 is not written.

:::example Aluminium oxide
Write the formula of aluminium oxide.
@step The ions are $\ce{Al^3+}$ and $\ce{O^2-}$.
@step The LCM of 3 and 2 is 6.
@step Aluminium: $6 \div 3 = 2$ ions, a total charge of 6+. Oxide: $6 \div 2 = 3$ ions, a total charge of 6−.
@answer $\ce{Al2O3}$
:::

In the same way, magnesium nitride ($\ce{Mg^2+}$ and $\ce{N^3-}$, LCM 6) is $\ce{Mg3N2}$, and potassium sulfide ($\ce{K+}$ and $\ce{S^2-}$, LCM 2) is $\ce{K2S}$.

:::mistake Use the fewest ions
Calcium oxide is $\ce{CaO}$, not $\ce{Ca2O2}$: one $\ce{Ca^2+}$ already cancels one $\ce{O^2-}$. If you use the shortcut of swapping the sizes of the charges into subscripts, always reduce the numbers to the lowest ratio.
:::

## Polyatomic ions

Some ions are made of several atoms bonded together. The whole group carries the charge and stays together, so it is treated as a single ion.

:::definition Polyatomic ion
A **polyatomic ion** is a group of bonded atoms that has an overall charge and acts as one ion. Sulfate, $\ce{SO4^2-}$, is one sulfur atom and four oxygen atoms with a combined charge of 2−.
:::

| Name | Formula | Charge |
|---|---|---|
| ammonium | $\ce{NH4+}$ | 1+ |
| acetate | $\ce{CH3COO-}$ | 1− |
| cyanide | $\ce{CN-}$ | 1− |
| hydroxide | $\ce{OH-}$ | 1− |
| hydrogen carbonate | $\ce{HCO3-}$ | 1− |
| perchlorate | $\ce{ClO4-}$ | 1− |
| chlorate | $\ce{ClO3-}$ | 1− |
| chlorite | $\ce{ClO2-}$ | 1− |
| hypochlorite | $\ce{ClO-}$ | 1− |
| nitrate | $\ce{NO3-}$ | 1− |
| nitrite | $\ce{NO2-}$ | 1− |
| permanganate | $\ce{MnO4-}$ | 1− |
| dihydrogen phosphate | $\ce{H2PO4-}$ | 1− |
| hydrogen sulfate | $\ce{HSO4-}$ | 1− |
| hydrogen sulfite | $\ce{HSO3-}$ | 1− |
| thiocyanate | $\ce{SCN-}$ | 1− |
| carbonate | $\ce{CO3^2-}$ | 2− |
| chromate | $\ce{CrO4^2-}$ | 2− |
| dichromate | $\ce{Cr2O7^2-}$ | 2− |
| hydrogen phosphate | $\ce{HPO4^2-}$ | 2− |
| oxalate | $\ce{C2O4^2-}$ | 2− |
| sulfate | $\ce{SO4^2-}$ | 2− |
| sulfite | $\ce{SO3^2-}$ | 2− |
| thiosulfate | $\ce{S2O3^2-}$ | 2− |
| phosphate | $\ce{PO4^3-}$ | 3− |

:::tip Patterns in the names
- An **-ate** ion has one more oxygen atom than the matching **-ite** ion: nitrate $\ce{NO3-}$ and nitrite $\ce{NO2-}$; sulfate $\ce{SO4^2-}$ and sulfite $\ce{SO3^2-}$.
- In the chlorine family, **per…ate** has one more oxygen than -ate, and **hypo…ite** one fewer than -ite: $\ce{ClO4-}$, $\ce{ClO3-}$, $\ce{ClO2-}$, $\ce{ClO-}$.
- Adding **hydrogen** adds one H and makes the charge one less negative: $\ce{CO3^2-}$ → $\ce{HCO3-}$, and $\ce{PO4^3-}$ → $\ce{HPO4^2-}$ → $\ce{H2PO4-}$.
- Some books call hydrogen carbonate *bicarbonate* and acetate *ethanoate*.
:::

Ammonium, $\ce{NH4+}$, is the only positive ion in the table. It takes the place of a metal ion, so ammonium compounds are ionic even though they contain no metal.

When a formula needs more than one of a polyatomic ion, put the ion in brackets and write the number after the bracket: calcium hydroxide, with one $\ce{Ca^2+}$ and two $\ce{OH-}$, is $\ce{Ca(OH)2}$. With only one polyatomic ion, leave the brackets out: $\ce{NaOH}$, $\ce{CaCO3}$.

:::example Ammonium phosphate
Write the formula of ammonium phosphate.
@step The ions are $\ce{NH4+}$ and $\ce{PO4^3-}$. The LCM of 1 and 3 is 3.
@step Three ammonium ions give 3+, which cancels one phosphate ion.
@step There is more than one ammonium ion, so it goes in brackets.
@answer $\ce{(NH4)3PO4}$
:::

:::mistake Keep the ion whole
$\ce{CaOH2}$ would mean one oxygen and two hydrogen atoms, not two hydroxide ions. Never change the subscripts inside a polyatomic ion; show how many of the ion there are with brackets.
:::

## Naming ionic compounds

Name the **cation first, then the anion**:

- A metal cation keeps the name of its element: sodium, calcium, aluminium.
- A monatomic anion ends in **-ide**: chloride, oxide, nitride, sulfide.
- A polyatomic ion keeps its own name: ammonium, nitrate, sulfate.

So $\ce{K2S}$ is potassium sulfide, $\ce{Al2O3}$ is aluminium oxide, $\ce{CaCO3}$ is calcium carbonate and $\ce{NH4NO3}$ is ammonium nitrate.

:::mistake No prefixes in ionic names
The name of an ionic compound does not say how many of each ion there are, because the charges already fix that. $\ce{MgCl2}$ is magnesium chloride, not magnesium dichloride. Prefixes such as di- and tri- belong to molecular compounds, in the next lesson.
:::

## Metals with more than one charge

Many transition metals, and a few others such as tin and lead, can form ions with different charges. Iron, for example, forms both $\ce{Fe^2+}$ and $\ce{Fe^3+}$, so the name must say which one is present.

:::definition Roman numeral
In the name of an ionic compound, a **Roman numeral** in brackets straight after the metal gives the charge of the metal ion: iron(II) is $\ce{Fe^2+}$ and iron(III) is $\ce{Fe^3+}$. The numerals are capital letters, with no space before the bracket: I = 1, II = 2, III = 3 and IV = 4.
:::

| Metal | Its ions |
|---|---|
| iron | iron(II) $\ce{Fe^2+}$, iron(III) $\ce{Fe^3+}$ |
| copper | copper(I) $\ce{Cu+}$, copper(II) $\ce{Cu^2+}$ |
| tin | tin(II) $\ce{Sn^2+}$, tin(IV) $\ce{Sn^4+}$ |
| lead | lead(II) $\ce{Pb^2+}$, lead(IV) $\ce{Pb^4+}$ |
| cobalt | cobalt(II) $\ce{Co^2+}$, cobalt(III) $\ce{Co^3+}$ |
| chromium | chromium(II) $\ce{Cr^2+}$, chromium(III) $\ce{Cr^3+}$ |
| manganese | manganese(II) $\ce{Mn^2+}$, manganese(IV) $\ce{Mn^4+}$ |
| nickel | nickel(II) $\ce{Ni^2+}$, nickel(III) $\ce{Ni^3+}$ |
| gold | gold(I) $\ce{Au+}$, gold(III) $\ce{Au^3+}$ |
| mercury | mercury(I) $\ce{Hg2^2+}$, mercury(II) $\ce{Hg^2+}$ |

Metals with only one common charge, such as those in groups 1 and 2, aluminium, zinc ($\ce{Zn^2+}$), cadmium ($\ce{Cd^2+}$) and silver ($\ce{Ag+}$), never take a Roman numeral: zinc chloride, silver nitrate.

:::note Mercury(I) comes in pairs
The mercury(I) ion is two mercury atoms bonded together, $\ce{Hg2^2+}$, with a charge of 2+ shared between them. So mercury(I) chloride is $\ce{Hg2Cl2}$, not HgCl. Treat $\ce{Hg2^2+}$ as one ion and never split it.
:::

To name a compound from its formula, find the metal's charge from the anions: add up their negative charge, which the metal ions must cancel, and divide it by the number of metal ions. In $\ce{FeCl3}$, three $\ce{Cl-}$ ions give 3−, so the one iron ion is $\ce{Fe^3+}$: iron(III) chloride.

:::example Sharing the charge
Name $\ce{Fe2O3}$.
@step Each oxide ion is $\ce{O^2-}$, so three of them give 3 × 2− = 6−.
@step The two iron ions must give 6+ between them, so each is 3+: $\ce{Fe^3+}$.
@answer iron(III) oxide
:::

:::mistake The numeral is a charge, not a count
In $\ce{Cu2O}$ the 2 counts the copper ions. Two copper ions cancel one $\ce{O^2-}$, so each is 1+: the name is copper(I) oxide, not copper(II) oxide.
:::

Going the other way, the Roman numeral gives you the charge to use in the formula.

:::example From a name to a formula
Write the formula of iron(III) sulfate.
@step Iron(III) is $\ce{Fe^3+}$ and sulfate is $\ce{SO4^2-}$. The LCM of 3 and 2 is 6.
@step Two iron ions give 6+ and three sulfate ions give 6−.
@step There is more than one sulfate ion, so it goes in brackets.
@answer $\ce{Fe2(SO4)3}$
:::

## Properties of ionic compounds

The lattice of ions explains how ionic compounds behave.

- **High melting points.** Every ion is held by strong attractions to all the oppositely charged ions around it. Melting means pulling the ions away from their places, which takes a lot of energy: sodium chloride melts at 801 °C, and magnesium oxide, with its 2+ and 2− ions, at over 2800 °C.
- **Hard but brittle.** The strong attractions make the crystals hard, but a sharp blow shatters them. Shifting one layer of ions brings ions of the same charge face to face; they repel each other, and the crystal splits apart.
- **Conducting electricity.** A current needs charged particles that can move. In a solid the ions are locked in the lattice, so solid ionic compounds do not conduct. When the compound is melted (molten) or dissolved in water, the ions are free to move, and it conducts.

| | Ionic compound | Molecular compound | Metal |
|---|---|---|---|
| Particles | a lattice of positive and negative ions | separate, neutral molecules | metal ions in a sea of moving electrons |
| Melting point | high | low | usually high |
| The solid | hard and brittle | often soft | can be bent or hammered into shape |
| Conducts as a solid? | no | no | yes |
| Conducts when melted or dissolved? | yes | no | yes, when melted |

Acids, which you will meet in the next lesson, are the exception among molecular compounds: they form ions when they dissolve in water, so their solutions conduct.

:::example Which kind of substance?
A white solid melts at 770 °C. It does not conduct electricity as a solid, but its solution in water does. Is it an ionic compound, a molecular compound or a metal?
@step A metal would conduct as a solid, so it is not a metal.
@step A molecular compound would melt at a low temperature, and its solution would not conduct.
@step A high melting point, and conducting only when dissolved, point to ions that are fixed in the solid but free to move in water.
@answer It is an ionic compound.
:::

:::summary
- An ionic bond is the attraction between oppositely charged ions, after electrons transfer from a metal to a non-metal.
- Ionic compounds are crystal lattices of ions, not molecules; the formula gives the ratio of the ions.
- Formula: the fewest ions whose charges add to zero (use the LCM of the charges). Brackets go round a polyatomic ion when there is more than one.
- Name: cation first, then anion. Monatomic anions end in -ide; polyatomic ions keep their names; no prefixes.
- A Roman numeral gives the charge of a metal with more than one charge; find it from the anions' total charge.
- Ionic compounds have high melting points, are hard and brittle, and conduct only when melted or dissolved.
:::
