A car's airbag fills in less than a tenth of a second. In the classic design, a crash sensor sets off a small charge of sodium azide, which decomposes into sodium and nitrogen gas, and the nitrogen inflates the bag. The engineers who design it cannot weigh the nitrogen as the bag fills, but they can calculate the volume of nitrogen that a few grams of solid will produce.

In this lesson you will extend the stoichiometry of lesson 4.4 to gases, measured by volume at STP, at SATP or with the ideal gas law, and to solutions, measured by volume and concentration.

## The method stays the same

In lesson 4.4 you found the mass of one substance from the mass of another in three steps: change the given quantity to moles, use the mole ratio from the balanced equation, and change the moles back to the quantity you want. Gases and solutions follow exactly the same path. Only the first and last steps change, because gases and solutions are measured by volume rather than weighed.

$$\text{given quantity} \rightarrow n(\text{given}) \xrightarrow{\ \times \frac{\text{wanted coefficient}}{\text{given coefficient}}\ } n(\text{wanted}) \rightarrow \text{wanted quantity}$$

The amount in moles is always in the middle. This table collects the ways in and out of moles from earlier lessons:

| Substance measured by | To find moles | From moles back |
|---|---|---|
| mass (lesson 4.3) | $n = \dfrac{m}{M}$ | $m = nM$ |
| volume of a gas at STP or SATP (lesson 2.6) | $n = \dfrac{V}{V_m}$ | $V = nV_m$ |
| volume of a gas at other conditions (lesson 2.7) | $n = \dfrac{PV}{RT}$ | $V = \dfrac{nRT}{P}$ |
| volume and concentration of a solution (lesson 3.4) | $n = cV$ | $V = \dfrac{n}{c}$ |

:::definition Gas and solution stoichiometry
**Gas stoichiometry** uses a balanced equation to relate the volume of a gas to the amount, mass or volume of another substance in the reaction. **Solution stoichiometry** does the same for the volume and concentration of a solution.
:::

Many questions say that one reactant is **in excess**. That means there is more than enough of it, so it never runs out: the other reactant decides how much product forms. Lesson 4.6 looks at what happens when you cannot be sure which one runs out first.

## Gases at STP and SATP

Lesson 2.6 showed that one mole of any gas fills the same volume at the same temperature and pressure: the **molar volume**, $V_m$. At STP ($0^\circ\text{C}$ and $101.325\ \text{kPa}$) it is $22.4\ \text{L/mol}$, and at SATP ($25^\circ\text{C}$ and $100\ \text{kPa}$) it is $24.8\ \text{L/mol}$. To end with a gas volume, multiply the moles of gas by $V_m$.

:::example Hydrogen from magnesium
Magnesium reacts with excess hydrochloric acid: $\ce{Mg + 2HCl -> MgCl2 + H2}$. What volume of $\ce{H2}$ at STP forms from $5.00\ \text{g}$ of $\ce{Mg}$? Give the answer to the nearest hundredth. At STP ($0^\circ\text{C}$ and $101.325\ \text{kPa}$), the molar volume of a gas is $22.4\ \text{L/mol}$. Use the molar mass Mg $24.31\ \text{g/mol}$.
@step $n(\ce{Mg}) = \dfrac{5.00}{24.31} = 0.2057\ \text{mol}$
@step $n(\ce{H2}) = 0.2057 \times \dfrac{1}{1} = 0.2057\ \text{mol}$
@step $V = nV_m = 0.2057 \times 22.4 = 4.61\ \text{L}$
@answer $4.61\ \text{L}$ of hydrogen
:::

:::example Filling an airbag
An airbag inflates when sodium azide decomposes: $\ce{2NaN3 -> 2Na + 3N2}$. What volume of $\ce{N2}$ at SATP forms from $65.0\ \text{g}$ of $\ce{NaN3}$? Give the answer to the nearest tenth. At SATP ($25^\circ\text{C}$ and $100\ \text{kPa}$), the molar volume of a gas is $24.8\ \text{L/mol}$. Use these molar masses: Na $22.99\ \text{g/mol}$, N $14.01\ \text{g/mol}$.
@step $M(\ce{NaN3}) = 22.99 + 3(14.01) = 65.02\ \text{g/mol}$
@step $n(\ce{NaN3}) = \dfrac{65.0}{65.02} = 0.9997\ \text{mol}$
@step $n(\ce{N2}) = 0.9997 \times \dfrac{3}{2} = 1.500\ \text{mol}$
@step $V = 1.500 \times 24.8 = 37.2\ \text{L}$
@answer $37.2\ \text{L}$ of nitrogen
:::

The path also runs backwards. To find the mass of a solid needed to make a volume of gas, divide the volume by $V_m$ first, then use the mole ratio and $m = nM$.

:::example Carbon dioxide from limestone
Limestone, calcium carbonate, breaks down when heated: $\ce{CaCO3 -> CaO + CO2}$. What mass of $\ce{CaCO3}$ is needed to make $10.0\ \text{L}$ of $\ce{CO2}$ at SATP? Give the answer to the nearest tenth. At SATP the molar volume of a gas is $24.8\ \text{L/mol}$. Use these molar masses: Ca $40.08\ \text{g/mol}$, C $12.01\ \text{g/mol}$, O $16.00\ \text{g/mol}$.
@step $n(\ce{CO2}) = \dfrac{V}{V_m} = \dfrac{10.0}{24.8} = 0.4032\ \text{mol}$
@step $n(\ce{CaCO3}) = 0.4032 \times \dfrac{1}{1} = 0.4032\ \text{mol}$
@step $M(\ce{CaCO3}) = 40.08 + 12.01 + 3(16.00) = 100.09\ \text{g/mol}$, so $m = 0.4032 \times 100.09 = 40.36\ \text{g}$
@answer $40.4\ \text{g}$ of calcium carbonate
:::

:::example Oxygen from potassium chlorate
Potassium chlorate breaks down when heated: $\ce{2KClO3 -> 2KCl + 3O2}$. What mass of $\ce{KClO3}$ is needed to make $5.00\ \text{L}$ of $\ce{O2}$ at STP? Give the answer to the nearest tenth. At STP the molar volume of a gas is $22.4\ \text{L/mol}$. Use these molar masses: K $39.10\ \text{g/mol}$, Cl $35.45\ \text{g/mol}$, O $16.00\ \text{g/mol}$.
@step $n(\ce{O2}) = \dfrac{5.00}{22.4} = 0.2232\ \text{mol}$
@step The wanted substance is $\ce{KClO3}$ (coefficient 2) and the given one is $\ce{O2}$ (coefficient 3): $n(\ce{KClO3}) = 0.2232 \times \dfrac{2}{3} = 0.1488\ \text{mol}$
@step $M(\ce{KClO3}) = 39.10 + 35.45 + 3(16.00) = 122.55\ \text{g/mol}$, so $m = 0.1488 \times 122.55 = 18.24\ \text{g}$
@answer $18.2\ \text{g}$ of potassium chlorate
:::

:::mistake The molar volume is for gases only
$V_m$ changes moles of a **gas** to litres. It says nothing about a solid, a liquid or a solution. In $\ce{CaCO3 -> CaO + CO2}$, only the $\ce{CO2}$ has a molar volume; the calcium carbonate is weighed, so it goes through $n = \dfrac{m}{M}$.
:::

:::note Gas to gas
When two gases in a reaction are at the same temperature and pressure, every mole of each fills the same volume, so their volumes are in the same ratio as their coefficients. In $\ce{C3H8 + 5O2 -> 3CO2 + 4H2O}$, $2.00\ \text{L}$ of propane needs $2.00 \times \dfrac{5}{1} = 10.0\ \text{L}$ of oxygen at the same conditions. This is Avogadro's law at work.
:::

## Gases at other conditions

Real reactions rarely happen at exactly STP or SATP. At any other temperature and pressure, use the ideal gas law from lesson 2.7, $PV = nRT$, for the gas step, with $R = 8.314\ \text{kPa·L/(mol·K)}$ and the temperature in kelvins. The stoichiometry before it does not change.

:::example Hydrogen on a cool day
Zinc reacts with excess hydrochloric acid: $\ce{Zn + 2HCl -> ZnCl2 + H2}$. What volume of $\ce{H2}$ at $22^\circ\text{C}$ and $98\ \text{kPa}$ forms from $3.50\ \text{g}$ of $\ce{Zn}$? Give the answer to the nearest hundredth. Use $PV = nRT$ with $R = 8.314\ \text{kPa·L/(mol·K)}$, and $T(\text{K}) = T(^\circ\text{C}) + 273.15$. Use the molar mass Zn $65.38\ \text{g/mol}$.
@step $n(\ce{Zn}) = \dfrac{3.50}{65.38} = 0.05353\ \text{mol}$, and $n(\ce{H2}) = 0.05353 \times \dfrac{1}{1} = 0.05353\ \text{mol}$
@step $T = 22 + 273.15 = 295.15\ \text{K}$
@step $V = \dfrac{nRT}{P} = \dfrac{0.05353 \times 8.314 \times 295.15}{98} = 1.340\ \text{L}$
@answer $1.34\ \text{L}$ of hydrogen
:::

:::example Burning propane
Propane burns completely in a barbecue: $\ce{C3H8 + 5O2 -> 3CO2 + 4H2O}$. What volume of $\ce{O2}$ at $18^\circ\text{C}$ and $101\ \text{kPa}$ is needed to burn $10.0\ \text{g}$ of $\ce{C3H8}$? Give the answer to the nearest tenth. Use $PV = nRT$ with $R = 8.314\ \text{kPa·L/(mol·K)}$, and $T(\text{K}) = T(^\circ\text{C}) + 273.15$. Use these molar masses: C $12.01\ \text{g/mol}$, H $1.01\ \text{g/mol}$.
@step $M(\ce{C3H8}) = 3(12.01) + 8(1.01) = 44.11\ \text{g/mol}$, so $n(\ce{C3H8}) = \dfrac{10.0}{44.11} = 0.2267\ \text{mol}$
@step $n(\ce{O2}) = 0.2267 \times \dfrac{5}{1} = 1.134\ \text{mol}$
@step $T = 18 + 273.15 = 291.15\ \text{K}$, so $V = \dfrac{nRT}{P} = \dfrac{1.134 \times 8.314 \times 291.15}{101} = 27.18\ \text{L}$
@answer $27.2\ \text{L}$ of oxygen
:::

:::mistake Celsius in the ideal gas law
$PV = nRT$ needs the temperature in kelvins. Using $22$ instead of $295.15$ in the zinc example gives $0.0999\ \text{L}$, more than ten times too small. Add 273.15 before you substitute.
:::

## Solutions

A reaction in solution is measured with a pipette or a burette, not a balance. Lesson 3.4 gave the link between a solution's volume and the amount of solute in it: $n = cV$, with the volume in **litres**. Divide millilitres by 1000 first.

:::example A silver chloride precipitate
$25.0\ \text{mL}$ of $0.200\ \text{mol/L}$ silver nitrate reacts with excess sodium chloride: $\ce{AgNO3(aq) + NaCl(aq) -> AgCl(s) + NaNO3(aq)}$. What mass of silver chloride precipitates, to the nearest thousandth? Use these molar masses: Ag $107.87\ \text{g/mol}$, Cl $35.45\ \text{g/mol}$.
@step $n(\ce{AgNO3}) = cV = 0.200\ \text{mol/L} \times 0.0250\ \text{L} = 0.005000\ \text{mol}$
@step $n(\ce{AgCl}) = 0.005000 \times \dfrac{1}{1} = 0.005000\ \text{mol}$
@step $M(\ce{AgCl}) = 107.87 + 35.45 = 143.32\ \text{g/mol}$, so $m = 0.005000 \times 143.32 = 0.7166\ \text{g}$
@answer $0.717\ \text{g}$ of silver chloride
:::

:::example A calcium phosphate precipitate
$36.0\ \text{mL}$ of $0.250\ \text{mol/L}$ calcium chloride reacts with excess sodium phosphate: $\ce{3CaCl2(aq) + 2Na3PO4(aq) -> Ca3(PO4)2(s) + 6NaCl(aq)}$. What mass of calcium phosphate precipitates, to the nearest thousandth? Use these molar masses: Ca $40.08\ \text{g/mol}$, P $30.97\ \text{g/mol}$, O $16.00\ \text{g/mol}$.
@step $n(\ce{CaCl2}) = 0.250 \times 0.0360 = 0.009000\ \text{mol}$
@step $n(\ce{Ca3(PO4)2}) = 0.009000 \times \dfrac{1}{3} = 0.003000\ \text{mol}$
@step $M(\ce{Ca3(PO4)2}) = 3(40.08) + 2(30.97) + 8(16.00) = 310.18\ \text{g/mol}$
@step $m = 0.003000 \times 310.18 = 0.9305\ \text{g}$
@answer $0.931\ \text{g}$ of calcium phosphate
:::

To find the volume of a solution that reacts exactly with something, work out the moles of the solute you need, then $V = \dfrac{n}{c}$. The answer comes out in litres; multiply by 1000 for millilitres.

:::example Dissolving magnesium
In the reaction $\ce{Mg + 2HCl -> MgCl2 + H2}$, what volume of $0.250\ \text{mol/L}$ hydrochloric acid reacts exactly with $0.150\ \text{g}$ of magnesium? Give the answer in millilitres, to the nearest tenth. Use the molar mass Mg $24.31\ \text{g/mol}$.
@step $n(\ce{Mg}) = \dfrac{0.150}{24.31} = 0.006170\ \text{mol}$
@step $n(\ce{HCl}) = 0.006170 \times \dfrac{2}{1} = 0.01234\ \text{mol}$
@step $V = \dfrac{n}{c} = \dfrac{0.01234}{0.250} = 0.04936\ \text{L}$
@answer $49.4\ \text{mL}$ of hydrochloric acid
:::

:::example An antacid tablet
An antacid contains magnesium hydroxide, which neutralises stomach acid: $\ce{Mg(OH)2 + 2HCl -> MgCl2 + 2H2O}$. What volume of $0.100\ \text{mol/L}$ hydrochloric acid reacts exactly with $0.250\ \text{g}$ of magnesium hydroxide? Give the answer in millilitres, to the nearest tenth. Use these molar masses: Mg $24.31\ \text{g/mol}$, O $16.00\ \text{g/mol}$, H $1.01\ \text{g/mol}$.
@step $M(\ce{Mg(OH)2}) = 24.31 + 2(16.00) + 2(1.01) = 58.33\ \text{g/mol}$, so $n = \dfrac{0.250}{58.33} = 0.004286\ \text{mol}$
@step $n(\ce{HCl}) = 0.004286 \times \dfrac{2}{1} = 0.008572\ \text{mol}$
@step $V = \dfrac{0.008572}{0.100} = 0.08572\ \text{L}$
@answer $85.7\ \text{mL}$ of hydrochloric acid
:::

When both substances are solutions, the first step uses $n = cV$ and the last uses $V = \dfrac{n}{c}$. This is the calculation behind titration, in lesson 4.8.

:::example Two solutions
In the reaction $\ce{H2SO4 + 2NaOH -> Na2SO4 + 2H2O}$, what volume of $0.250\ \text{mol/L}$ sodium hydroxide reacts exactly with $20.0\ \text{mL}$ of $0.150\ \text{mol/L}$ sulfuric acid? Give the answer in millilitres, to the nearest tenth.
@step $n(\ce{H2SO4}) = 0.150 \times 0.0200 = 0.003000\ \text{mol}$
@step $n(\ce{NaOH}) = 0.003000 \times \dfrac{2}{1} = 0.006000\ \text{mol}$
@step $V = \dfrac{0.006000}{0.250} = 0.02400\ \text{L}$
@answer $24.0\ \text{mL}$ of sodium hydroxide
:::

:::mistake This is not a dilution
The dilution formula $c_1V_1 = c_2V_2$ from lesson 3.5 describes one solute in two volumes of solution. It leaves out the mole ratio, so it fails for a reaction between two different substances. In the last example it gives $\dfrac{0.150 \times 20.0}{0.250} = 12.0\ \text{mL}$, half the right answer, because each $\ce{H2SO4}$ needs two $\ce{NaOH}$.
:::

:::tip Check the size of the answer
More moles of solute need more solution, and a more concentrated solution needs less volume. In the last example the base needs twice the moles of the acid, at a concentration less than twice as high, so its volume should be a little larger than $20.0\ \text{mL}$. It is.
:::

:::summary
- Every stoichiometry problem has the same core: change the given quantity to moles, multiply by $\dfrac{\text{wanted coefficient}}{\text{given coefficient}}$, and change the moles to the quantity you want.
- A gas at STP or SATP: $n = \dfrac{V}{V_m}$ and $V = nV_m$, with $V_m = 22.4\ \text{L/mol}$ at STP and $24.8\ \text{L/mol}$ at SATP.
- A gas at other conditions: $n = \dfrac{PV}{RT}$ and $V = \dfrac{nRT}{P}$, with $R = 8.314\ \text{kPa·L/(mol·K)}$ and $T$ in kelvins.
- A solution: $n = cV$ and $V = \dfrac{n}{c}$, with $V$ in litres. Multiply litres by 1000 to give millilitres.
- A reactant **in excess** never runs out, so the other reactant decides how much forms.
- For gases at the same temperature and pressure, volumes are in the same ratio as the coefficients.
:::
