Blow into a balloon and it grows. Each breath adds more gas particles, and more particles need more room. In lesson 2.5 the amount of gas never changed; now it does.

In this lesson you will see why equal volumes of gases hold equal numbers of particles, recap the **mole** as the chemist's way of counting particles, and use the **molar volume** to move between the amount, the volume and the mass of a gas.

## Avogadro's law

In 1811 the Italian scientist Amedeo Avogadro suggested that **equal volumes of gases, at the same temperature and pressure, contain equal numbers of particles**, whichever gas they are. A litre of hydrogen and a litre of carbon dioxide, side by side in the same room, hold the same number of molecules, even though each carbon dioxide molecule is about 22 times heavier.

The kinetic molecular theory from lesson 2.1 explains why. Gas particles are tiny compared with the spaces between them, so their size hardly affects the volume. At the same temperature the particles of every gas have the same average kinetic energy, so on average each one pushes on the walls just as hard: heavier particles move more slowly, but hit harder. The room a gas needs depends only on **how many** particles there are.

The amount of gas, $n$, is counted in **moles** (mol); the next section recaps what a mole is. Double the amount of gas and, at the same temperature and pressure, the volume doubles too.

:::definition Avogadro's law
At constant temperature and pressure, the volume of a gas is directly proportional to the amount of gas: $\dfrac{V_1}{n_1} = \dfrac{V_2}{n_2}$. The volumes must be in the same unit.
:::

:::example Tripling the amount
A flexible container holds $0.80\ \text{mol}$ of gas in $19.4\ \text{L}$. At the same temperature and pressure, what volume would $2.40\ \text{mol}$ of the gas occupy, to the nearest tenth?
@step Avogadro's law: $\dfrac{V_1}{n_1} = \dfrac{V_2}{n_2}$, so $V_2 = V_1 \times \dfrac{n_2}{n_1}$.
@step $V_2 = 19.4 \times \dfrac{2.40}{0.80} = 58.2\ \text{L}$
@answer $58.2\ \text{L}$. The amount was tripled, so the volume tripled too.
:::

The ratio $\dfrac{n_2}{n_1}$ need not be a whole number. Check that the answer moves the right way: less gas must take up less room.

:::example A smaller amount
A flexible container holds $1.25\ \text{mol}$ of gas in $30.5\ \text{L}$. At the same temperature and pressure, what volume would $0.60\ \text{mol}$ of the gas occupy, to the nearest tenth?
@step $V_2 = V_1 \times \dfrac{n_2}{n_1} = 30.5 \times \dfrac{0.60}{1.25} = 14.64\ \text{L}$
@step Check: $0.60\ \text{mol}$ is less than half of $1.25\ \text{mol}$, and $14.6\ \text{L}$ is less than half of $30.5\ \text{L}$.
@answer $14.6\ \text{L}$
:::

:::check c20-avogadro 2
:::

Sometimes you need the amount of gas to **add**. Find the total amount the new volume needs, then subtract what is already there.

:::example How much gas to add
A flexible container holds $0.40\ \text{mol}$ of gas in $9.8\ \text{L}$. How many moles of gas must be added to make its volume $16.5\ \text{L}$ at the same temperature and pressure? Give the answer to the nearest thousandth.
@step The total amount needed: $n_2 = n_1 \times \dfrac{V_2}{V_1} = 0.40 \times \dfrac{16.5}{9.8} = 0.673\ \text{mol}$
@step Subtract what is already there: $0.673 - 0.40 = 0.273\ \text{mol}$
@answer $0.273\ \text{mol}$ must be added.
:::

:::mistake The total is not the amount added
In the last example, $0.673\ \text{mol}$ is the amount the container holds **after** the gas is added. The question asks how much is added, so subtract the $0.40\ \text{mol}$ that was there to begin with.
:::

## The mole: counting particles

Particles are far too small and far too numerous to count one by one. Chemists count them in a very large bundle instead, much as eggs are counted in dozens. You will use moles much more later in the course; here they count the particles in a gas.

:::definition Mole
The **mole** (symbol mol) is the unit of **amount of substance**. One mole of anything contains about $6.02 \times 10^{23}$ particles, a number called **Avogadro's number**. A mole of helium is about $6.02 \times 10^{23}$ helium atoms; a mole of oxygen gas is about $6.02 \times 10^{23}$ $\ce{O2}$ molecules.
:::

You cannot count out $6.02 \times 10^{23}$ molecules, but you can weigh them.

:::definition Molar mass
The **molar mass**, $M$, of a substance is the mass of one mole of it, in grams per mole ($\text{g/mol}$). Add up the molar masses of the atoms in its formula, using the periodic table.
:::

The amount in moles is then the mass divided by the molar mass: $n = \dfrac{m}{M}$, with $m$ in grams.

:::example From mass to moles
How many moles are in $16.0\ \text{g}$ of oxygen gas, $\ce{O2}$? Use the molar mass O $16.00\ \text{g/mol}$.
@step Oxygen gas is made of $\ce{O2}$ molecules: $M = 2 \times 16.00 = 32.00\ \text{g/mol}$.
@step $n = \dfrac{m}{M} = \dfrac{16.0\ \text{g}}{32.00\ \text{g/mol}} = 0.500\ \text{mol}$
@answer $0.500\ \text{mol}$ of $\ce{O2}$
:::

:::mistake Diatomic gases
Hydrogen, nitrogen, oxygen and chlorine are made of two-atom molecules: $\ce{H2}$, $\ce{N2}$, $\ce{O2}$ and $\ce{Cl2}$. The molar mass of chlorine gas is $2 \times 35.45 = 70.90\ \text{g/mol}$, not $35.45\ \text{g/mol}$. The noble gases, such as helium, $\ce{He}$, and argon, $\ce{Ar}$, are single atoms.
:::

## Molar volume

Equal volumes hold equal numbers of particles, so one mole of **any** gas takes up the same volume at a given temperature and pressure.

:::definition Molar volume
The **molar volume**, $V_m$, of a gas is the volume of one mole of it at a stated temperature and pressure, in litres per mole ($\text{L/mol}$). At the same temperature and pressure it is the same for every gas.
:::

Chemists often compare gases at one of two standard sets of conditions.

:::definition STP and SATP
**STP** (standard temperature and pressure) is $0^\circ\text{C}$ and $101.325\ \text{kPa}$. At STP the molar volume of a gas is $22.4\ \text{L/mol}$. **SATP** (standard ambient temperature and pressure) is $25^\circ\text{C}$ and $100\ \text{kPa}$. At SATP the molar volume is $24.8\ \text{L/mol}$.
:::

| Conditions | Temperature | Pressure | Molar volume |
|---|---|---|---|
| STP | $0^\circ\text{C}$ | $101.325\ \text{kPa}$ | $22.4\ \text{L/mol}$ |
| SATP | $25^\circ\text{C}$ | $100\ \text{kPa}$ | $24.8\ \text{L/mol}$ |

The molar volume at SATP is larger because the gas is warmer and under slightly less pressure, and both make a gas expand. The combined gas law from lesson 2.5 confirms it: $22.4 \times \dfrac{298.15}{273.15} \times \dfrac{101.325}{100} = 24.8\ \text{L}$.

:::note Check the conditions
Some books define STP slightly differently. Always use the conditions and the molar volume that a question gives you.
:::

Because the volume is the same for every gas, the masses are not. At STP, one mole of each of these gases fills $22.4\ \text{L}$:

| Gas | Molar mass | Volume of one mole at STP |
|---|---|---|
| hydrogen, $\ce{H2}$ | $2.02\ \text{g/mol}$ | $22.4\ \text{L}$ |
| nitrogen, $\ce{N2}$ | $28.02\ \text{g/mol}$ | $22.4\ \text{L}$ |
| oxygen, $\ce{O2}$ | $32.00\ \text{g/mol}$ | $22.4\ \text{L}$ |
| carbon dioxide, $\ce{CO2}$ | $44.01\ \text{g/mol}$ | $22.4\ \text{L}$ |

To change between amount and volume, multiply or divide by the molar volume: $V = n \times V_m$ and $n = \dfrac{V}{V_m}$.

:::example From moles to volume
What volume does $1.75\ \text{mol}$ of nitrogen, $\ce{N2}$, occupy at STP, to the nearest tenth? At STP ($0^\circ\text{C}$ and $101.325\ \text{kPa}$), the molar volume of a gas is $22.4\ \text{L/mol}$.
@step $V = n \times V_m = 1.75\ \text{mol} \times 22.4\ \text{L/mol}$
@answer $39.2\ \text{L}$
:::

:::check c20-molar-volume 1
:::

:::example From volume to moles
How many moles of carbon dioxide, $\ce{CO2}$, are in $50.0\ \text{L}$ of the gas at SATP? Give the answer to the nearest hundredth. At SATP ($25^\circ\text{C}$ and $100\ \text{kPa}$), the molar volume of a gas is $24.8\ \text{L/mol}$.
@step $n = \dfrac{V}{V_m} = \dfrac{50.0\ \text{L}}{24.8\ \text{L/mol}} = 2.016\ \text{mol}$
@answer $2.02\ \text{mol}$
:::

:::tip Let the units decide
Multiply when you want litres: $\text{mol} \times \text{L/mol} = \text{L}$. Divide when you want moles: $\text{L} \div \text{L/mol} = \text{mol}$.
:::

:::mistake The wrong molar volume
$22.4\ \text{L/mol}$ belongs to STP only. Using it at SATP in the last example would give $50.0 \div 22.4 = 2.23\ \text{mol}$ instead of $2.02\ \text{mol}$. Read the conditions before you choose.
:::

## From mass to volume

Moles connect mass and volume. Divide a mass by the molar mass to get moles, then multiply by the molar volume to get litres:

$$m \xrightarrow{\ \div M\ } n \xrightarrow{\ \times V_m\ } V$$

:::example Mass to volume
What volume does $25.0\ \text{g}$ of carbon dioxide, $\ce{CO2}$, occupy at STP, to the nearest tenth? At STP the molar volume of a gas is $22.4\ \text{L/mol}$. Use these molar masses: C $12.01\ \text{g/mol}$, O $16.00\ \text{g/mol}$.
@step $M = 12.01 + 2 \times 16.00 = 44.01\ \text{g/mol}$
@step $n = \dfrac{25.0}{44.01} = 0.5681\ \text{mol}$
@step $V = 0.5681 \times 22.4 = 12.7\ \text{L}$
@answer $12.7\ \text{L}$
:::

The same path works backwards: divide a volume by the molar volume to get moles, then multiply by the molar mass to get grams.

:::example Volume to mass
What is the mass of $5.00\ \text{L}$ of ammonia, $\ce{NH3}$, at SATP, to the nearest hundredth? At SATP the molar volume of a gas is $24.8\ \text{L/mol}$. Use these molar masses: N $14.01\ \text{g/mol}$, H $1.01\ \text{g/mol}$.
@step $n = \dfrac{5.00}{24.8} = 0.2016\ \text{mol}$
@step $M = 14.01 + 3 \times 1.01 = 17.04\ \text{g/mol}$
@step $m = n \times M = 0.2016 \times 17.04 = 3.44\ \text{g}$
@answer $3.44\ \text{g}$
:::

:::tip Round only at the end
Keep the unrounded amount in your calculator between steps, and round only the final answer. Writing $n$ to four significant figures, as above, is enough to show your working.
:::

:::

:::summary
- **Avogadro's law:** at the same temperature and pressure, equal volumes of gases hold equal numbers of particles, so $\dfrac{V_1}{n_1} = \dfrac{V_2}{n_2}$.
- To find the amount of gas to add, find the new total amount, then subtract the amount already there.
- A **mole** is about $6.02 \times 10^{23}$ particles. The **molar mass**, $M$, is the mass of one mole, and $n = \dfrac{m}{M}$.
- The **molar volume**, $V_m$, is the volume of one mole of any gas: $22.4\ \text{L/mol}$ at **STP** ($0^\circ\text{C}$, $101.325\ \text{kPa}$) and $24.8\ \text{L/mol}$ at **SATP** ($25^\circ\text{C}$, $100\ \text{kPa}$).
- $V = n \times V_m$ and $n = \dfrac{V}{V_m}$.
- Mass to volume: divide by $M$, then multiply by $V_m$. Volume to mass: divide by $V_m$, then multiply by $M$.
:::
