In a molecule of hydrogen, $\ce{H2}$, two identical atoms share a pair of electrons perfectly evenly. In hydrogen chloride, $\ce{HCl}$, the sharing is lopsided: the chlorine atom pulls the shared pair closer to itself than the hydrogen atom does. How hard each atom pulls is its **electronegativity**, and comparing the electronegativities of two atoms tells you whether they share their electrons evenly, share them unevenly, or whether one atom takes the electrons altogether.

## What electronegativity measures

:::definition Electronegativity
**Electronegativity** is how strongly an atom in a bond attracts the shared electrons. It is a number with no units. Most books use the **Pauling scale**, on which fluorine has the highest value, 4.0.
:::

Picture a bond as a tug-of-war over the shared electrons, with electronegativity as each atom's strength. This course uses these Pauling values, rounded to one decimal place:

| Period | Group 1 | Group 2 | Group 13 | Group 14 | Group 15 | Group 16 | Group 17 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | H 2.2 | | | | | | |
| 2 | Li 1.0 | Be 1.6 | B 2.0 | C 2.6 | N 3.0 | O 3.4 | F 4.0 |
| 3 | Na 0.9 | Mg 1.3 | Al 1.6 | Si 1.9 | P 2.2 | S 2.6 | Cl 3.2 |
| 4 | K 0.8 | Ca 1.0 | | | | | Br 3.0 |
| 5 | | | | | | | I 2.7 |
| 6 | Cs 0.8 | Ba 0.9 | | | | | |

Other tables may round a few values differently (carbon is sometimes listed as 2.5), so always use the values a question gives you.

## Trends in the periodic table

- **Across a period**, from left to right, electronegativity **increases**. Each step adds a proton to the nucleus while the valence electrons stay in the same energy level, so the nucleus pulls harder on shared electrons.
- **Down a group**, electronegativity **decreases**. Each step adds an energy level, so the valence electrons sit farther from the nucleus and are shielded by more inner electrons.

So the most electronegative elements are non-metals at the top right — fluorine, oxygen, nitrogen and chlorine — and the least electronegative are metals at the bottom left, such as caesium.

:::note What about the noble gases?
Noble gases are usually not given electronegativity values. They already have full valence shells and rarely form bonds, so there is little to measure.
:::

:::example Using the trends
Without the table, decide which atom is more electronegative: sulfur or chlorine? Oxygen or sulfur?
@step Sulfur and chlorine are in the same period, and chlorine is further right, so chlorine is more electronegative.
@step Oxygen and sulfur are in the same group, and oxygen is higher up, so oxygen is more electronegative.
@answer Chlorine (3.2 against 2.6) and oxygen (3.4 against 2.6). The table agrees.
:::

## The electronegativity difference

How unevenly two atoms share their electrons depends on the gap between their electronegativities, the **electronegativity difference**:

$$\Delta\text{EN} = \text{larger value} - \text{smaller value}$$

Always subtract the smaller value from the larger, so $\Delta\text{EN}$ is never negative. Two identical atoms give $\Delta\text{EN} = 0$.

:::example Finding the difference
Find $\Delta\text{EN}$ for the H–Cl bond and for the Na–Cl bond. (H 2.2, Na 0.9, Cl 3.2)
@step H–Cl: chlorine has the larger value, so $\Delta\text{EN} = 3.2 - 2.2 = 1.0$.
@step Na–Cl: chlorine again has the larger value, so $\Delta\text{EN} = 3.2 - 0.9 = 2.3$.
@answer $\Delta\text{EN} = 1.0$ for H–Cl and $2.3$ for Na–Cl. In both bonds chlorine pulls the electrons towards itself, but much harder against sodium.
:::

:::mistake Subtract, don't add
$\Delta\text{EN}$ is a difference. For H–Cl it is $3.2 - 2.2 = 1.0$, not $3.2 + 2.2 = 5.4$, and not $2.2 - 3.2 = -1.0$.
:::

## Three kinds of bond

The size of $\Delta\text{EN}$ decides what happens to the shared electrons.

:::definition Non-polar covalent bond
A **non-polar covalent bond** is a covalent bond in which the electrons are shared equally, or almost equally, because the two atoms have the same or nearly the same electronegativity.
:::

:::definition Polar covalent bond
A **polar covalent bond** is a covalent bond in which the electrons are shared unequally: they spend more of their time near the more electronegative atom.
:::

When $\Delta\text{EN}$ is large enough, the sharing stops: the more electronegative atom takes the electrons, and the bond is **ionic**, the attraction between oppositely charged ions. Gradileo's questions use this rule:

| $\Delta\text{EN}$ | Bond type | The electrons are… |
|---|---|---|
| below 0.5 | non-polar covalent | shared equally, or almost |
| 0.5 up to (but not including) 1.7 | polar covalent | shared unequally |
| 1.7 or more | ionic | transferred, so ions form |

:::example Classifying H–Cl and Na–Cl
Use the differences found above: 1.0 for H–Cl and 2.3 for Na–Cl.
@step H–Cl: 1.0 is from 0.5 up to 1.7, so the bond is polar covalent.
@step Na–Cl: 2.3 is 1.7 or more, so the bond is ionic. Sodium gives its electron to chlorine, making $\ce{Na+}$ and $\ce{Cl-}$ ions.
@answer H–Cl is polar covalent; Na–Cl is ionic.
:::

:::example Two non-polar bonds
Classify the C–H bond (C 2.6, H 2.2) and the Cl–Cl bond in $\ce{Cl2}$ (Cl 3.2).
@step C–H: $\Delta\text{EN} = 2.6 - 2.2 = 0.4$, which is below 0.5.
@step Cl–Cl: $\Delta\text{EN} = 3.2 - 3.2 = 0$, which is below 0.5.
@answer Both are non-polar covalent. Two identical atoms always share equally, as in $\ce{H2}$, $\ce{N2}$ and $\ce{O2}$.
:::

## Partial charges

:::definition Partial charge
In a polar covalent bond, the more electronegative atom carries a small negative **partial charge**, written $\delta-$ ("delta minus"), and the other atom carries a small positive partial charge, $\delta+$. They are smaller than the full charges of ions, because the electrons are still shared, not handed over.
:::

For hydrogen chloride we write $\overset{\delta+}{\text{H}}-\overset{\delta-}{\text{Cl}}$: chlorine wins the tug-of-war, so it is the slightly negative end.

:::example Partial charges on an O–H bond
Each water molecule has two O–H bonds. Classify the bond and mark its partial charges. (O 3.4, H 2.2)
@step $\Delta\text{EN} = 3.4 - 2.2 = 1.2$
@step 1.2 is from 0.5 up to 1.7, so the bond is polar covalent.
@step Oxygen is more electronegative, so it gets $\delta-$; hydrogen gets $\delta+$.
@answer Polar covalent: $\overset{\delta-}{\text{O}}-\overset{\delta+}{\text{H}}$.
:::

:::mistake Where the δ− goes
The $\delta-$ always goes on the atom with the **larger** electronegativity, wherever it is written in the formula. And a polar bond has no full charges: hydrogen chloride gas is made of polar molecules, not of $\ce{H+}$ and $\ce{Cl-}$ ions.
:::

## A continuum, not three boxes

:::note Why every question states its rule
Bonds change gradually as $\Delta\text{EN}$ grows: a bond with a difference of 1.6 is not very different from one with 1.8. The cut-offs 0.5 and 1.7 are convenient lines drawn across a smooth scale, and books draw them in slightly different places (some use 0.4, and some put the ionic line at 2.0). That is why every Gradileo question tells you the rule it uses. Always apply the rule given.
:::

The rule can also surprise you. Aluminium is a metal, yet the Al–I bond has $\Delta\text{EN} = 2.7 - 1.6 = 1.1$, which makes it polar covalent, and aluminium iodide does behave more like a molecular substance than an ionic one. The electronegativity difference is a better guide than "metal plus non-metal".

:::summary
- Electronegativity is how strongly an atom in a bond attracts the shared electrons; fluorine is highest at 4.0.
- It increases across a period and decreases down a group. Noble gases are usually not given values.
- $\Delta\text{EN}$ = larger value − smaller value; it is never negative.
- Below 0.5: non-polar covalent. 0.5 up to 1.7: polar covalent. 1.7 or more: ionic.
- In a polar bond the more electronegative atom is $\delta-$ and the other is $\delta+$.
- Bonding is a continuum, and books draw the lines differently, so use the rule each question gives.
:::
