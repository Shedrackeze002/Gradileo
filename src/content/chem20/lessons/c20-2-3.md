Hold a finger over the tip of a syringe and push the plunger in. The trapped air is squeezed into a smaller space, and the further you push, the harder it pushes back. Let go, and the plunger springs back out as the air expands again.

In this lesson you will see exactly how the volume of a gas depends on the pressure on it, explain the link with the kinetic molecular theory, and use **Boyle's law** to find a new volume or a new pressure.

## Pressure and volume

Imagine a sample of air trapped in a gas syringe sealed at the tip. No gas can get in or out, and the temperature is kept the same throughout. The pressure on the gas is raised step by step, and its volume is measured each time:

| Pressure (kPa) | Volume (L) | Pressure × volume (kPa·L) |
|---|---|---|
| 50 | 4.00 | 200 |
| 80 | 2.50 | 200 |
| 100 | 2.00 | 200 |
| 200 | 1.00 | 200 |
| 250 | 0.80 | 200 |
| 400 | 0.50 | 200 |

When the pressure doubles, from $100\ \text{kPa}$ to $200\ \text{kPa}$, the volume halves, from $2.00\ \text{L}$ to $1.00\ \text{L}$. When the pressure is multiplied by 4, from $100\ \text{kPa}$ to $400\ \text{kPa}$, the volume is divided by 4. In every row the pressure times the volume comes to the same number, $200\ \text{kPa·L}$.

On a graph of volume against pressure, the points lie on a curve that falls steeply at first and then levels off:

From A to B the pressure doubles and the volume halves; from B to C the same thing happens again. Each doubling of the pressure squeezes the gas by a smaller amount in litres, which is why the curve flattens out. It comes closer and closer to the pressure axis but never reaches it.

:::definition Inversely proportional
Two quantities are **inversely proportional** when their product stays the same. If one is multiplied by a factor, the other is divided by the same factor: double one and the other halves; triple one and the other falls to a third.
:::

:::definition Boyle's law
For a fixed amount of gas at constant temperature, the volume is inversely proportional to the pressure: $P_1V_1 = P_2V_2$. Here $P_1$ and $V_1$ are the pressure and volume before a change, and $P_2$ and $V_2$ are the pressure and volume after it.
:::

The law is named after Robert Boyle, who published his measurements on air trapped in a glass tube in 1662.

## Why squeezing a gas raises its pressure

The kinetic molecular theory explains Boyle's law. The pressure of a gas comes from its particles hitting the walls of the container. At constant temperature the particles keep the same average speed, so each collision with a wall is just as hard as before. What changes is **how often** they hit.

Halve the volume, and the same particles are packed into half the space. Each part of the wall now has twice as many particles close to it, so it is hit twice as often every second. Twice as many collisions on each square centimetre of wall means twice the pressure. Let the gas expand instead, and the particles spread out: they hit the walls less often, and the pressure falls.

:::mistake Squeezing a gas does not speed up its particles
At constant temperature the average speed of the particles stays the same, because it depends only on the temperature. A smaller volume raises the pressure because the particles hit the walls **more often**, not harder.
:::

:::note Pumps get warm
Pump up a tyre quickly and the pump gets warm: squeezing a gas fast heats it. Boyle's law describes the gas once it is back at its starting temperature, so the measurements in the table were taken slowly, letting the syringe cool between readings.
:::

:::note Breathing
When you breathe in, muscles enlarge your chest and the volume of your lungs increases. The pressure of the air inside falls a little below the pressure outside, and air flows in. When you breathe out, your chest gets smaller, the pressure inside rises, and air is pushed out.
:::

## Using Boyle's law

Follow these steps for any Boyle's law problem:

1. **Write down** the pressure and volume before the change, $P_1$ and $V_1$, and the one quantity you know after it.
2. **Check the units.** Both pressures must be in the same unit, and both volumes in the same unit. In these lessons pressures are in kilopascals and volumes in litres.
3. **Rearrange** $P_1V_1 = P_2V_2$ for the unknown: $V_2 = \dfrac{P_1V_1}{P_2}$, or $P_2 = \dfrac{P_1V_1}{V_2}$.
4. **Substitute and calculate**, then round as the question asks.
5. **Check the direction.** If the pressure went up, the volume must have gone down, and the other way round.

:::example Doubling the pressure
A gas in a cylinder with a movable piston has a volume of $6.0\ \text{L}$ at $120\ \text{kPa}$. The pressure changes to $240\ \text{kPa}$ while the temperature stays the same. What is the new volume, to the nearest hundredth?
@step The pressure has doubled, so the volume should halve, to about $3\ \text{L}$.
@step $V_2 = \dfrac{P_1V_1}{P_2} = \dfrac{120 \times 6.0}{240} = 3.00\ \text{L}$
@answer $V_2 = 3.00\ \text{L}$. The pressure went up, so the volume went down.
:::

:::

When the pressure changes by an awkward factor, the formula does the work.

:::example Any change in pressure
A gas in a cylinder with a movable piston has a volume of $4.5\ \text{L}$ at $150\ \text{kPa}$. The pressure changes to $380\ \text{kPa}$ while the temperature stays the same. What is the new volume, to the nearest hundredth?
@step $V_2 = \dfrac{P_1V_1}{P_2} = \dfrac{150 \times 4.5}{380} = 1.776\ldots$
@step Round to the nearest hundredth: $1.78\ \text{L}$.
@answer $V_2 = 1.78\ \text{L}$. The pressure went up, so the volume went down.
:::

:::mistake Putting the pressures the wrong way up
A gas has a volume of $2.5\ \text{L}$ at $250\ \text{kPa}$, and the pressure falls to $90\ \text{kPa}$. Working out $\dfrac{90 \times 2.5}{250} = 0.90\ \text{L}$ puts the pressures the wrong way up: the pressure went **down**, so the volume must go **up**. The correct answer is $V_2 = \dfrac{250 \times 2.5}{90} = 6.94\ \text{L}$, to the nearest hundredth.
:::

:::

## Finding a new pressure

To find the pressure that will squeeze a gas to a given volume, solve Boyle's law for $P_2$ instead.

:::example A new pressure
A gas in a cylinder with a movable piston is at $210\ \text{kPa}$ with a volume of $8.4\ \text{L}$. At the same temperature, what pressure would make its volume $5.2\ \text{L}$? Give the pressure to the nearest whole number.
@step The volume gets smaller, so the pressure must rise above $210\ \text{kPa}$.
@step $P_2 = \dfrac{P_1V_1}{V_2} = \dfrac{210 \times 8.4}{5.2} = 339.23\ldots$
@answer $P_2 = 339\ \text{kPa}$
:::

:::tip Round once, at the end
Keep every digit on your calculator until the last step, then round as the question asks. The questions here ask for about three significant figures: a volume such as $1.78\ \text{L}$ to the nearest hundredth, $16.8\ \text{L}$ to the nearest tenth, and a pressure such as $339\ \text{kPa}$ to the nearest whole number.
:::

:::note Other units
Boyle's law works in any units, as long as both pressures share a unit and both volumes share a unit. If one pressure is in atmospheres and the other in kilopascals, convert first, using $1\ \text{atm} = 101.325\ \text{kPa}$.
:::

:::check c20-boyle 3
:::

Boyle's law holds only while the temperature and the amount of gas stay the same. If the gas is warmed or cooled as it is squeezed, or some of it leaks out, $P_1V_1 = P_2V_2$ no longer applies. The combined gas law, in lesson 2.5, deals with a change in temperature as well.

:::summary
- For a fixed amount of gas at constant temperature, pressure and volume are **inversely proportional**: when one is multiplied by a factor, the other is divided by it.
- **Boyle's law**: $P_1V_1 = P_2V_2$.
- A graph of volume against pressure is a curve that falls steeply and then levels off.
- Kinetic molecular theory: in a smaller volume the particles hit each part of the wall more often, so the pressure is higher. Their average speed does not change.
- Solve for $V_2 = \dfrac{P_1V_1}{P_2}$ or $P_2 = \dfrac{P_1V_1}{V_2}$, and check that a higher pressure gives a smaller volume.
- Use the same units on both sides, and round only at the end.
:::
