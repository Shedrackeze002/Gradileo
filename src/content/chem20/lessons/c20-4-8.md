How much acid is in a bottle of vinegar? A food-testing lab cannot see the acid, or weigh it apart from the water around it. Instead, a chemist adds a base of known concentration, drop by drop, until the acid is exactly used up, and the volume of base it took gives away how much acid there was.

In this lesson you will see how a **titration** is done, use titration data to find the concentration of a solution, read the equivalence point from a titration curve, choose an indicator, and recognise the curves of strong and weak acids and bases.

## How a titration is done

A titration is solution stoichiometry, from lesson 4.5, carried out in the laboratory with very precise glassware.

:::definition Titration
A **titration** is the gradual addition of one solution from a burette to a measured volume of another, until the reaction between them is complete. The solution added from the burette is the **titrant**. It is usually a **standard solution**, one whose concentration is known precisely (lesson 3.4).
:::

:::definition Burette
A **burette** is a long glass tube marked in millilitres, with a tap at the bottom. Its scale reads downward, from $0.00\ \text{mL}$ at the top, so the volume added is the **final reading minus the initial reading**.
:::

The steps of a titration:

1. **Pipette** an exact volume of the sample, such as $25.00\ \text{mL}$, into a clean conical flask. A volumetric pipette delivers one precise volume.
2. **Add an indicator**, a few drops, to the flask.
3. **Fill the burette** with the titrant and record the initial reading.
4. **Titrate**: add the titrant while swirling the flask, quickly at first and then drop by drop, until one drop makes the indicator change colour for good. Record the final reading.
5. **Repeat**. The first trial is a quick, rough trial to find roughly where the colour changes; it usually overshoots. Repeat carefully until three trials agree within $0.20\ \text{mL}$.

Read the burette at eye level, at the bottom of the meniscus, to the nearest $0.05\ \text{mL}$. A reading is written with two decimal places, such as $18.40\ \text{mL}$ or $18.45\ \text{mL}$.

:::definition Equivalence point and endpoint
The **equivalence point** is the moment when the titrant added has reacted exactly with the sample, in the mole ratio of the balanced equation. The **endpoint** is the moment the indicator changes colour. A well-chosen indicator changes colour at, or within a drop of, the equivalence point, so the endpoint marks the equivalence point.
:::

:::note Rinse with the right liquid
Rinse the burette with a little of the titrant and the pipette with a little of the sample, so that no water dilutes them. The conical flask can be wet with distilled water: extra water changes the volume in the flask but not the **amount** of sample, and the amount is what reacts.
:::

## From titration data to a concentration

The calculation is solution stoichiometry: $n = cV$ for the titrant, the mole ratio to the sample, and $c = \dfrac{n}{V}$ for the sample, with every volume in litres.

$$c(\text{titrant}),\ V(\text{titrant}) \xrightarrow{\ n = cV\ } n(\text{titrant}) \xrightarrow{\ \text{mole ratio}\ } n(\text{sample}) \xrightarrow{\ c = n/V\ } c(\text{sample})$$

:::example Hydrochloric acid
A $25.00\ \text{mL}$ sample of hydrochloric acid, $\ce{HCl(aq)}$, is titrated with $0.150\ \text{mol/L}$ sodium hydroxide, $\ce{NaOH(aq)}$: $\ce{HCl(aq) + NaOH(aq) -> NaCl(aq) + H2O(l)}$. The endpoint is reached after $21.40\ \text{mL}$ of sodium hydroxide. What is the concentration of the hydrochloric acid, to the nearest thousandth?
@step $n(\ce{NaOH}) = 0.150 \times 0.02140 = 0.003210\ \text{mol}$
@step $n(\ce{HCl}) = 0.003210 \times \dfrac{1}{1} = 0.003210\ \text{mol}$
@step $c = \dfrac{n}{V} = \dfrac{0.003210}{0.02500} = 0.1284\ \text{mol/L}$
@answer $0.128\ \text{mol/L}$
:::

:::example Acid in vinegar
A $10.00\ \text{mL}$ sample of vinegar, acetic acid $\ce{CH3COOH(aq)}$, is titrated with $0.400\ \text{mol/L}$ sodium hydroxide: $\ce{CH3COOH(aq) + NaOH(aq) -> NaCH3COO(aq) + H2O(l)}$. The endpoint is reached after $20.85\ \text{mL}$ of sodium hydroxide. What is the concentration of the acetic acid, to the nearest thousandth?
@step $n(\ce{NaOH}) = 0.400 \times 0.02085 = 0.008340\ \text{mol}$
@step $n(\ce{CH3COOH}) = 0.008340 \times \dfrac{1}{1} = 0.008340\ \text{mol}$
@step $c = \dfrac{0.008340}{0.01000} = 0.834\ \text{mol/L}$
@answer $0.834\ \text{mol/L}$
:::

When the coefficients are not equal, the mole ratio matters. Sulfuric acid gives up two hydrogen ions, so each $\ce{H2SO4}$ needs two $\ce{NaOH}$.

:::example Sulfuric acid
A $20.00\ \text{mL}$ sample of sulfuric acid, $\ce{H2SO4(aq)}$, is titrated with $0.150\ \text{mol/L}$ sodium hydroxide: $\ce{H2SO4(aq) + 2NaOH(aq) -> Na2SO4(aq) + 2H2O(l)}$. The endpoint is reached after $24.80\ \text{mL}$. What is the concentration of the sulfuric acid, to four decimal places?
@step $n(\ce{NaOH}) = 0.150 \times 0.02480 = 0.003720\ \text{mol}$
@step $n(\ce{H2SO4}) = 0.003720 \times \dfrac{1}{2} = 0.001860\ \text{mol}$
@step $c = \dfrac{0.001860}{0.02000} = 0.0930\ \text{mol/L}$
@answer $0.0930\ \text{mol/L}$
:::

:::mistake Leaving out the mole ratio
Skipping the ratio in the last example gives $\dfrac{0.003720}{0.02000} = 0.186\ \text{mol/L}$, twice the right answer. The shortcut $c_1V_1 = c_2V_2$ works only when the coefficients of the two reactants are equal; the full method always works.
:::

## Several trials

One titration could hide a mistake, such as an extra drop or a misread burette, so chemists repeat it. The first trial is the rough one. Average only the trials that agree within $0.20\ \text{mL}$, and leave out the rough trial and any other trial that does not agree.

:::example Sodium carbonate
A $25.00\ \text{mL}$ sample of sodium carbonate, $\ce{Na2CO3(aq)}$, is titrated with $0.200\ \text{mol/L}$ hydrochloric acid: $\ce{Na2CO3(aq) + 2HCl(aq) -> 2NaCl(aq) + H2O(l) + CO2(g)}$. The table shows the burette readings. What is the concentration of the sodium carbonate, to four decimal places?

| Trial | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Final reading (mL) | 24.15 | 23.95 | 23.45 | 24.80 |
| Initial reading (mL) | 0.50 | 1.20 | 0.65 | 2.10 |
@step Volumes used (final − initial): $23.65$, $22.75$, $22.80$ and $22.70\ \text{mL}$.
@step Trial 1 is the rough trial. Trials 2 to 4 agree within $0.20\ \text{mL}$, so the average is $\dfrac{22.75 + 22.80 + 22.70}{3} = 22.75\ \text{mL}$.
@step $n(\ce{HCl}) = 0.200 \times 0.02275 = 0.004550\ \text{mol}$
@step $n(\ce{Na2CO3}) = 0.004550 \times \dfrac{1}{2} = 0.002275\ \text{mol}$
@step $c = \dfrac{0.002275}{0.02500} = 0.0910\ \text{mol/L}$
@answer $0.0910\ \text{mol/L}$
:::

:::mistake Averaging every trial
Including the rough trial gives an average of $22.98\ \text{mL}$ and a concentration of $0.0919\ \text{mol/L}$, too high. The rough trial overshot the endpoint, so it is left out. Also check which reading is which: the volume used is always the final reading minus the initial reading.
:::

The curve starts near pH 1, the pH of $0.100\ \text{mol/L}$ of a strong acid. For a long time the pH rises only slowly, because each drop of base meets plenty of acid. Close to the equivalence point almost no acid is left, and the pH shoots up: from about pH 4 at $24.95\ \text{mL}$ to about pH 10 at $25.05\ \text{mL}$, in two drops. After that the base is in excess and the curve levels off near pH 12.5.

:::definition Reading the equivalence point
The equivalence point is in the **middle of the steep, nearly vertical part** of a titration curve. Read straight down from it to find the volume of titrant, and across to find the pH.
:::

For a strong acid and a strong base, the equivalence point is at pH 7: the solution then holds only water and a neutral salt, such as $\ce{NaCl}$.

:::example Reading a curve
In the graph above, what volume of sodium hydroxide has been added at the equivalence point, and what is the concentration of the hydrochloric acid?
@step The steep part is centred on $25.0\ \text{mL}$, at pH 7.
@step $n(\ce{NaOH}) = 0.100 \times 0.0250 = 0.00250\ \text{mol}$, and $n(\ce{HCl}) = 0.00250 \times \dfrac{1}{1} = 0.00250\ \text{mol}$
@step $c = \dfrac{0.00250}{0.02500} = 0.100\ \text{mol/L}$
@answer $25.0\ \text{mL}$; the acid is $0.100\ \text{mol/L}$, as expected.
:::

- **It starts higher**, near pH 2.9 instead of 1, because a weak acid ionises only partly (lesson 3.8) and makes fewer hydronium ions.

## Choosing an indicator

An indicator changes colour over a range of about one or two pH units. For the endpoint to match the equivalence point, choose the indicator whose colour-change range **includes the pH at the equivalence point**.

| Indicator | Colour change | pH range |
|---|---|---|
| methyl orange | red to yellow | 3.2 to 4.4 |
| methyl red | red to yellow | 4.8 to 6.0 |
| bromothymol blue | yellow to blue | 6.0 to 7.6 |
| phenolphthalein | colourless to pink | 8.2 to 10.0 |

:::example An indicator for vinegar
Which indicator should be used to titrate acetic acid with sodium hydroxide? Use the ranges in the table.
@step The equivalence point of a weak acid titrated with a strong base is at about pH 8.7.
@step Phenolphthalein changes colour from pH 8.2 to 10.0, which includes 8.7.
@answer Phenolphthalein. Bromothymol blue would change colour before the equivalence point, while some acid was still left.
:::

:::tip Strong with strong
For a strong acid and a strong base the jump is so steep, from about pH 4 to pH 10 in two drops, that several indicators change colour within a drop of the equivalence point. The best choice is still the one whose range includes pH 7: bromothymol blue. For a weak base titrated with a strong acid, with the equivalence point near pH 5.3, choose methyl red.
:::

:::check c20-titration-curve 2
:::

To recognise a curve, read two things from it: where it starts and where its equivalence point is.

:::example Which titration?
A titration curve starts near pH 11 and falls as the titrant is added. The middle of its steep part is at about pH 5.3. Which titration does it show?
@step It starts above 7, so a base is in the flask and an acid is being added.
@step It starts well below pH 13 and its equivalence point is below 7, so the base is weak.
@answer A weak base titrated with a strong acid, such as ammonia with hydrochloric acid.
:::

:::summary
- In a **titration**, a standard solution (the **titrant**) is added from a **burette** to a pipetted sample with an indicator, until the indicator changes colour at the **endpoint**. Burettes are read to $\pm 0.05\ \text{mL}$.
- The **equivalence point** is where the titrant has reacted exactly with the sample; a good indicator's endpoint matches it.
- Volume used = final reading − initial reading. Leave out the rough first trial, and average the trials that agree within $0.20\ \text{mL}$.
- Calculation: $n = cV$ for the titrant, the mole ratio, then $c = \dfrac{n}{V}$ for the sample, with volumes in litres.
- On a **titration curve** of pH against volume, the equivalence point is the middle of the steep part.
- Strong acid with strong base: starts near pH 1, equivalence at pH 7. Weak acid with strong base: starts near pH 2.9, buffer region, equivalence near pH 8.7. Base curves are the mirror images: equivalence at pH 7 for a strong base and near pH 5.3 for a weak base.
- Choose the indicator whose colour-change range includes the pH at the equivalence point.
:::
