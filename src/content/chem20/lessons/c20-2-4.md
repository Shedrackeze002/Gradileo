Carry a party balloon from a warm room out into freezing air and it shrinks; bring it back inside and it swells again. Leave a sealed aerosol can in hot sunshine and the pressure inside it rises. Changing the temperature of a gas changes its volume, its pressure, or both.

In this lesson you will see how the volume of a gas at constant pressure, and the pressure of a gas at constant volume, depend on its temperature, and why that temperature must always be in kelvins.

## Heating a gas at constant pressure

Imagine a gas trapped under a piston that slides freely, so the pressure on the gas stays the same. As the gas is warmed it pushes the piston out and its volume grows; as it is cooled its volume shrinks. A graph of volume against temperature in degrees Celsius is a straight line.

The graph shows three samples of gas of different sizes, measured between $-50^\circ\text{C}$ and $100^\circ\text{C}$ (the solid lines). Each line has been extended backwards to lower temperatures (the dashed lines). All three reach zero volume at the same temperature, about $-273^\circ\text{C}$.

No real gas ever shrinks to nothing: every gas turns into a liquid or a solid long before it gets that cold. The dashed lines only extend the pattern beyond the measurements. But every gas points to the same temperature, and it turns out to be the lowest temperature there can be.

:::definition Absolute zero
**Absolute zero** is the lowest possible temperature: $0\ \text{K}$, or $-273.15^\circ\text{C}$. At absolute zero the particles of a substance would have the least kinetic energy they can have.
:::

The Kelvin scale from lesson 2.2 starts at absolute zero, and a change of one kelvin is the same as a change of one degree Celsius, so $T(\text{K}) = T(^\circ\text{C}) + 273.15$. Measured in kelvins, each line on the graph would pass through the origin: double the Kelvin temperature and the volume doubles too.

:::definition Directly proportional
Two quantities are **directly proportional** when their ratio stays the same. If one is multiplied by a factor, the other is multiplied by the same factor.
:::

:::definition Charles's law
For a fixed amount of gas at constant pressure, the volume is directly proportional to the Kelvin temperature: $\dfrac{V_1}{T_1} = \dfrac{V_2}{T_2}$.
:::

The law is named after the French scientist and balloonist Jacques Charles. The kinetic molecular theory explains it. When a gas is heated, its particles move faster on average, so they hit the walls more often and harder. In a balloon, or under a free piston, the walls can move, so the gas expands. As it spreads out, its particles reach each part of the wall less often, until the pressure inside matches the pressure outside again. A hotter gas at the same pressure therefore takes up more room.

## Using Charles's law

Solve Charles's law for the unknown: $V_2 = V_1 \times \dfrac{T_2}{T_1}$, or $T_2 = T_1 \times \dfrac{V_2}{V_1}$. Both temperatures must be in kelvins.

:::example Kelvin temperatures
A balloon holds $2.40\ \text{L}$ of gas at $300\ \text{K}$. At the same pressure, what is its volume at $360\ \text{K}$, to the nearest hundredth?
@step The Kelvin temperature is multiplied by $\dfrac{360}{300} = 1.2$, so the volume is multiplied by $1.2$ too.
@step $V_2 = V_1 \times \dfrac{T_2}{T_1} = 2.40 \times \dfrac{360}{300} = 2.88\ \text{L}$
@answer $V_2 = 2.88\ \text{L}$
:::

:::

Most temperatures are measured in degrees Celsius, so change them to kelvins first.

:::example Celsius temperatures
A gas has a volume of $3.50\ \text{L}$ at $20^\circ\text{C}$. The pressure stays the same. What is its volume at $85^\circ\text{C}$, to the nearest hundredth? Use $T(\text{K}) = T(^\circ\text{C}) + 273.15$.
@step $T_1 = 20 + 273.15 = 293.15\ \text{K}$ and $T_2 = 85 + 273.15 = 358.15\ \text{K}$
@step $V_2 = 3.50 \times \dfrac{358.15}{293.15} = 4.276\ldots$
@answer $V_2 = 4.28\ \text{L}$
:::

:::mistake Using degrees Celsius
With Celsius temperatures, $3.50 \times \dfrac{85}{20} = 14.9\ \text{L}$: more than four times the volume, when the Kelvin temperature rose by only about a fifth. Celsius temperatures do not start at absolute zero, so the volume is not proportional to them. A gas at $0^\circ\text{C}$ would even mean dividing by zero. Always change to kelvins first.
:::

:::check c20-charles 2
:::

:::example Finding a temperature
A gas has a volume of $1.80\ \text{L}$ at $20^\circ\text{C}$. At the same pressure, at what temperature in degrees Celsius will its volume be $2.25\ \text{L}$? Round to the nearest degree. Use $T(\text{K}) = T(^\circ\text{C}) + 273.15$.
@step $T_1 = 20 + 273.15 = 293.15\ \text{K}$
@step $T_2 = T_1 \times \dfrac{V_2}{V_1} = 293.15 \times \dfrac{2.25}{1.80} = 366.44\ \text{K}$
@step Change back to degrees Celsius: $366.44 - 273.15 = 93.29^\circ\text{C}$
@answer $T_2 = 93^\circ\text{C}$, to the nearest degree.
:::

## Heating a gas at constant volume

A gas in a rigid container, such as a steel gas cylinder or an aerosol can, cannot expand. When it is heated, its particles move faster and hit the walls more often and harder. The walls cannot move outwards, so the pressure rises. Cooling the gas does the opposite. Measured in kelvins, the pressure of the gas is directly proportional to its temperature, and graphs of pressure against Celsius temperature point back to absolute zero too.

:::definition Gay-Lussac's law
For a fixed amount of gas at constant volume, the pressure is directly proportional to the Kelvin temperature: $\dfrac{P_1}{T_1} = \dfrac{P_2}{T_2}$.
:::

The law is named after the French chemist Joseph Louis Gay-Lussac. Some books call it Amontons's law, or simply the pressure law. To use it, solve for $P_2 = P_1 \times \dfrac{T_2}{T_1}$ or $T_2 = T_1 \times \dfrac{P_2}{P_1}$, with temperatures in kelvins.

:::example A heated aerosol can
The gas in a sealed aerosol can is at $250\ \text{kPa}$ and $22^\circ\text{C}$. What is its pressure at $65^\circ\text{C}$, to the nearest whole number? Use $T(\text{K}) = T(^\circ\text{C}) + 273.15$.
@step $T_1 = 22 + 273.15 = 295.15\ \text{K}$ and $T_2 = 65 + 273.15 = 338.15\ \text{K}$
@step $P_2 = P_1 \times \dfrac{T_2}{T_1} = 250 \times \dfrac{338.15}{295.15} = 286.42\ldots$
@answer $P_2 = 286\ \text{kPa}$
:::

:::note Never heat a sealed container
A sealed container gives its gas no room to expand, so heating it raises the pressure inside. In a fire the temperature can climb by hundreds of degrees, and the pressure keeps rising with the Kelvin temperature until the container bursts. That is why aerosol cans carry warnings not to heat them, pierce them or throw them on a fire, even when they seem empty, and why gas cylinders are stored away from heat. A pressure cooker has a valve that lets gas escape before the pressure gets too high.
:::

:::example Finding a temperature
The gas in a sealed steel gas cylinder is at $180\ \text{kPa}$ and $15^\circ\text{C}$. At what temperature in degrees Celsius will its pressure be $240\ \text{kPa}$? Round to the nearest degree. Use $T(\text{K}) = T(^\circ\text{C}) + 273.15$.
@step $T_1 = 15 + 273.15 = 288.15\ \text{K}$
@step $T_2 = T_1 \times \dfrac{P_2}{P_1} = 288.15 \times \dfrac{240}{180} = 384.20\ \text{K}$
@step Change back to degrees Celsius: $384.20 - 273.15 = 111.05^\circ\text{C}$
@answer $T_2 = 111^\circ\text{C}$, to the nearest degree.
:::

:::tip Check that the answer makes sense
A higher pressure in the same container needs a higher Kelvin temperature, and a larger volume at the same pressure needs a higher Kelvin temperature. If your answer goes the other way, a fraction is upside down.
:::

:::summary
- **Absolute zero**, $0\ \text{K}$ or $-273.15^\circ\text{C}$, is the lowest possible temperature. Graphs of gas volume against Celsius temperature all extrapolate to it.
- Use $T(\text{K}) = T(^\circ\text{C}) + 273.15$. The gas laws work only with Kelvin temperatures.
- **Directly proportional** quantities change by the same factor.
- **Charles's law**: at constant pressure, $\dfrac{V_1}{T_1} = \dfrac{V_2}{T_2}$. A hotter gas takes up more room.
- **Gay-Lussac's law**: at constant volume, $\dfrac{P_1}{T_1} = \dfrac{P_2}{T_2}$. A hotter gas in a rigid container has a higher pressure.
- Kinetic molecular theory: hotter particles move faster and hit the walls more often and harder.
- To find a temperature in degrees Celsius, work in kelvins, then subtract 273.15 at the end.
- Never heat a sealed container: its pressure rises until it may burst.
:::
