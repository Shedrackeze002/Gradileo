A bottle of fruit-juice concentrate says "mix one part with four parts water". The water makes the drink weaker, but it does not change how much fruit juice is in the glass. Chemists work the same way: acids and other reagents are kept as concentrated **stock solutions**, and a small portion is diluted with water whenever a weaker solution is needed.

In this lesson you will use the dilution equation, $c_1V_1 = c_2V_2$, to find a new concentration, the volume of stock solution to use and the volume of water to add, and see how a dilution is made in the lab.

## What stays the same

:::definition Dilution
**Dilution** is adding more solvent to a solution to lower its concentration. A **stock solution** is a concentrated solution that is kept in store and diluted as needed.
:::

Adding water makes the volume larger, but nothing is added to or taken from the solute. The **amount of solute** in moles is the same before and after the dilution; it is simply spread through more solution. From lesson 3.4, the amount is the concentration times the volume, $n = cV$. So $c_1V_1$ before the dilution equals $c_2V_2$ after it.

:::definition The dilution equation
When a solution is diluted, $c_1V_1 = c_2V_2$. Here $c_1$ and $V_1$ are the concentration and volume of the concentrated solution you start with, and $c_2$ and $V_2$ are the concentration and volume of the diluted solution. The volumes can be in millilitres or in litres, as long as both use the same unit.
:::

The volumes appear on both sides of the equation, so their unit cancels. That is why you can leave them in millilitres instead of changing them to litres. Here is what happens when $25.0\ \text{mL}$ of a $2.00\ \text{mol/L}$ solution is diluted to $100.0\ \text{mL}$:

| | Before dilution | After dilution |
|---|---|---|
| Volume | $25.0\ \text{mL}$ | $100.0\ \text{mL}$ |
| Amount of solute | $2.00 \times 0.0250 = 0.0500\ \text{mol}$ | $0.0500\ \text{mol}$ |
| Concentration | $2.00\ \text{mol/L}$ | $\dfrac{0.0500\ \text{mol}}{0.1000\ \text{L}} = 0.500\ \text{mol/L}$ |

The volume became four times larger, so the concentration became four times smaller.

## Finding the new concentration

To find the concentration after a dilution, divide both sides of the equation by $V_2$: $c_2 = \dfrac{c_1V_1}{V_2}$.

:::example A new concentration
$40\ \text{mL}$ of a $1.85\ \text{mol/L}$ solution is diluted with water to $200\ \text{mL}$. What is the new concentration, to the nearest thousandth?
@step $c_1V_1 = c_2V_2$, so $c_2 = \dfrac{c_1V_1}{V_2}$.
@step $c_2 = \dfrac{1.85 \times 40}{200} = 0.370\ \text{mol/L}$
@answer $0.370\ \text{mol/L}$. The volume was multiplied by 5, so the concentration was divided by 5.
:::

:::mistake Mixing millilitres and litres
Both volumes must be in the same unit. In the last example, writing $V_1 = 40\ \text{mL}$ but $V_2 = 0.200\ \text{L}$ gives $\dfrac{1.85 \times 40}{0.200} = 370\ \text{mol/L}$, a thousand times too large.
:::

:::tip Check the direction
Diluting always lowers the concentration and raises the volume. If your $c_2$ is larger than $c_1$, or your $V_1$ is larger than $V_2$, something is upside down.
:::

## Finding the volume of stock solution

When you plan a dilution, you usually know the concentration and volume you want, $c_2$ and $V_2$, and the concentration of the stock solution, $c_1$. The unknown is how much stock solution to measure out: $V_1 = \dfrac{c_2V_2}{c_1}$.

:::example How much stock solution
What volume of a $6.00\ \text{mol/L}$ stock solution is needed to make $500\ \text{mL}$ of a $0.750\ \text{mol/L}$ solution, to the nearest tenth?
@step $c_1V_1 = c_2V_2$, so $V_1 = \dfrac{c_2V_2}{c_1}$.
@step $V_1 = \dfrac{0.750 \times 500}{6.00} = 62.5\ \text{mL}$
@step Check: the concentration falls to $\dfrac{0.750}{6.00} = \dfrac{1}{8}$ of the stock's, so the volume must be 8 times larger, and $8 \times 62.5 = 500$.
@answer $62.5\ \text{mL}$ of the stock solution.
:::

## The volume of water added

$V_2$ is the volume of the **final solution**, not the volume of water. The final solution is the stock solution plus the water, so the water added is the difference: $V_2 - V_1$.

:::example How much water
A student makes $450\ \text{mL}$ of a $0.300\ \text{mol/L}$ solution from a $1.75\ \text{mol/L}$ stock solution. What volume of water is added to the stock solution, to the nearest whole number?
@step Find the volume of stock solution first: $V_1 = \dfrac{c_2V_2}{c_1} = \dfrac{0.300 \times 450}{1.75} = 77.1\ \text{mL}$
@step The water makes up the rest of the final volume: $450 - 77.1 = 372.9\ \text{mL}$
@answer $373\ \text{mL}$ of water.
:::

:::mistake The final volume is not the water
In the last example the student does not add $450\ \text{mL}$ of water. $450\ \text{mL}$ is the volume of the whole solution, and $77.1\ \text{mL}$ of it is the stock solution. Only the remaining $373\ \text{mL}$ is water.
:::

## Dilution factor

Chemists often describe a dilution by how many times the volume grows.

:::definition Dilution factor
The **dilution factor** is the final volume divided by the starting volume, $\dfrac{V_2}{V_1}$. The concentration falls by the same factor: $\dfrac{V_2}{V_1} = \dfrac{c_1}{c_2}$.
:::

:::example Using a dilution factor
$10.0\ \text{mL}$ of a $2.00\ \text{mol/L}$ solution is diluted to $250.0\ \text{mL}$. What is the dilution factor, and what is the new concentration?
@step Dilution factor: $\dfrac{250.0}{10.0} = 25$
@step The concentration falls by the same factor: $c_2 = \dfrac{2.00}{25} = 0.0800\ \text{mol/L}$
@answer A dilution factor of 25, giving $0.0800\ \text{mol/L}$. The dilution equation agrees: $\dfrac{2.00 \times 10.0}{250.0} = 0.0800$.
:::

A "tenfold dilution" has a dilution factor of 10: $10.0\ \text{mL}$ made up to $100.0\ \text{mL}$, or $25.0\ \text{mL}$ made up to $250.0\ \text{mL}$. Either way, the concentration becomes one tenth of what it was.

## Preparing a dilution in the lab

A dilution is only as accurate as the two volumes, so both are measured with precise glassware. A **pipette** measures and delivers an exact volume of liquid, and the **volumetric flask** from lesson 3.4 holds an exact volume when it is filled to the mark on its neck.

To make a volume $V_2$ of a solution with concentration $c_2$ from a stock solution:

1. **Calculate** the volume of stock solution, $V_1$, with $c_1V_1 = c_2V_2$.
2. **Half-fill** a volumetric flask of volume $V_2$ with distilled water.
3. **Pipette** the volume $V_1$ of stock solution into the flask. Rinse the pipette first with a little of the stock solution, so that any water left inside it does not dilute what you measure.
4. **Fill to the mark**: swirl to mix, then add distilled water until the bottom of the meniscus sits on the mark. Use a dropper for the last few drops.
5. **Mix**: stopper the flask and turn it upside down several times, so that the concentration is the same throughout.

In the lab you do not measure the water separately: filling to the mark makes the final volume exact. The volume of water, $V_2 - V_1$, still tells you about how much will go in.

:::note Safety: add acid to water
Diluting a concentrated acid, especially sulfuric acid, releases a lot of heat. Always add the acid slowly to water, never water to acid. If water is poured into a concentrated acid, the first small amount of water can boil at once and spatter acid out of the container. That is why the flask is half-filled with water before the stock solution goes in. Wear eye protection whenever you dilute an acid or a base.
:::

:::example Planning a dilution
Describe how to make $250.0\ \text{mL}$ of a $0.0800\ \text{mol/L}$ copper(II) sulfate solution from a $0.800\ \text{mol/L}$ stock solution.
@step $V_1 = \dfrac{c_2V_2}{c_1} = \dfrac{0.0800 \times 250.0}{0.800} = 25.0\ \text{mL}$. This is a tenfold dilution.
@step Half-fill a $250\ \text{mL}$ volumetric flask with distilled water. Rinse a $25\ \text{mL}$ pipette with the stock solution, then pipette $25.0\ \text{mL}$ of stock solution into the flask.
@step Swirl, add distilled water up to the mark, then stopper the flask and invert it several times.
@answer Pipette $25.0\ \text{mL}$ of the stock solution into a $250\ \text{mL}$ volumetric flask, fill it to the mark with distilled water and mix.
:::

:::summary
- Dilution adds solvent: the volume rises, the concentration falls, and the amount of solute stays the same.
- When a solution is diluted, $c_1V_1 = c_2V_2$. Both volumes must be in the same unit, which can be millilitres.
- New concentration: $c_2 = \dfrac{c_1V_1}{V_2}$. Volume of stock solution: $V_1 = \dfrac{c_2V_2}{c_1}$.
- $V_2$ is the volume of the final solution. The water added is $V_2 - V_1$.
- The dilution factor is $\dfrac{V_2}{V_1} = \dfrac{c_1}{c_2}$.
- In the lab: pipette the stock solution into a volumetric flask, fill to the mark with distilled water, then stopper and mix.
- Always add acid to water, never water to acid.
:::
