Everything around you — the air, the water you drink, your own body — is built from atoms. There are more than a hundred kinds of atom, called **elements**, and the periodic table arranges them in an order that turns out to be remarkably useful.

In this lesson you will look inside an atom and count the particles it is made of. Then you will use the periodic table to predict how an atom gains or loses electrons to become an **ion** — the first step towards understanding how atoms bond together.

## Inside an atom

An atom has a tiny, dense **nucleus** at its centre, surrounded by electrons. Three kinds of particle make up every atom:

| Particle | Charge | Relative mass | Where it is |
|---|---|---|---|
| proton | $+1$ | 1 | in the nucleus |
| neutron | $0$ | 1 | in the nucleus |
| electron | $-1$ | about $\tfrac{1}{1836}$ | around the nucleus, in energy levels |

Protons and neutrons carry almost all of an atom's mass. Electrons are so light that we leave them out when we count mass.

:::definition Atomic number
The **atomic number** is the number of protons in the nucleus. It identifies the element: every carbon atom has 6 protons, and any atom with 6 protons is carbon. The periodic table gives each element's atomic number.
:::

A neutral atom has no overall charge, so its electrons balance its protons: **electrons = protons = atomic number**.

:::definition Mass number
The **mass number** is the number of protons plus the number of neutrons in the nucleus. So
$$\text{neutrons} = \text{mass number} - \text{atomic number}$$
:::

:::definition Isotope
**Isotopes** are atoms of the same element — the same number of protons — with different numbers of neutrons, and so different mass numbers. An isotope is written as the element's name and its mass number, such as **chlorine-35**, or as its symbol with the mass number at the top left and the atomic number at the bottom left: $\ce{^{35}_{17}Cl}$.
:::

Chlorine has two common isotopes. Chlorine-35 has $35 - 17 = 18$ neutrons, and chlorine-37 has $37 - 17 = 20$. Both have 17 protons and, as neutral atoms, 17 electrons, so both are chlorine and both react in the same way.

:::example Counting particles in an atom
How many protons, neutrons and electrons are in a neutral atom of $\ce{^{56}_{26}Fe}$?
@step The bottom number is the atomic number, 26, so the atom has 26 protons.
@step The top number is the mass number, 56. Neutrons $= 56 - 26 = 30$.
@step The atom is neutral, so electrons = protons = 26.
@answer 26 protons, 30 neutrons and 26 electrons.
:::

:::mistake Mass number is not the atomic mass
The periodic table also shows an **atomic mass**, such as about 35.5 for chlorine. It is an average over all the isotopes of the element (about three in every four chlorine atoms are chlorine-35), so it is usually not a whole number. To count neutrons, use the mass number of the isotope in the question, not the atomic mass from the table.
:::

## Valence electrons and the periodic table

Electrons are arranged in **energy levels** (sometimes called shells) around the nucleus. The electrons in the outermost level are the ones an atom uses when it reacts.

:::definition Valence electron
A **valence electron** is an electron in the outermost occupied energy level of an atom. Valence electrons are the electrons that take part in chemical bonding.
:::

The columns of the periodic table are called **groups**, numbered 1 to 18. Groups 1, 2 and 13 to 18 hold the **main-group elements**, and for these the group number gives the number of valence electrons:

- groups 1 and 2: valence electrons = the group number;
- groups 13 to 18: valence electrons = the group number − 10.

| Group | 1 | 2 | 13 | 14 | 15 | 16 | 17 | 18 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Valence electrons | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 | 6 | 7 | 8 |
| Example | Na | Mg | Al | C | N | O | Cl | Ar |

The rule is for main-group elements only; the transition metals in groups 3 to 12 do not follow it. Elements in the same group have the same number of valence electrons, which is why they behave alike. Some groups have family names: the group 1 metals are the **alkali metals**, group 2 the **alkaline-earth metals**, group 17 the **halogens** and group 18 the **noble gases**.

:::note Helium is the exception
Helium sits in group 18 but has only 2 electrons in all. Its single energy level is full with 2, so helium has 2 valence electrons, not 8.
:::

:::example Valence electrons from the group
How many valence electrons do calcium (group 2) and bromine (group 17) have?
@step Calcium is in group 2, so it has 2 valence electrons.
@step Bromine is in group 17, so it has $17 - 10 = 7$ valence electrons.
@answer Calcium has 2 valence electrons and bromine has 7.
:::

## How atoms become ions

The noble gases in group 18 hardly react at all, because their outer energy level is full: 8 valence electrons, called a **stable octet** (2 for helium). Atoms of other main-group elements tend to gain or lose electrons until they have the same arrangement of electrons as the nearest noble gas.

:::definition Ion, cation and anion
An **ion** is an atom or group of atoms with an electric charge, because its number of electrons is not equal to its number of protons.

- A **cation** is a positive ion. It forms when an atom **loses** electrons.
- An **anion** is a negative ion. It forms when an atom **gains** electrons.
:::

**Metals lose electrons.** A sodium atom has 11 electrons, arranged 2, 8, 1. Losing its one valence electron leaves 10 electrons arranged 2, 8, the same as neon. With 11 protons and 10 electrons, the ion has a charge of 1+: $\ce{Na+}$.

**Non-metals gain electrons.** A chlorine atom has 17 electrons, arranged 2, 8, 7. Gaining one electron gives 18 electrons arranged 2, 8, 8, the same as argon. With 17 protons and 18 electrons, the ion has a charge of 1−: $\ce{Cl-}$.

So the charge of a main-group ion follows from its group:

| Group | 1 | 2 | 13 | 15 | 16 | 17 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Valence electrons | 1 | 2 | 3 | 5 | 6 | 7 |
| Electrons lost or gained | lose 1 | lose 2 | lose 3 | gain 3 | gain 2 | gain 1 |
| Charge of the ion | 1+ | 2+ | 3+ | 3− | 2− | 1− |
| Examples | $\ce{Li+}$, $\ce{Na+}$, $\ce{K+}$ | $\ce{Mg^2+}$, $\ce{Ca^2+}$, $\ce{Sr^2+}$, $\ce{Ba^2+}$ | $\ce{Al^3+}$ | $\ce{N^3-}$, $\ce{P^3-}$ | $\ce{O^2-}$, $\ce{S^2-}$ | $\ce{F-}$, $\ce{Cl-}$, $\ce{Br-}$, $\ce{I-}$ |

Carbon and silicon, in group 14, would have to gain or lose four electrons, so they usually share electrons instead. The noble gases already have a full outer level and do not form ions.

A cation keeps the name of its element: $\ce{Na+}$ is the sodium ion and $\ce{Al^3+}$ is the aluminium ion (aluminium is spelled *aluminum* in North America). An anion changes the end of the element's name to **-ide**: chloride $\ce{Cl-}$, oxide $\ce{O^2-}$, nitride $\ce{N^3-}$, sulfide $\ce{S^2-}$ and phosphide $\ce{P^3-}$.

A charge is written at the top right, number first and then the sign: $\ce{Mg^2+}$ has a charge of 2+. A charge of 1 is written with the sign alone: $\ce{Na+}$, not Na¹⁺.

:::tip Typing a charge
In an answer box, type the charge after ^, with the number before the sign: `Mg^2+`, `SO4^2-` or `Cl^-`.
:::

:::example Predicting a cation
Which ion does calcium form?
@step Calcium is in group 2, so it has 2 valence electrons.
@step It is a metal, so it loses those 2 electrons and is left with the arrangement of argon.
@step It now has 20 protons and 18 electrons, a charge of 2+.
@answer $\ce{Ca^2+}$, the calcium ion.
:::

:::example Predicting an anion
Which ion does phosphorus form, and what is it called?
@step Phosphorus is in group 15, so it has $15 - 10 = 5$ valence electrons.
@step It is a non-metal, so it gains $8 - 5 = 3$ electrons to complete an octet, like argon.
@step Three extra electrons give a charge of 3−.
@answer $\ce{P^3-}$, the phosphide ion.
:::

## Counting electrons in an ion

Forming an ion changes only the electrons; the nucleus stays the same. To count the electrons in an ion, start from the atomic number and take away the charge:
$$\text{electrons} = \text{atomic number} - \text{charge}$$
A positive charge means fewer electrons than protons. A negative charge means more: taking away a negative number adds electrons.

:::example Counting particles in an ion
How many protons, neutrons and electrons are in the ion $\ce{^{27}_{13}Al^3+}$?
@step The atomic number is 13, so the ion has 13 protons.
@step Neutrons $= 27 - 13 = 14$.
@step Electrons $= 13 - 3 = 10$, the same number as neon.
@answer 13 protons, 14 neutrons and 10 electrons.
:::

:::mistake Gaining electrons makes a negative ion
Electrons are negative, so an atom that **gains** electrons becomes negative. The sulfide ion $\ce{S^2-}$ still has 16 protons, but it has $16 - (-2) = 18$ electrons, not 14.
:::

:::summary
- Atomic number = protons. Mass number = protons + neutrons, so neutrons = mass number − atomic number.
- Isotopes of an element have the same number of protons but different numbers of neutrons.
- Valence electrons: the group number for groups 1 and 2, the group number − 10 for groups 13 to 18 (helium has 2).
- Metals lose their valence electrons to form cations; non-metals gain electrons to form anions. Both end with the electron arrangement of the nearest noble gas, usually an octet.
- Charges by group: 1+, 2+, 3+ for groups 1, 2, 13; 3−, 2−, 1− for groups 15, 16, 17. Anions end in -ide.
- Electrons in an ion = atomic number − charge.
:::
