A bicycle workshop needs exactly two wheels for every frame. To build 50 bicycles it orders 100 wheels and 50 frames: a ratio of 2 to 1 by count, even though a frame weighs far more than a wheel. A balanced chemical equation is the same kind of parts list for a reaction, and chemists count its parts in moles.

In this lesson you will read the **mole ratio** between any two substances from a balanced equation, use it to find an amount in moles, and then find the mass of one substance in a reaction from the mass of another.

## What a balanced equation tells you

The coefficients of a balanced equation count particles. In $\ce{2H2 + O2 -> 2H2O}$, two hydrogen molecules react with one oxygen molecule to make two water molecules. Multiply every coefficient by Avogadro's number and the ratio stays the same, so the coefficients count moles as well.

| | $\ce{2H2}$ | $\ce{O2}$ | $\ce{2H2O}$ |
|---|---|---|---|
| Molecules | 2 | 1 | 2 |
| Moles | $2\ \text{mol}$ | $1\ \text{mol}$ | $2\ \text{mol}$ |
| Mass | $2(2.02) = 4.04\ \text{g}$ | $32.00\ \text{g}$ | $2(18.02) = 36.04\ \text{g}$ |

The masses obey the law of conservation of mass, $4.04 + 32.00 = 36.04\ \text{g}$, but they are not in the ratio 2 : 1 : 2. Only the amounts in moles follow the coefficients.

:::definition Stoichiometry
**Stoichiometry** is the calculation of the quantities of reactants and products in a chemical reaction, using the balanced equation.
:::

:::definition Mole ratio
A **mole ratio** is the ratio of the coefficients of two substances in a balanced equation. It is the ratio of their amounts in moles: in $\ce{2H2 + O2 -> 2H2O}$, the mole ratio of $\ce{H2}$ to $\ce{O2}$ is 2 : 1.
:::

:::mistake Coefficients are not grams
$\ce{2H2 + O2 -> 2H2O}$ does not mean that $2\ \text{g}$ of hydrogen react with $1\ \text{g}$ of oxygen. A coefficient counts particles, and particles of different substances have different masses. To use a mole ratio, you need amounts in moles.
:::

## Using a mole ratio

To find the amount of one substance from the amount of another, multiply by the mole ratio, written as the wanted coefficient over the given coefficient:

$$n(\text{wanted}) = n(\text{given}) \times \dfrac{\text{wanted coefficient}}{\text{given coefficient}}$$

:::example Ammonia from nitrogen
In the reaction $\ce{N2 + 3H2 -> 2NH3}$, how many moles of $\ce{NH3}$ form from $1.25\ \text{mol}$ of $\ce{N2}$? Give the answer to the nearest hundredth.
@step Given: $\ce{N2}$, coefficient 1. Wanted: $\ce{NH3}$, coefficient 2.
@step $n(\ce{NH3}) = 1.25 \times \dfrac{2}{1}$
@answer $2.50\ \text{mol}$ of $\ce{NH3}$
:::

:::example Burning butane
In the reaction $\ce{2C4H10 + 13O2 -> 8CO2 + 10H2O}$, how many moles of $\ce{O2}$ react with $0.640\ \text{mol}$ of $\ce{C4H10}$? Give the answer to the nearest hundredth.
@step Given: $\ce{C4H10}$, coefficient 2. Wanted: $\ce{O2}$, coefficient 13.
@step $n(\ce{O2}) = 0.640 \times \dfrac{13}{2}$
@answer $4.16\ \text{mol}$ of $\ce{O2}$
:::

:::tip Does the answer make sense?
The substance with the larger coefficient takes part in the larger amount. Oxygen's coefficient, 13, is bigger than butane's, 2, so the answer must be bigger than $0.640\ \text{mol}$, and it is.
:::

:::example Working backwards
In the reaction $\ce{2Al2O3 -> 4Al + 3O2}$, how many moles of $\ce{Al2O3}$ are needed to make $5.00\ \text{mol}$ of $\ce{Al}$? Give the answer to the nearest hundredth.
@step Given: $\ce{Al}$, coefficient 4. Wanted: $\ce{Al2O3}$, coefficient 2.
@step $n(\ce{Al2O3}) = 5.00 \times \dfrac{2}{4}$
@answer $2.50\ \text{mol}$ of $\ce{Al2O3}$
:::

:::mistake The ratio upside down
Writing $5.00 \times \dfrac{4}{2} = 10.0\ \text{mol}$ in the last example would mean more aluminium oxide than aluminium, yet each formula unit of $\ce{Al2O3}$ holds two aluminium atoms. The coefficient of the substance you want always goes on top.
:::

The coefficients are only a mole ratio when the equation is balanced. If it is not, balance it first, as in lesson 4.1.

:::example Balance first
In the reaction $\ce{Fe + Cl2 -> FeCl3}$ (not yet balanced), how many moles of $\ce{Cl2}$ react with $0.840\ \text{mol}$ of $\ce{Fe}$? Give the answer to the nearest hundredth.
@step Balanced: $\ce{2Fe + 3Cl2 -> 2FeCl3}$. Check: Fe 2 = 2, Cl 6 = 6.
@step $n(\ce{Cl2}) = 0.840 \times \dfrac{3}{2}$
@answer $1.26\ \text{mol}$ of $\ce{Cl2}$
:::

## Gravimetric stoichiometry

A balance measures mass, not moles. To go from the mass of one substance to the mass of another, pass through moles.

:::definition Gravimetric stoichiometry
**Gravimetric stoichiometry** finds the mass of one substance in a reaction from the mass of another. "Gravimetric" means measured by mass.
:::

1. **Write the balanced equation.**
2. **Change the given quantity to moles**: $n = \dfrac{m}{M}$.
3. **Use the mole ratio**: multiply by the wanted coefficient over the given coefficient.
4. **Change the wanted amount to mass**: $m = nM$.

$$m(\text{given}) \xrightarrow{\ \div M\ } n(\text{given}) \xrightarrow{\ \times \frac{\text{wanted coefficient}}{\text{given coefficient}}\ } n(\text{wanted}) \xrightarrow{\ \times M\ } m(\text{wanted})$$

When the amount of the given substance is already in moles, skip step 2.

:::example From moles to a mass
In the reaction $\ce{2Al + 3CuCl2 -> 2AlCl3 + 3Cu}$, what mass of $\ce{Cu}$ forms from $0.500\ \text{mol}$ of $\ce{Al}$? Give the answer to the nearest tenth. Use the molar mass Cu $63.55\ \text{g/mol}$.
@step $n(\ce{Cu}) = 0.500 \times \dfrac{3}{2} = 0.750\ \text{mol}$
@step $m = nM = 0.750 \times 63.55 = 47.66\ \text{g}$
@answer $47.7\ \text{g}$ of copper
:::

:::example Iron from thermite
The thermite reaction, used to weld steel rails, makes molten iron: $\ce{2Al + Fe2O3 -> Al2O3 + 2Fe}$. What mass of $\ce{Fe}$ forms from $50.0\ \text{g}$ of $\ce{Fe2O3}$? Give the answer to the nearest tenth. Use these molar masses: Fe $55.85\ \text{g/mol}$, O $16.00\ \text{g/mol}$.
@step $M(\ce{Fe2O3}) = 2(55.85) + 3(16.00) = 159.70\ \text{g/mol}$
@step $n(\ce{Fe2O3}) = \dfrac{50.0}{159.70} = 0.3131\ \text{mol}$
@step $n(\ce{Fe}) = 0.3131 \times \dfrac{2}{1} = 0.6262\ \text{mol}$
@step $m = 0.6262 \times 55.85 = 34.97\ \text{g}$
@answer $35.0\ \text{g}$ of iron
:::

:::example Oxygen for a barbecue
Propane burns completely: $\ce{C3H8 + 5O2 -> 3CO2 + 4H2O}$. What mass of $\ce{O2}$ is needed to burn $25.0\ \text{g}$ of $\ce{C3H8}$? Give the answer to the nearest tenth. Use these molar masses: C $12.01\ \text{g/mol}$, H $1.01\ \text{g/mol}$, O $16.00\ \text{g/mol}$.
@step $M(\ce{C3H8}) = 3(12.01) + 8(1.01) = 44.11\ \text{g/mol}$, so $n(\ce{C3H8}) = \dfrac{25.0}{44.11} = 0.5668\ \text{mol}$
@step $n(\ce{O2}) = 0.5668 \times \dfrac{5}{1} = 2.834\ \text{mol}$
@step $M(\ce{O2}) = 2(16.00) = 32.00\ \text{g/mol}$, so $m = 2.834 \times 32.00 = 90.68\ \text{g}$
@answer $90.7\ \text{g}$ of oxygen
:::

:::mistake Grams do not go through the ratio
Multiplying the mass by the ratio, $25.0 \times \dfrac{5}{1} = 125\ \text{g}$, is wrong: the coefficients compare moles, not grams. Always change the given mass to moles before you use the mole ratio.
:::

:::tip Round only at the end
Keep at least four significant figures in each step, or better, keep the unrounded value in your calculator. Round only the final mass.
:::

:::example From an unbalanced equation
In the reaction $\ce{C2H5OH + O2 -> CO2 + H2O}$ (not yet balanced), what mass of $\ce{CO2}$ forms from $10.0\ \text{g}$ of $\ce{C2H5OH}$? Give the answer to the nearest tenth. Use these molar masses: C $12.01\ \text{g/mol}$, H $1.01\ \text{g/mol}$, O $16.00\ \text{g/mol}$.
@step Balanced: $\ce{C2H5OH + 3O2 -> 2CO2 + 3H2O}$. Check: C 2 = 2, H 6 = 6, O 7 = 7.
@step $M(\ce{C2H5OH}) = 2(12.01) + 6(1.01) + 16.00 = 46.08\ \text{g/mol}$, so $n = \dfrac{10.0}{46.08} = 0.2170\ \text{mol}$
@step $n(\ce{CO2}) = 0.2170 \times \dfrac{2}{1} = 0.4340\ \text{mol}$
@step $M(\ce{CO2}) = 12.01 + 2(16.00) = 44.01\ \text{g/mol}$, so $m = 0.4340 \times 44.01 = 19.10\ \text{g}$
@answer $19.1\ \text{g}$ of carbon dioxide
:::

## Gravimetric analysis in the laboratory

Chemists use gravimetric stoichiometry to find how much of a substance a sample holds. To measure the barium chloride dissolved in a solution, for example, they add sodium sulfate solution **in excess**, more than enough to react with all of it, so that every barium ion ends up in a precipitate of barium sulfate. They filter off the precipitate, rinse it, dry it and weigh it, then work back to the barium chloride.

:::example Barium chloride in a solution
A solution of barium chloride is mixed with excess sodium sulfate solution: $\ce{BaCl2(aq) + Na2SO4(aq) -> BaSO4(s) + 2NaCl(aq)}$. The dried precipitate of $\ce{BaSO4}$ has a mass of $2.33\ \text{g}$. What mass of $\ce{BaCl2}$ was in the solution, to the nearest hundredth? Use these molar masses: Ba $137.33\ \text{g/mol}$, S $32.06\ \text{g/mol}$, O $16.00\ \text{g/mol}$, Cl $35.45\ \text{g/mol}$.
@step $M(\ce{BaSO4}) = 137.33 + 32.06 + 4(16.00) = 233.39\ \text{g/mol}$, so $n(\ce{BaSO4}) = \dfrac{2.33}{233.39} = 0.009983\ \text{mol}$
@step $n(\ce{BaCl2}) = 0.009983 \times \dfrac{1}{1} = 0.009983\ \text{mol}$
@step $M(\ce{BaCl2}) = 137.33 + 2(35.45) = 208.23\ \text{g/mol}$, so $m = 0.009983 \times 208.23 = 2.079\ \text{g}$
@answer $2.08\ \text{g}$ of barium chloride
:::

:::note Dry to constant mass
A wet precipitate weighs more than the solid alone, and the extra water would make the answer too large. The filter paper and solid are dried, weighed, dried again and weighed again until the mass stops changing.
:::

:::summary
- The coefficients of a balanced equation count particles, and so moles: they give the **mole ratio** of any two substances. They are not a ratio of masses.
- $n(\text{wanted}) = n(\text{given}) \times \dfrac{\text{wanted coefficient}}{\text{given coefficient}}$. Balance the equation first, and check that the answer moves the right way.
- **Gravimetric stoichiometry**: given mass → moles ($n = \dfrac{m}{M}$) → mole ratio → moles of the wanted substance → mass ($m = nM$).
- Never put grams through the mole ratio. Keep four significant figures in the working and round only at the end.
- In gravimetric analysis, an excess of one reactant turns all of a substance into a precipitate, which is filtered, dried and weighed.
:::
