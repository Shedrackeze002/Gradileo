Acetic acid in vinegar and hydrochloric acid in a laboratory bottle can have the same concentration, yet one is safe to sprinkle on food and the other burns skin. So far, acids have been simply "strong" or "weak". Among the weak acids, though, strength varies a million-fold, and chemists measure it with an equilibrium constant.

In this lesson you will write the acid and base ionisation constants, $K_a$ and $K_b$, and use them to compare the strengths of acids and bases, and you will use $K_aK_b = K_w$ to find one constant from the other.

## The acid ionisation constant

A weak acid, HA, ionises only partly in water. Its ionisation is an equilibrium, so it has an equilibrium constant.

$$\ce{HA(aq) + H2O(l) <=> H3O+(aq) + A-(aq)}$$

:::definition Acid ionisation constant
The **acid ionisation constant**, $K_a$, is the equilibrium constant for the ionisation of an acid in water:
$$K_a = \dfrac{[\ce{H3O+}][\ce{A-}]}{[\ce{HA}]}$$
Water, the solvent, is left out (lesson 4.2). The **larger** $K_a$, the more the acid ionises, and the **stronger** it is.
:::

| Acid | $K_a$ at $25^\circ\text{C}$ | Strength |
|---|---|---|
| hydrofluoric acid, $\ce{HF}$ | $6.6 \times 10^{-4}$ | strongest of these three |
| acetic acid, $\ce{CH3COOH}$ | $1.8 \times 10^{-5}$ | |
| hydrocyanic acid, $\ce{HCN}$ | $6.2 \times 10^{-10}$ | weakest of these three |

:::example Writing a Ka expression
Write the equation for the ionisation of nitrous acid, $\ce{HNO2}$, in water, and its $K_a$ expression.
@step $\ce{HNO2}$ gives a proton to water: $\ce{HNO2(aq) + H2O(l) <=> H3O+(aq) + NO2-(aq)}$
@step Products over reactants, leaving out liquid water.
@answer $K_a = \dfrac{[\ce{H3O+}][\ce{NO2-}]}{[\ce{HNO2}]}$
:::

## The base ionisation constant

A weak base, B, takes a proton from water, making its conjugate acid and hydroxide ions.

$$\ce{B(aq) + H2O(l) <=> HB+(aq) + OH-(aq)}$$

:::definition Base ionisation constant
The **base ionisation constant**, $K_b$, is the equilibrium constant for the reaction of a base with water:
$$K_b = \dfrac{[\ce{HB+}][\ce{OH-}]}{[\ce{B}]}$$
The **larger** $K_b$, the **stronger** the base.
:::

Weak bases include molecules such as ammonia and the anions of weak acids, such as fluoride, acetate and cyanide ions.

:::example Two Kb expressions
Write the $K_b$ expressions for ammonia, $\ce{NH3}$, and for the cyanide ion, $\ce{CN-}$.
@step $\ce{NH3(aq) + H2O(l) <=> NH4+(aq) + OH-(aq)}$, so $K_b = \dfrac{[\ce{NH4+}][\ce{OH-}]}{[\ce{NH3}]}$
@step $\ce{CN-(aq) + H2O(l) <=> HCN(aq) + OH-(aq)}$, so $K_b = \dfrac{[\ce{HCN}][\ce{OH-}]}{[\ce{CN-}]}$
@answer $K_b(\ce{NH3}) = \dfrac{[\ce{NH4+}][\ce{OH-}]}{[\ce{NH3}]}$ and $K_b(\ce{CN-}) = \dfrac{[\ce{HCN}][\ce{OH-}]}{[\ce{CN-}]}$
:::

:::mistake Hydroxide, not hydronium
A base in water makes $\ce{OH-}$ ions, so $[\ce{OH-}]$ belongs in every $K_b$ expression. Writing $[\ce{H3O+}]$ in a $K_b$ expression, or putting $[\ce{H2O}]$ on the bottom, is a common slip.
:::

## Ka × Kb = Kw

Recall that water ionises slightly: $\ce{2H2O(l) <=> H3O+(aq) + OH-(aq)}$. Its equilibrium constant is the **ion product of water**.

:::definition Ion product of water
$K_w = [\ce{H3O+}][\ce{OH-}] = 1.0 \times 10^{-14}$ at $25^\circ\text{C}$, in pure water and in every aqueous solution.
:::

Multiply $K_a$ of an acid by $K_b$ of its conjugate base, and almost everything cancels:
$$K_a \times K_b = \dfrac{[\ce{H3O+}][\ce{A-}]}{[\ce{HA}]} \times \dfrac{[\ce{HA}][\ce{OH-}]}{[\ce{A-}]} = [\ce{H3O+}][\ce{OH-}] = K_w$$

:::definition Ka and Kb of a conjugate pair
For any conjugate acid–base pair, $K_aK_b = K_w$. So $K_b = \dfrac{K_w}{K_a}$ and $K_a = \dfrac{K_w}{K_b}$.
:::

Because the product is fixed, a **stronger acid has a weaker conjugate base**, and the other way round, which is exactly the pattern in the table of relative acid strengths in lesson 4.5.

:::example Kb of a conjugate base
At $25^\circ\text{C}$, $K_a$ for hydrofluoric acid is $6.6 \times 10^{-4}$. What is $K_b$ for the fluoride ion, to two significant figures?
@step The fluoride ion, $\ce{F-}$, is the conjugate base of $\ce{HF}$.
@step $K_b = \dfrac{K_w}{K_a} = \dfrac{1.0 \times 10^{-14}}{6.6 \times 10^{-4}}$
@answer $K_b = 1.5 \times 10^{-11}$
:::

:::example Ka of a conjugate acid
At $25^\circ\text{C}$, $K_b$ for ammonia is $1.8 \times 10^{-5}$. What is $K_a$ for the ammonium ion, to two significant figures?
@step The ammonium ion, $\ce{NH4+}$, is the conjugate acid of $\ce{NH3}$.
@step $K_a = \dfrac{K_w}{K_b} = \dfrac{1.0 \times 10^{-14}}{1.8 \times 10^{-5}}$
@answer $K_a = 5.6 \times 10^{-10}$
:::

:::tip Typing scientific notation
Type $1.5 \times 10^{-11}$ as `1.5e-11` or `1.5*10^-11`.
:::

## Comparing acids and bases

$K_a$ and $K_b$ make comparisons quick:

- The acid with the **largest** $K_a$ is the strongest, and at the same concentration gives the **lowest** pH.
- The **smallest** $K_a$ belongs to the weakest acid, whose conjugate base is the **strongest** base.
- The weakest base, with the smallest $K_b$, has the strongest conjugate acid.

:::example The strongest conjugate base
Three weak acids have these $K_a$ values at $25^\circ\text{C}$: methanoic acid, $\ce{HCOOH}$, $1.8 \times 10^{-4}$; hypochlorous acid, $\ce{HClO}$, $4.0 \times 10^{-8}$; benzoic acid, $\ce{C6H5COOH}$, $6.3 \times 10^{-5}$. Which conjugate base is the strongest base?
@step The weakest acid has the smallest $K_a$: $\ce{HClO}$.
@step Its conjugate base has the largest $K_b$: $\dfrac{1.0 \times 10^{-14}}{4.0 \times 10^{-8}} = 2.5 \times 10^{-7}$.
@answer The hypochlorite ion, $\ce{ClO-}$.
:::

:::definition pKa
Just as $\text{pH} = -\log[\ce{H3O+}]$, chemists often write $\text{p}K_a = -\log K_a$. A **smaller** $\text{p}K_a$ means a **larger** $K_a$ and a stronger acid. For example, $\text{p}K_a$ is 3.18 for hydrofluoric acid, 4.74 for acetic acid and 9.21 for hydrocyanic acid. You will use $\text{p}K_a$ with buffers in lesson 4.8.
:::

## Amphiprotic ions: acidic or basic?

An amphiprotic ion such as $\ce{HCO3-}$ can give a proton (as an acid) or take one (as a base). Whether its solution is acidic or basic depends on which it does more, so compare its $K_a$ with its $K_b$. Its $K_b$ comes from the $K_a$ of its **conjugate acid**, not from its own $K_a$.

:::example Is sodium hydrogen carbonate acidic or basic?
At $25^\circ\text{C}$, $K_a$ for carbonic acid, $\ce{H2CO3}$, is $4.5 \times 10^{-7}$, and $K_a$ for $\ce{HCO3-}$ is $4.7 \times 10^{-11}$. Is a solution of sodium hydrogen carbonate acidic or basic?
@step As a base, $\ce{HCO3-}$ forms $\ce{H2CO3}$, so $K_b = \dfrac{1.0 \times 10^{-14}}{4.5 \times 10^{-7}} = 2.2 \times 10^{-8}$.
@step As an acid, $K_a = 4.7 \times 10^{-11}$, which is much smaller.
@answer $K_b > K_a$: the ion takes protons more than it gives them, so the solution is basic.
:::

:::mistake Using the ion's own Ka for its Kb
$K_aK_b = K_w$ works only for a **conjugate pair**. The $K_b$ of $\ce{HCO3-}$ pairs with the $K_a$ of $\ce{H2CO3}$, the acid it turns into; the $K_a$ of $\ce{HCO3-}$ itself pairs with the $K_b$ of $\ce{CO3^2-}$.
:::

:::check c30-ka-kb 3
:::

:::summary
- $K_a = \dfrac{[\ce{H3O+}][\ce{A-}]}{[\ce{HA}]}$ for $\ce{HA + H2O <=> H3O+ + A-}$; $K_b = \dfrac{[\ce{HB+}][\ce{OH-}]}{[\ce{B}]}$ for $\ce{B + H2O <=> HB+ + OH-}$. Water is left out of both.
- A larger $K_a$ means a stronger acid; a larger $K_b$ means a stronger base.
- $K_w = [\ce{H3O+}][\ce{OH-}] = 1.0 \times 10^{-14}$ at $25^\circ\text{C}$.
- For a conjugate pair, $K_aK_b = K_w$: the stronger the acid, the weaker its conjugate base.
- An amphiprotic ion is basic if its $K_b$ (from the $K_a$ of its conjugate acid) is larger than its own $K_a$, and acidic if its $K_a$ is larger.
:::
