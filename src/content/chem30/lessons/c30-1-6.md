There are millions of possible reactions, and no data table could list the enthalpy change of every one. Instead, tables list a single number for each compound: the energy change when it forms from its elements. With Hess's law, those numbers are enough to find the enthalpy change of any reaction between the compounds in the table.

In this lesson you will write formation equations, with each element in its standard state, and calculate the enthalpy change of a reaction from **standard enthalpies of formation**.

## Standard states and formation equations

Enthalpy changes depend on conditions, so tables give them for **standard conditions**. In this unit those are SATP: $25^\circ\text{C}$ and $100\ \text{kPa}$. A symbol with a small circle, as in $\Delta_r H^\circ$, means the value is for standard conditions.

:::definition Standard state
The **standard state** of a substance is its most stable form at SATP: a solid, a liquid or a gas, and for an element, the form its atoms take, such as single atoms or diatomic molecules.
:::

| Element | Standard state |
|---|---|
| hydrogen, oxygen, nitrogen, fluorine, chlorine | diatomic gases: $\ce{H2(g)}$, $\ce{O2(g)}$, $\ce{N2(g)}$, $\ce{F2(g)}$, $\ce{Cl2(g)}$ |
| bromine | a diatomic liquid: $\ce{Br2(l)}$ |
| iodine | a diatomic solid: $\ce{I2(s)}$ |
| mercury | a liquid: $\ce{Hg(l)}$ |
| carbon | a solid (graphite): $\ce{C(s)}$ |
| the other metals | solids, written as atoms: $\ce{Na(s)}$, $\ce{Al(s)}$, $\ce{Fe(s)}$ |

:::definition Formation equation
A **formation equation** shows **one mole** of a compound forming from its elements in their standard states. Because exactly one mole of product forms, fractions are often needed in front of the elements.
:::

:::definition Standard enthalpy of formation
The **standard enthalpy of formation**, $\Delta_f H^\circ$, of a compound is the enthalpy change of its formation equation at standard conditions, in $\text{kJ/mol}$. For an element in its standard state, $\Delta_f H^\circ = 0$: nothing has to happen to form it.
:::

:::example Writing formation equations
Write the formation equation of water, $\ce{H2O(l)}$, and of aluminium oxide, $\ce{Al2O3(s)}$.
@step One $\ce{H2O}$ has 2 H atoms, which is one $\ce{H2}$ molecule, and 1 O atom, which is half an $\ce{O2}$ molecule.
@step One $\ce{Al2O3}$ has 2 Al atoms and 3 O atoms, which is $\tfrac{3}{2}$ $\ce{O2}$ molecules.
@answer $\ce{H2(g) + 1/2 O2(g) -> H2O(l)}$ and $\ce{2Al(s) + 3/2 O2(g) -> Al2O3(s)}$
:::

:::example A compound of three elements
Write the formation equation of ethanol, $\ce{C2H5OH(l)}$.
@step One molecule has 2 C, 6 H and 1 O atoms.
@step Carbon is $\ce{C(s)}$: 2 mol. Six H atoms are $3\ \ce{H2(g)}$. One O atom is $\tfrac{1}{2}\ \ce{O2(g)}$.
@answer $\ce{2C(s) + 3H2(g) + 1/2 O2(g) -> C2H5OH(l)}$
:::

:::example A metal, carbon and oxygen
Write the formation equation of calcium carbonate, $\ce{CaCO3(s)}$.
@step One formula unit has 1 Ca, 1 C and 3 O atoms.
@step Calcium is a metal, written $\ce{Ca(s)}$; carbon is $\ce{C(s)}$; three O atoms are $\tfrac{3}{2}$ $\ce{O2(g)}$ molecules.
@answer $\ce{Ca(s) + C(s) + 3/2 O2(g) -> CaCO3(s)}$
:::

:::mistake Clearing the fractions spoils a formation equation
$\ce{2H2(g) + O2(g) -> 2H2O(l)}$ is balanced, but it is not a formation equation: it makes **two** moles of water, and its enthalpy change is twice $\Delta_f H^\circ$. Keep the fractions. Watch the states too: hydrogen and oxygen must be written as $\ce{H2(g)}$ and $\ce{O2(g)}$, not as single atoms, and bromine as $\ce{Br2(l)}$, not a gas.
:::

## Enthalpy changes from enthalpies of formation

Any reaction can be imagined in two steps through the elements: first take the reactants apart into their elements (the reverse of their formation equations), then build the products from those elements (their formation equations). By Hess's law, the enthalpy change is the sum of the two steps.

For the combustion of methane, taking $\ce{CH4(g) + 2O2(g)}$ apart into its elements absorbs $74.6\ \text{kJ}$ (the reverse of $\Delta_f H^\circ = -74.6\ \text{kJ/mol}$; oxygen is already an element). Building $\ce{CO2(g) + 2H2O(g)}$ from the elements releases $393.5 + 2(241.8) = 877.1\ \text{kJ}$. Overall, $\Delta_r H^\circ = +74.6 - 877.1 = -802.5\ \text{kJ}$. In general:

$$\Delta_r H^\circ = \sum n\,\Delta_f H^\circ(\text{products}) - \sum n\,\Delta_f H^\circ(\text{reactants})$$

where each $n$ is the coefficient of a substance in the balanced equation.

| Substance | $\Delta_f H^\circ$ (kJ/mol) | Substance | $\Delta_f H^\circ$ (kJ/mol) |
|---|---|---|---|
| $\ce{H2O(l)}$ | −285.8 | $\ce{CH4(g)}$ | −74.6 |
| $\ce{H2O(g)}$ | −241.8 | $\ce{C3H8(g)}$ | −103.8 |
| $\ce{CO2(g)}$ | −393.5 | $\ce{C2H5OH(l)}$ | −277.6 |
| $\ce{CO(g)}$ | −110.5 | $\ce{Fe2O3(s)}$ | −824.2 |

These are the values used in this unit; each question gives the values it needs.

:::note What the sign of an enthalpy of formation says
Most compounds have negative enthalpies of formation: forming them from their elements releases energy, so they are lower in enthalpy than their elements. A few, such as ethyne, $\ce{C2H2(g)}$ ($+227.4\ \text{kJ/mol}$), have positive values: they hold more enthalpy than their elements, which is one reason ethyne burns with such a hot flame. The two values for water differ by $44.0\ \text{kJ/mol}$, the energy absorbed when one mole of liquid water evaporates at $25^\circ\text{C}$.
:::

:::example The combustion of methane
Find $\Delta_r H^\circ$ for $\ce{CH4(g) + 2O2(g) -> CO2(g) + 2H2O(g)}$ from the table.
@step Products: $(-393.5) + 2(-241.8) = -877.1\ \text{kJ}$
@step Reactants: $(-74.6) + 2(0) = -74.6\ \text{kJ}$, since $\ce{O2(g)}$ is an element in its standard state.
@step $\Delta_r H^\circ = -877.1 - (-74.6) = -802.5\ \text{kJ}$
@answer $\Delta_r H^\circ = -802.5\ \text{kJ}$
:::

:::example Smelting iron
In a blast furnace, carbon monoxide reduces iron(III) oxide: $\ce{Fe2O3(s) + 3CO(g) -> 2Fe(s) + 3CO2(g)}$. Find $\Delta_r H^\circ$.
@step Products: $2(0) + 3(-393.5) = -1180.5\ \text{kJ}$
@step Reactants: $(-824.2) + 3(-110.5) = -1155.7\ \text{kJ}$
@step $\Delta_r H^\circ = -1180.5 - (-1155.7) = -24.8\ \text{kJ}$
@answer $\Delta_r H^\circ = -24.8\ \text{kJ}$: slightly exothermic.
:::

:::mistake Products minus reactants, with every coefficient
Subtract in the order **products − reactants**; the other way round gives the right size with the wrong sign. Multiply every $\Delta_f H^\circ$ by its coefficient before adding, and use the value for the right state: $\ce{H2O(l)}$ and $\ce{H2O(g)}$ differ by $44.0\ \text{kJ/mol}$.
:::

The same equation can be solved for an unknown enthalpy of formation when $\Delta_r H^\circ$ has been measured, usually by burning the compound in a bomb calorimeter.

:::example Working backwards
For $\ce{C3H8(g) + 5O2(g) -> 3CO2(g) + 4H2O(g)}$, $\Delta_r H^\circ = -2043.9\ \text{kJ}$. Find $\Delta_f H^\circ$ of propane, using $-393.5\ \text{kJ/mol}$ for $\ce{CO2(g)}$ and $-241.8\ \text{kJ/mol}$ for $\ce{H2O(g)}$.
@step Products: $3(-393.5) + 4(-241.8) = -2147.7\ \text{kJ}$
@step $-2043.9 = -2147.7 - x$, where $x = \Delta_f H^\circ(\ce{C3H8(g)})$ and oxygen counts as zero.
@step $x = -2147.7 + 2043.9 = -103.8$
@answer $\Delta_f H^\circ(\ce{C3H8(g)}) = -103.8\ \text{kJ/mol}$
:::

:::summary
- A **formation equation** makes one mole of a compound from its elements in their **standard states** at SATP; fractions are allowed.
- The **standard enthalpy of formation**, $\Delta_f H^\circ$, is the enthalpy change of that equation, in $\text{kJ/mol}$. For an element in its standard state it is zero.
- $\Delta_r H^\circ = \sum n\Delta_f H^\circ(\text{products}) - \sum n\Delta_f H^\circ(\text{reactants})$, which is Hess's law with a route through the elements.
- Use every coefficient, the right state of each substance, and products minus reactants. The same equation can be solved for an unknown $\Delta_f H^\circ$.
:::
