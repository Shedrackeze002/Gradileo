On a sunny afternoon at the beach, the dry sand can be too hot to walk on while the sea is still cool. Both have had the same sunshine all day. Water simply needs far more energy than sand to warm up by each degree. That property of water makes it ideal for measuring energy: let a reaction warm or cool a known mass of water, read the thermometer, and you can work out how much energy moved.

In this lesson you will calculate the heat gained or lost by a substance from its mass, its specific heat capacity and its change in temperature, solve the same relationship for a mass, a specific heat capacity or a temperature, and explain the assumptions behind a simple calorimeter.

## Heat and specific heat capacity

:::definition Heat
**Heat**, $Q$, is energy that moves from one substance to another because of a difference in temperature. It always flows from the hotter substance to the colder one, and it is measured in joules ($\text{J}$) or kilojoules ($1\ \text{kJ} = 1000\ \text{J}$).
:::

Temperature and heat are not the same thing. A bathtub of warm water holds far more energy than a cup of boiling water, even though the cup is hotter. How much heat a substance gains or loses depends on how much of it there is, what it is made of, and how far its temperature changes.

:::definition Specific heat capacity
The **specific heat capacity**, $c$, of a substance is the heat needed to raise the temperature of one gram of it by one degree Celsius. Its unit is $\text{J/(g·°C)}$.
:::

| Substance | $c$ ($\text{J/(g·°C)}$) |
|---|---|
| water | 4.19 |
| ethanol | 2.44 |
| aluminium | 0.897 |
| glass | 0.840 |
| iron | 0.449 |
| copper | 0.385 |
| lead | 0.129 |

Water's value is one of the largest of any common substance: warming a gram of water takes almost eleven times as much heat as warming a gram of copper. These are the values used in this unit; each question states the values it needs.

## Calculating heat: $Q = mc\Delta t$

$$Q = mc\Delta t$$

Here $m$ is the mass in grams, $c$ is the specific heat capacity in $\text{J/(g·°C)}$, and $\Delta t = t_\text{final} - t_\text{initial}$ is the change in temperature in degrees Celsius. With these units, $Q$ comes out in joules.

A positive $\Delta t$ means the substance warmed and **gained** heat; a negative $\Delta t$ means it cooled and **lost** heat. The questions in this unit ask for the size of $Q$ and then whether the heat was gained or lost.

:::example Warming water
How much heat is needed to warm $250.0\ \text{g}$ of water from $20.0^\circ\text{C}$ to $75.0^\circ\text{C}$? Give the answer in kilojoules, to the nearest tenth. Use $c = 4.19\ \text{J/(g·°C)}$ for water.
@step $\Delta t = 75.0 - 20.0 = 55.0^\circ\text{C}$
@step $Q = mc\Delta t = 250.0 \times 4.19 \times 55.0 = 57\,612.5\ \text{J}$
@step Divide by 1000 for kilojoules: $57.6125\ \text{kJ}$.
@answer $Q = 57.6\ \text{kJ}$, gained by the water.
:::

:::example A block that cools
A $1.50\ \text{kg}$ iron block cools from $85.0^\circ\text{C}$ to $22.0^\circ\text{C}$. How much heat does it lose, in kilojoules? Use $c = 0.449\ \text{J/(g·°C)}$ for iron.
@step Change the mass to grams: $1.50\ \text{kg} = 1500\ \text{g}$.
@step $\Delta t = 22.0 - 85.0 = -63.0^\circ\text{C}$: the block cooled, so it lost heat.
@step $Q = 1500 \times 0.449 \times 63.0 = 42\,430.5\ \text{J}$
@answer $42.4\ \text{kJ}$ lost.
:::

:::mistake Grams, not kilograms
The specific heat capacity is per **gram**, so the mass must be in grams. Putting $1.50$ for the iron block would give $42.4\ \text{J}$, a thousand times too small. A volume of water or of a dilute solution becomes a mass through its density, $1.00\ \text{g/mL}$: $250.0\ \text{mL}$ of water has a mass of $250.0\ \text{g}$.
:::

When a container warms along with the water inside it, each absorbs its own share of heat. Find $Q$ for each and add them.

:::example A saucepan of water
A $0.500\ \text{kg}$ aluminium saucepan holds $1.20\ \text{L}$ of water at $18.0^\circ\text{C}$. How much heat brings both to $100.0^\circ\text{C}$? Use $0.897\ \text{J/(g·°C)}$ for aluminium, $4.19\ \text{J/(g·°C)}$ for water, and a density of $1.00\ \text{kg/L}$ for water.
@step Both warm by $\Delta t = 100.0 - 18.0 = 82.0^\circ\text{C}$. The masses are $500\ \text{g}$ of aluminium and $1200\ \text{g}$ of water.
@step Aluminium: $Q = 500 \times 0.897 \times 82.0 = 36\,777\ \text{J}$
@step Water: $Q = 1200 \times 4.19 \times 82.0 = 412\,296\ \text{J}$
@step Total: $36\,777 + 412\,296 = 449\,073\ \text{J}$
@answer About $449\ \text{kJ}$. More than nine tenths of it goes into the water.
:::

## Solving for a mass, a heat capacity or a temperature

The same equation can be rearranged for any one of its quantities:

$$m = \frac{Q}{c\Delta t} \qquad c = \frac{Q}{m\Delta t} \qquad \Delta t = \frac{Q}{mc}$$

Keep $Q$ in joules whenever $c$ is in $\text{J/(g·°C)}$.

:::example Identifying a metal
A $50.0\ \text{g}$ sample of an unknown metal absorbs $1150\ \text{J}$ of heat as it warms from $21.0^\circ\text{C}$ to $72.0^\circ\text{C}$. Find its specific heat capacity, and identify it from the table above.
@step $\Delta t = 72.0 - 21.0 = 51.0^\circ\text{C}$
@step $c = \dfrac{Q}{m\Delta t} = \dfrac{1150}{50.0 \times 51.0} = 0.451\ \text{J/(g·°C)}$
@answer $c = 0.451\ \text{J/(g·°C)}$, closest to iron ($0.449$).
:::

:::example A final temperature
$200.0\ \text{g}$ of water at $21.5^\circ\text{C}$ absorbs $5.00\ \text{kJ}$ of heat. What is its final temperature?
@step $Q = 5.00\ \text{kJ} = 5000\ \text{J}$
@step $\Delta t = \dfrac{Q}{mc} = \dfrac{5000}{200.0 \times 4.19} = 5.97^\circ\text{C}$
@step The water gained heat, so it warmed: $t_\text{final} = 21.5 + 5.97 = 27.47^\circ\text{C}$
@answer $27.5^\circ\text{C}$
:::

## Heat lost equals heat gained

When a hot object is placed in cold water inside an insulated container, heat flows from the object to the water until both reach the same final temperature. If no heat escapes, energy is conserved:

$$\text{heat lost by the hot object} = \text{heat gained by the cold water}$$

Each side is a $Q = mc\Delta t$, where each $\Delta t$ is measured to the shared final temperature, $t_f$.

:::example A hot metal in cold water
A $100.0\ \text{g}$ piece of copper is heated to $98.0^\circ\text{C}$ in boiling water and dropped into $150.0\ \text{g}$ of water at $20.0^\circ\text{C}$ in an insulated cup. What is the final temperature? Use $0.385\ \text{J/(g·°C)}$ for copper and $4.19\ \text{J/(g·°C)}$ for water.
@step Heat lost by the copper = heat gained by the water: $100.0(0.385)(98.0 - t_f) = 150.0(4.19)(t_f - 20.0)$
@step $38.5(98.0 - t_f) = 628.5(t_f - 20.0)$, so $3773 - 38.5\,t_f = 628.5\,t_f - 12\,570$
@step Collect the terms: $667.0\,t_f = 16\,343$
@answer $t_f = 24.5^\circ\text{C}$. The copper cools by $73.5^\circ\text{C}$ but the water warms by only $4.5^\circ\text{C}$, because water's specific heat capacity is so much larger.
:::

## The simple calorimeter and its assumptions

A **calorimeter** is a container in which an energy change is measured through the temperature change of the water around it. A simple calorimeter for reactions in solution is two nested polystyrene cups with a lid, a thermometer through the lid, and a stirrer. The reactants are mixed in the cup, the solution is stirred, and the highest or lowest temperature reached is recorded.

The calculation $Q = mc\Delta t$ for the water rests on these assumptions:

1. **No heat is lost to, or gained from, the surroundings outside the cup.** Polystyrene is a good insulator, and the lid cuts down heat loss by evaporation and to the air.
2. **The cup, thermometer and stirrer absorb a negligible amount of heat.**
3. **A dilute aqueous solution behaves like water**: it has the density, $1.00\ \text{g/mL}$, and the specific heat capacity, $4.19\ \text{J/(g·°C)}$, of water.
4. **All the energy released or absorbed by the reaction shows up as a temperature change of the water.** Stirring spreads the heat evenly, so the thermometer reads the temperature of the whole solution.

No calorimeter is perfect. Some heat always leaks away, so a measured temperature change is usually a little smaller than it would be in a perfectly isolated system.

:::mistake The mass in Q = mcΔt is the water's
When a solid dissolves or reacts in a calorimeter, $Q = mc\Delta t$ is worked out for the **water** (or the solution), whose temperature is measured, not for the solid. Use the mass of the water and the specific heat capacity of water.
:::

:::summary
- **Heat**, $Q$, is energy transferred because of a temperature difference. The **specific heat capacity**, $c$, is the heat needed to warm $1\ \text{g}$ of a substance by $1^\circ\text{C}$; for water it is $4.19\ \text{J/(g·°C)}$.
- $Q = mc\Delta t$, with $m$ in grams, $c$ in $\text{J/(g·°C)}$ and $\Delta t = t_\text{final} - t_\text{initial}$. A rise means heat was gained; a fall means heat was lost.
- Rearrange for $m = \dfrac{Q}{c\Delta t}$, $c = \dfrac{Q}{m\Delta t}$ or $\Delta t = \dfrac{Q}{mc}$, keeping $Q$ in joules.
- With no heat lost, heat lost by a hot object = heat gained by the cold water, both ending at the same temperature.
- A simple calorimeter assumes no heat is exchanged with the outside, the cup absorbs almost none, and a dilute solution behaves like water.
:::
