How much energy is released when carbon burns to carbon monoxide, $\ce{C(s) + 1/2 O2(g) -> CO(g)}$? It sounds like a simple experiment, but it cannot be done cleanly: whenever carbon burns in oxygen, some of it goes on to form carbon dioxide, and the measured energy is a mixture of both reactions. Chemists find the answer another way, by combining reactions that **can** be measured.

In this lesson you will reverse and multiply thermochemical equations, changing their enthalpy changes to match, and combine equations with **Hess's law** to find the enthalpy change of a reaction that is hard or impossible to measure directly.

## Reversing and multiplying equations

A thermochemical equation can be changed, as long as its enthalpy change is changed to match.

- **Reversing** an equation reverses the energy change. If a reaction releases $483.6\ \text{kJ}$ one way, it absorbs $483.6\ \text{kJ}$ the other way: **change the sign** of $\Delta H$.
- **Multiplying** an equation by a number multiplies the amounts, and so the energy, by the same number: **multiply** $\Delta H$ by it. Fractions are allowed, such as $\tfrac{1}{2}$ or $\tfrac{3}{2}$.

:::example Reversing and halving
Given $\ce{2H2(g) + O2(g) -> 2H2O(g)}\qquad \Delta_r H = -483.6\ \text{kJ}$, find $\Delta_r H$ for $\ce{H2O(g) -> H2(g) + 1/2 O2(g)}$.
@step The water vapour has moved to the reactant side, so the equation is reversed: $\Delta_r H$ becomes $+483.6\ \text{kJ}$.
@step The coefficient of water went from 2 to 1, so the equation is multiplied by $\tfrac{1}{2}$: $\tfrac{1}{2} \times (+483.6) = +241.8\ \text{kJ}$.
@answer $\Delta_r H = +241.8\ \text{kJ}$
:::

:::example Tripling
Given $\ce{N2(g) + 3H2(g) -> 2NH3(g)}\qquad \Delta_r H = -91.8\ \text{kJ}$, find $\Delta_r H$ for $\ce{3N2(g) + 9H2(g) -> 6NH3(g)}$.
@step Same direction, every coefficient multiplied by 3.
@answer $\Delta_r H = 3 \times (-91.8) = -275.4\ \text{kJ}$
:::

:::mistake Multiplying does not change the sign
Only reversing changes the sign. Multiplying by 3 makes $-91.8\ \text{kJ}$ into $-275.4\ \text{kJ}$, still negative: three times the reaction releases three times the energy. And when you reverse **and** multiply, do both: $\ce{6NH3(g) -> 3N2(g) + 9H2(g)}$ has $\Delta_r H = +275.4\ \text{kJ}$.
:::

## Hess's law

Enthalpy depends only on the state of a system, not on how it got there. So the enthalpy change from reactants to products is the same whichever route the reaction takes: in one step, or in several.

:::definition Hess's law
**Hess's law**: the enthalpy change of a reaction is the same whether the reaction takes place in one step or in a series of steps. If equations add up to a target equation, their enthalpy changes add up to the enthalpy change of the target.
:::

The diagram shows two routes from carbon and oxygen to carbon dioxide. Carbon can burn straight to $\ce{CO2}$, or first to $\ce{CO}$, which then burns to $\ce{CO2}$. Both routes start and finish at the same enthalpies, so the two steps must add up to the one-step value.

:::note Why Hess's law must be true
Suppose the two routes in the diagram had different enthalpy changes. You could then go from $\ce{C(s) + O2(g)}$ to $\ce{CO2(g)}$ by the route that releases more energy, and back by the route that absorbs less, ending where you started with energy left over. That would create energy from nothing, which never happens. Hess's law is the law of conservation of energy applied to chemical reactions.
:::

:::definition Target equation
The **target equation** is the reaction whose enthalpy change you want to find. The given equations are rearranged so that, when they are added, everything except the target cancels.
:::

To combine equations:

1. **Find each substance of the target** in the given equations. Start with a substance that appears in only one of them.
2. **Reverse** an equation if that substance is on the wrong side, and change the sign of its $\Delta H$.
3. **Multiply** the equation so the coefficient matches the target, and multiply its $\Delta H$ too.
4. **Add** the equations. Substances that appear on both sides cancel; what is left must be the target equation.
5. **Add** the enthalpy changes.

:::example Carbon monoxide
Use these equations to find $\Delta_r H$ for $\ce{C(s) + 1/2 O2(g) -> CO(g)}$:

(1) $\ce{C(s) + O2(g) -> CO2(g)}\qquad \Delta_r H = -393.5\ \text{kJ}$

(2) $\ce{CO(g) + 1/2 O2(g) -> CO2(g)}\qquad \Delta_r H = -283.0\ \text{kJ}$
@step The target has $\ce{C(s)}$ as a reactant, as in (1): keep (1) as it is, $-393.5\ \text{kJ}$.
@step The target has $\ce{CO(g)}$ as a product, but (2) has it as a reactant: reverse (2), $\ce{CO2(g) -> CO(g) + 1/2 O2(g)}$, $+283.0\ \text{kJ}$.
@step Add: $\ce{CO2}$ cancels, and of the $\ce{O2}$, $1 - \tfrac{1}{2} = \tfrac{1}{2}$ mol is left on the reactant side: $\ce{C(s) + 1/2 O2(g) -> CO(g)}$.
@answer $\Delta_r H = -393.5 + 283.0 = -110.5\ \text{kJ}$
:::

Three equations work the same way. A common use is finding the enthalpy change of a reaction that does not happen cleanly, such as making methane from carbon and hydrogen, from the combustion of each substance, which is easy to measure in a bomb calorimeter.

:::example Methane from its elements
Use these equations to find $\Delta_r H$ for $\ce{C(s) + 2H2(g) -> CH4(g)}$:

(1) $\ce{C(s) + O2(g) -> CO2(g)}\qquad \Delta_r H = -393.5\ \text{kJ}$

(2) $\ce{H2(g) + 1/2 O2(g) -> H2O(l)}\qquad \Delta_r H = -285.8\ \text{kJ}$

(3) $\ce{CH4(g) + 2O2(g) -> CO2(g) + 2H2O(l)}\qquad \Delta_r H = -890.5\ \text{kJ}$
@step $\ce{C(s)}$, a reactant in the target, is a reactant in (1): keep (1), $-393.5\ \text{kJ}$.
@step The target has $2\ \text{mol}$ of $\ce{H2(g)}$: multiply (2) by 2, $\ce{2H2(g) + O2(g) -> 2H2O(l)}$, $2 \times (-285.8) = -571.6\ \text{kJ}$.
@step $\ce{CH4(g)}$ is a product in the target but a reactant in (3): reverse (3), $\ce{CO2(g) + 2H2O(l) -> CH4(g) + 2O2(g)}$, $+890.5\ \text{kJ}$.
@step Add: $\ce{CO2}$, $\ce{H2O}$ and all $3\ \text{mol}$ of $\ce{O2}$ cancel, leaving $\ce{C(s) + 2H2(g) -> CH4(g)}$.
@answer $\Delta_r H = -393.5 - 571.6 + 890.5 = -74.6\ \text{kJ}$
:::

:::example Fractions in Hess's law
When steam is passed over hot coke (carbon), it makes a fuel gas: $\ce{C(s) + H2O(g) -> CO(g) + H2(g)}$. Find $\Delta_r H$ from:

(1) $\ce{C(s) + O2(g) -> CO2(g)}\qquad \Delta_r H = -393.5\ \text{kJ}$

(2) $\ce{2CO(g) + O2(g) -> 2CO2(g)}\qquad \Delta_r H = -566.0\ \text{kJ}$

(3) $\ce{2H2(g) + O2(g) -> 2H2O(g)}\qquad \Delta_r H = -483.6\ \text{kJ}$
@step Keep (1): $\ce{C(s) + O2(g) -> CO2(g)}$, $-393.5\ \text{kJ}$.
@step The target makes $1\ \text{mol}$ of $\ce{CO(g)}$: reverse (2) and multiply by $\tfrac{1}{2}$, $\ce{CO2(g) -> CO(g) + 1/2 O2(g)}$, $+283.0\ \text{kJ}$.
@step The target uses $1\ \text{mol}$ of $\ce{H2O(g)}$: reverse (3) and multiply by $\tfrac{1}{2}$, $\ce{H2O(g) -> H2(g) + 1/2 O2(g)}$, $+241.8\ \text{kJ}$.
@step Add: $\ce{CO2}$ cancels, and the $1\ \text{mol}$ of $\ce{O2}$ used in (1) cancels the two half moles formed.
@answer $\Delta_r H = -393.5 + 283.0 + 241.8 = +131.3\ \text{kJ}$: endothermic, so the coke must be kept hot.
:::

:::tip Check before you add
Before adding the enthalpy changes, add the equations themselves and cross out what cancels. If what is left is not exactly the target equation, one of the equations has the wrong direction or the wrong multiplier.
:::

:::summary
- Reversing an equation changes the sign of $\Delta H$. Multiplying an equation by a number multiplies $\Delta H$ by that number; fractions are allowed.
- **Hess's law**: the enthalpy change from reactants to products is the same by any route, so the enthalpy changes of equations that add up to a **target equation** add up to its enthalpy change.
- Rearrange each given equation to match the target (reverse, multiply), add the equations so that everything else cancels, and add their enthalpy changes.
- Hess's law finds enthalpy changes that are hard to measure directly, from reactions that can be measured, such as combustions.
:::
