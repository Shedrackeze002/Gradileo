A data table lists the molar enthalpy of combustion of methane as $-802.5\ \text{kJ/mol}$. An engineering handbook writes the same reaction with "$802.5\ \text{kJ}$" at the end of the equation, and a textbook draws it as two lines with an arrow pointing down. All three say the same thing. Chemists have several ways to communicate an energy change, and you need to read and write each of them, and move from one to another.

In this lesson you will write an energy change as a term in a thermochemical equation, as an enthalpy change beside the equation, and as a molar enthalpy, and you will draw and read enthalpy diagrams for exothermic and endothermic reactions.

## Four ways to communicate an energy change

:::definition Thermochemical equation
A **thermochemical equation** is a balanced chemical equation, with states, that also shows its energy change, either as an energy term in the equation or as an enthalpy change beside it.
:::

Here is the combustion of methane, forming water vapour, written four ways:

| Method | The combustion of methane |
|---|---|
| 1. An energy term in the equation | $\ce{CH4(g) + 2O2(g) -> CO2(g) + 2H2O(g)} + 802.5\ \text{kJ}$ |
| 2. The enthalpy change beside the equation | $\ce{CH4(g) + 2O2(g) -> CO2(g) + 2H2O(g)}\qquad \Delta_r H = -802.5\ \text{kJ}$ |
| 3. A molar enthalpy | $\Delta_c H_m(\ce{CH4}) = -802.5\ \text{kJ/mol}$ |
| 4. An enthalpy diagram | the products drawn $802.5\ \text{kJ}$ below the reactants (below) |

For an exothermic reaction, energy is a product: it is written on the **right**, with the products, and $\Delta_r H$ is negative. For an endothermic reaction, energy is consumed like a reactant: it is written on the **left**, with the reactants, and $\Delta_r H$ is positive. An energy term is always written as a positive amount; the side it is on carries the meaning.

:::example From an energy term to ΔrH
Limestone decomposes when it is heated strongly: $\ce{CaCO3(s)} + 179.2\ \text{kJ} \mathrel{\ce{->}} \ce{CaO(s) + CO2(g)}$. What is $\Delta_r H$, and is the reaction exothermic or endothermic?
@step The energy is written with the reactant, so it is absorbed.
@step Absorbed energy means the system gains enthalpy: $\Delta_r H$ is positive.
@answer $\Delta_r H = +179.2\ \text{kJ}$: endothermic.
:::

:::mistake No minus signs in energy terms
Writing $\ce{CH4(g) + 2O2(g) -> CO2(g) + 2H2O(g)} - 802.5\ \text{kJ}$ mixes up two methods. The minus sign belongs to $\Delta_r H$, written beside the equation. As a term, the energy is simply placed on the side where it appears: $+ 802.5\ \text{kJ}$ with the products.
:::

## ΔrH and molar enthalpies

$\Delta_r H$ is the enthalpy change for the equation **exactly as written**, with the amounts given by its coefficients. To find the molar enthalpy for one substance, divide by that substance's coefficient:

$$\Delta_r H_m = \frac{\Delta_r H}{\text{coefficient}}$$

:::example One equation, three molar enthalpies
For $\ce{4NH3(g) + 5O2(g) -> 4NO(g) + 6H2O(g)}$, $\Delta_r H = -902.0\ \text{kJ}$. Find the molar enthalpy of reaction per mole of ammonia, of oxygen and of water.
@step $\Delta_r H$ is for $4\ \text{mol}$ of $\ce{NH3}$, $5\ \text{mol}$ of $\ce{O2}$ and $6\ \text{mol}$ of $\ce{H2O}$.
@step Ammonia: $\dfrac{-902.0}{4} = -225.5\ \text{kJ/mol}$; oxygen: $\dfrac{-902.0}{5} = -180.4\ \text{kJ/mol}$
@step Water: $\dfrac{-902.0}{6} = -150.3\ \text{kJ/mol}$
@answer $-225.5$, $-180.4$ and $-150.3\ \text{kJ/mol}$: one reaction, but a different molar enthalpy for each substance.
:::

Going the other way, multiply a molar enthalpy by the coefficient to get $\Delta_r H$ for the equation.

:::example From a molar enthalpy to ΔrH
The molar enthalpy of combustion of octane is $-5074.1\ \text{kJ/mol}$ (with water vapour as a product). Write the thermochemical equation $\ce{2C8H18(l) + 25O2(g) -> 16CO2(g) + 18H2O(g)}$ with $\Delta_r H$ beside it and with an energy term.
@step The equation burns $2\ \text{mol}$ of octane: $\Delta_r H = 2 \times (-5074.1) = -10\,148.2\ \text{kJ}$
@step The reaction is exothermic, so as a term the energy goes with the products.
@answer $\ce{2C8H18(l) + 25O2(g) -> 16CO2(g) + 18H2O(g)}\qquad \Delta_r H = -10\,148.2\ \text{kJ}$, or $\ce{2C8H18(l) + 25O2(g) -> 16CO2(g) + 18H2O(g)} + 10\,148.2\ \text{kJ}$
:::

## Enthalpy diagrams

:::definition Enthalpy diagram
An **enthalpy diagram** shows the enthalpy of the reactants and of the products as two horizontal lines on a vertical enthalpy axis, with an arrow from the reactants to the products labelled with $\Delta H$.
:::

The absolute heights are not known, since enthalpy itself cannot be measured; only the difference between the two lines matters. In an exothermic reaction the products are **lower** than the reactants, and the arrow points down:

To draw one: write the reactants on one line and the products on the other, put the lower-enthalpy side lower, draw the arrow from the reactants to the products, and label it with $\Delta H$ and its sign.

:::example Reading a diagram
An enthalpy diagram shows $\ce{2H2O(l)}$ on the lower line and $\ce{2H2(g) + O2(g)}$ on the upper line, with an arrow from the lower line up to the upper one labelled $571.6\ \text{kJ}$. Write the thermochemical equation with $\Delta_r H$, and say whether the reaction is exothermic or endothermic.
@step The arrow starts at the reactants: $\ce{2H2O(l)}$. It ends at the products: $\ce{2H2(g) + O2(g)}$.
@step The products are higher, so the system gains $571.6\ \text{kJ}$: $\Delta_r H$ is positive.
@answer $\ce{2H2O(l) -> 2H2(g) + O2(g)}\qquad \Delta_r H = +571.6\ \text{kJ}$: endothermic.
:::

A diagram, like an equation, gives $\Delta H$ for the amounts written on it. To find the enthalpy change for some other amount, find the molar enthalpy for the substance first.

:::example A diagram and a mass
Using the diagram in the example above, find the enthalpy change when $9.00\ \text{g}$ of water decomposes. $M(\ce{H2O}) = 18.02\ \text{g/mol}$.
@step The diagram is for $2\ \text{mol}$ of $\ce{H2O}$: $\Delta_r H_m = \dfrac{+571.6}{2} = +285.8\ \text{kJ/mol}$
@step $n = \dfrac{9.00}{18.02} = 0.4994\ \text{mol}$
@step $\Delta H = 0.4994 \times (+285.8) = +142.7\ \text{kJ}$
@answer $\Delta H = +143\ \text{kJ}$ (to three significant figures).
:::

:::example All four ways for photosynthesis
In a leaf, photosynthesis stores the energy of sunlight: $\ce{6CO2(g) + 6H2O(l) -> C6H12O6(s) + 6O2(g)}\qquad \Delta_r H = +2802.5\ \text{kJ}$. Write the energy change as an energy term, as the molar enthalpy per mole of glucose formed and per mole of carbon dioxide used, and describe its enthalpy diagram.
@step $\Delta_r H$ is positive, so the energy is absorbed and goes with the reactants: $\ce{6CO2(g) + 6H2O(l)} + 2802.5\ \text{kJ} \mathrel{\ce{->}} \ce{C6H12O6(s) + 6O2(g)}$
@step One mole of glucose forms: $\Delta_r H_m = \dfrac{+2802.5}{1} = +2802.5\ \text{kJ/mol}$ of glucose.
@step Six moles of carbon dioxide are used: $\Delta_r H_m = \dfrac{+2802.5}{6} = +467.1\ \text{kJ/mol}$ of carbon dioxide.
@answer On the enthalpy diagram, $\ce{6CO2(g) + 6H2O(l)}$ is the lower line and $\ce{C6H12O6(s) + 6O2(g)}$ the upper line, with the arrow pointing up, labelled $\Delta H = +2802.5\ \text{kJ}$.
:::

:::mistake An enthalpy diagram has no hump
An enthalpy diagram shows only the starting and finishing enthalpies. A diagram with a hump between the reactants and the products, plotted against the progress of the reaction, is a **potential energy diagram**; it also shows the activation energy, and you will use it in lesson 1.7.
:::

:::summary
- An energy change can be communicated as an **energy term** in the equation, as **$\Delta_r H$** beside it, as a **molar enthalpy**, or with an **enthalpy diagram**.
- Exothermic: the energy term is with the products and $\Delta_r H$ is negative. Endothermic: the energy term is with the reactants and $\Delta_r H$ is positive. Energy terms carry no minus sign.
- $\Delta_r H$ is for the equation as written. $\Delta_r H_m = \dfrac{\Delta_r H}{\text{coefficient}}$ for each substance, and $\Delta_r H = \text{coefficient} \times \Delta_r H_m$.
- On an enthalpy diagram the arrow goes from the reactants to the products: down for exothermic, up for endothermic, labelled with $\Delta H$.
:::
