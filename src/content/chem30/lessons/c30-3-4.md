Hand sanitiser, vinegar and nail-polish remover are all small organic molecules of two or three carbon atoms: ethanol, ethanoic acid and propanone. Each is a short carbon chain like ethane or propane, but with a small group of atoms attached that changes everything: how it smells, whether it dissolves in water, the temperature at which it boils, and how it reacts.

In this lesson you will identify the main families of organic compounds by their functional groups, name simple alcohols, haloalkanes and carboxylic acids, and use functional groups to explain boiling points and solubility.

## Functional groups

:::definition Functional group
A **functional group** is an atom or group of atoms in an organic molecule that gives it its typical properties and reactions. Compounds with the same functional group belong to the same family and behave alike.
:::

| Family | Functional group | Example | Name ending |
|---|---|---|---|
| alcohol | hydroxyl group, $\ce{-OH}$ | ethanol, $\ce{CH3-CH2-OH}$ | -ol |
| haloalkane | a halogen atom: $\ce{-F}$, $\ce{-Cl}$, $\ce{-Br}$, $\ce{-I}$ | chloroethane, $\ce{CH3-CH2Cl}$ | prefix fluoro-, chloro-, bromo-, iodo- |
| carboxylic acid | carboxyl group, $\ce{-COOH}$ | ethanoic acid, $\ce{CH3-COOH}$ | -oic acid |
| ester | $\ce{-COO-{}}$ between two carbon chains | methyl ethanoate, $\ce{CH3-COO-CH3}$ | -oate |
| aldehyde | $\ce{-CHO}$, a $\ce{C=O}$ at the end of the chain | ethanal, $\ce{CH3-CHO}$ | -al |
| ketone | $\ce{-CO-{}}$, a $\ce{C=O}$ inside the chain | propanone, $\ce{CH3-CO-CH3}$ | -one |
| ether | $\ce{-O-{}}$ between two carbon chains | methoxymethane, $\ce{CH3-O-CH3}$ | prefix methoxy-, ethoxy- |
| amine | amino group, $\ce{-NH2}$ | methanamine, $\ce{CH3-NH2}$ | -amine |

:::definition Hydroxyl group
The **hydroxyl group**, $\ce{-OH}$, is an oxygen atom bonded to a hydrogen atom and to a carbon of the chain. It makes a compound an **alcohol**.
:::

:::definition Carboxyl group
The **carboxyl group**, $\ce{-COOH}$, is a carbon atom with a double bond to one oxygen and a single bond to an $\ce{-OH}$. It makes a compound a **carboxylic acid**: the hydrogen of its $\ce{-OH}$ can be given away, which makes it acidic.
:::

Several families contain oxygen, so look closely at how the oxygen is joined. A condensed formula writes the $\ce{C=O}$ group as $\ce{CO}$: $\ce{-CHO}$ at the end of a chain is an aldehyde, $\ce{-CO-{}}$ inside a chain is a ketone, $\ce{-COOH}$ is an acid, and $\ce{-COO-{}}$ with carbon on both sides is an ester.

:::example Spotting the family
Name the family of $\ce{CH3-CH2-CHO}$, $\ce{CH3-CO-CH2-CH3}$ and $\ce{CH3-CH2-COO-CH3}$.
@step $\ce{CH3-CH2-CHO}$ ends in $\ce{-CHO}$: a $\ce{C=O}$ at the end of the chain, so an aldehyde (propanal).
@step $\ce{CH3-CO-CH2-CH3}$ has $\ce{-CO-{}}$ with carbon on both sides: a ketone (butanone).
@step $\ce{CH3-CH2-COO-CH3}$ has $\ce{-COO-{}}$ with carbon on both sides: an ester (methyl propanoate).
@answer An aldehyde, a ketone and an ester.
:::

:::mistake An alcohol is not a hydroxide
The $\ce{-OH}$ of an alcohol is joined to carbon by a covalent bond. It is not a hydroxide ion, $\ce{OH-}$, and alcohols are not bases: a solution of ethanol is neutral. Compare a carboxylic acid, whose $\ce{-COOH}$ group can give away a hydrogen ion, making it a weak acid.
:::

## Naming alcohols, haloalkanes and acids

**Alcohols.** Take the name of the alkane with the same chain, drop the final -e and add **-ol**, with the number of the carbon that carries the $\ce{-OH}$ group: propan-1-ol and propan-2-ol. Number the chain from the end nearer the $\ce{-OH}$ group. Methanol and ethanol need no number. An older style puts the number first: 1-propanol.

**Haloalkanes.** A halogen atom is a substituent, named as a prefix like an alkyl group: fluoro, chloro, bromo, iodo. Number from the end nearer the first substituent, and list all the prefixes, halogens and alkyl groups together, in alphabetical order: 2-bromo-2-methylpropane.

**Carboxylic acids.** Drop the -e of the alkane and add **-oic acid**. The carbon of the $\ce{-COOH}$ group is part of the chain and is always carbon 1, so it needs no number: $\ce{CH3-CH2-COOH}$ is propanoic acid.

:::example An alcohol
Name $\ce{CH3-CH2-CH(OH)-CH3}$.
@step The chain holding the $\ce{-OH}$ carbon has 4 carbons: butane becomes butanol.
@step Number from the right, nearer the $\ce{-OH}$ group: it is on carbon 2.
@answer butan-2-ol (or 2-butanol)
:::

:::example A haloalkane
Name $\ce{CH2Cl-CH2Cl}$ and $\ce{CH3-CBr(CH3)-CH3}$.
@step $\ce{CH2Cl-CH2Cl}$: a two-carbon chain with a chlorine atom on each carbon: 1,2-dichloroethane.
@step $\ce{CH3-CBr(CH3)-CH3}$: a three-carbon chain with a bromine atom and a methyl group both on carbon 2. Bromo comes before methyl in the alphabet.
@answer 1,2-dichloroethane and 2-bromo-2-methylpropane.
:::

:::example A carboxylic acid
Name $\ce{CH3-CH(CH3)-CH2-COOH}$.
@step Count the chain from the carbon of the $\ce{-COOH}$ group, which is carbon 1: $\ce{COOH}$, $\ce{CH2}$, $\ce{CH}$, $\ce{CH3}$ is 4 carbons, so butanoic acid.
@step The methyl branch is on carbon 3.
@answer 3-methylbutanoic acid
:::

:::mistake Leaving out the carboxyl carbon
In $\ce{CH3-CH2-COOH}$ there are three carbons, not two: the carbon of $\ce{-COOH}$ counts. The acid is propanoic acid, not ethanoic acid. In the same way, the carbon of $\ce{-COOH}$ is carbon 1, so the branch in $\ce{CH3-CH(CH3)-COOH}$ is on carbon 2: 2-methylpropanoic acid.
:::

## Functional groups and physical properties

Boiling points and solubility depend on the forces between molecules, which you met in Grade 11:

- **London dispersion forces** act between all molecules, and are stronger for larger molecules.
- **Dipole–dipole forces** act between polar molecules, such as those with a $\ce{C=O}$ group or a halogen atom.
- **Hydrogen bonds** form when a hydrogen atom bonded to oxygen or nitrogen is attracted to an oxygen or nitrogen atom of another molecule. Alcohols, carboxylic acids and amines can hydrogen bond to each other.

To see the effect of the functional group alone, compare molecules of about the same size, so that their dispersion forces are about the same:

| Compound | Family | Molar mass (g/mol) | Boiling point (approximate) |
|---|---|---|---|
| butane, $\ce{CH3-CH2-CH2-CH3}$ | alkane | 58.14 | $-1^\circ\text{C}$ |
| methoxyethane, $\ce{CH3-O-CH2-CH3}$ | ether | 60.11 | $7^\circ\text{C}$ |
| propanal, $\ce{CH3-CH2-CHO}$ | aldehyde | 58.09 | $49^\circ\text{C}$ |
| propanone, $\ce{CH3-CO-CH3}$ | ketone | 58.09 | $56^\circ\text{C}$ |
| propan-1-ol, $\ce{CH3-CH2-CH2-OH}$ | alcohol | 60.11 | $97^\circ\text{C}$ |
| ethanoic acid, $\ce{CH3-COOH}$ | carboxylic acid | 60.06 | $118^\circ\text{C}$ |

The alkane has only dispersion forces. The ether, aldehyde and ketone are polar, so dipole–dipole forces raise their boiling points. The alcohol and the acid can hydrogen bond, and the acid most strongly of all: pairs of acid molecules hold together by two hydrogen bonds.

**Solubility in water** follows the same idea: like dissolves like. Small molecules that can hydrogen bond with water, such as methanol, ethanol and ethanoic acid, mix with water in any proportion. Hydrocarbons and haloalkanes cannot hydrogen bond with water and hardly dissolve. Along a family, a longer hydrocarbon chain makes a compound less soluble: butan-1-ol dissolves only partly, and hexan-1-ol barely at all.

:::example Comparing boiling points
Propan-1-ol and butane have similar molar masses. Which has the higher boiling point, and why?
@step Their London dispersion forces are similar, because the molecules are about the same size.
@step Propan-1-ol has an $\ce{-OH}$ group, so its molecules hydrogen bond to each other. Butane molecules have only dispersion forces.
@answer Propan-1-ol, about $97^\circ\text{C}$ against about $-1^\circ\text{C}$ for butane: more energy is needed to separate molecules held by hydrogen bonds.
:::

:::summary
- A **functional group** gives a family its properties: alcohol $\ce{-OH}$, haloalkane (a halogen), carboxylic acid $\ce{-COOH}$, ester $\ce{-COO-{}}$, aldehyde $\ce{-CHO}$, ketone $\ce{-CO-{}}$, ether $\ce{-O-{}}$, amine $\ce{-NH2}$.
- Alcohols: -ol with the number of the $\ce{-OH}$ carbon (propan-2-ol). Haloalkanes: fluoro, chloro, bromo, iodo prefixes in alphabetical order (2-bromo-2-methylpropane). Acids: -oic acid, with the $\ce{-COOH}$ carbon as carbon 1 (propanoic acid).
- For molecules of similar size, boiling points rise from alkanes, to polar ethers, aldehydes and ketones, to alcohols and carboxylic acids, which hydrogen bond.
- Small alcohols and acids dissolve in water because they hydrogen bond with it; hydrocarbons and haloalkanes do not. Longer chains make a compound less soluble.
:::
