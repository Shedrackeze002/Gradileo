Strips of paper soaked in cobalt(II) chloride solution are used as simple humidity indicators: they are blue in dry air and turn pink in damp air. Behind the colour change is an equilibrium between a pink and a blue cobalt ion, and the paper simply responds to the amount of water around it. Every equilibrium responds to changes in its conditions, and one principle predicts how.

In this lesson you will use Le Châtelier's principle to predict how an equilibrium shifts when a concentration, the volume or pressure, or the temperature changes, and you will see why only a change in temperature changes the equilibrium constant, and what a catalyst does.

## Le Châtelier's principle

A system at equilibrium is balanced: the forward and reverse rates are equal. Disturb it, by adding a substance, squeezing it or heating it, and the balance is lost for a while. The system reacts until it reaches a new equilibrium.

:::definition Le Châtelier's principle
When a system at equilibrium is subjected to a change in conditions (a **stress**), the system **shifts** in the direction that partly counteracts the change, until a new equilibrium is reached.
:::

:::definition Shift
A **shift to the right** means the forward reaction runs faster for a while, making more products. A **shift to the left** means the reverse reaction runs faster, making more reactants.
:::

The word *partly* matters: the system never undoes the whole change. Add more of a reactant, and some of it is used up, but not all of it.

## Changing a concentration

Adding a reactant speeds up the forward reaction, so the system shifts **right**, using up some of what was added. Removing a product slows the reverse reaction, so the system also shifts **right**, replacing some of what was removed. Adding a product or removing a reactant shifts the system **left**.

- At $20\ \text{s}$, $[\ce{H2}]$ jumps from $0.10$ to $0.30\ \text{mol/L}$: the stress.
- The system shifts right. $[\ce{H2}]$ falls, but only to about $0.25\ \text{mol/L}$, still higher than before: the change is only partly undone.
- $[\ce{I2}]$ falls to about $0.05\ \text{mol/L}$, because iodine reacts with the extra hydrogen, and $[\ce{HI}]$ rises to about $0.80\ \text{mol/L}$.

Why does the system shift? Just after the hydrogen is added, the ratio in the expression, called the **reaction quotient** $Q$, is no longer equal to $K_c$:
$$Q = \dfrac{[\ce{HI}]^2}{[\ce{H2}][\ce{I2}]} = \dfrac{(0.70)^2}{(0.30)(0.10)} = 16.3$$
$Q$ is less than $K_c = 49.0$, so the forward reaction runs faster until the ratio is back at 49.0: with the new concentrations, $\dfrac{(0.797)^2}{(0.252)(0.0515)} \approx 49$.

:::example Removing a product
The system $\ce{N2(g) + 3H2(g) <=> 2NH3(g)}$ is at equilibrium. Some ammonia is removed at constant volume. How does the equilibrium respond, and what happens to $[\ce{H2}]$?
@step Removing a product lowers $[\ce{NH3}]$; the system shifts to replace some of it.
@step A shift to the right uses up nitrogen and hydrogen.
@answer The equilibrium shifts to the right, and $[\ce{H2}]$ decreases.
:::

:::example A colour change
Iron(III) ions and thiocyanate ions form a blood-red complex ion: $\ce{Fe^3+(aq) + SCN^-(aq) <=> FeSCN^2+(aq)}$. A few crystals of potassium thiocyanate are dissolved in the mixture. What happens to the colour?
@step $\ce{KSCN}$ dissolves, adding $\ce{SCN-}$ ions, a reactant. ($\ce{K+}$ ions take no part.)
@step The system shifts to the right, using up some of the added $\ce{SCN-}$ and making more $\ce{FeSCN^2+}$.
@answer The red colour gets darker.
:::

## Changing the volume or pressure

Squeezing a mixture of gases into a smaller volume raises the pressure. The system responds by shifting towards the side with **fewer gas molecules**, which lowers the pressure again. Increasing the volume lowers the pressure, and the system shifts towards the side with more gas molecules.

Count only the gases, using the coefficients:

| Equilibrium | Moles of gas: left → right | Smaller volume shifts it |
|---|---|---|
| $\ce{N2(g) + 3H2(g) <=> 2NH3(g)}$ | 4 → 2 | right |
| $\ce{N2O4(g) <=> 2NO2(g)}$ | 1 → 2 | left |
| $\ce{CaCO3(s) <=> CaO(s) + CO2(g)}$ | 0 → 1 | left |
| $\ce{H2(g) + I2(g) <=> 2HI(g)}$ | 2 → 2 | no shift |

:::example Squeezing a brown gas
The system $\ce{N2O4(g) <=> 2NO2(g)}$ is at equilibrium in a syringe. The plunger is pushed in, halving the volume. How does the equilibrium respond?
@step There is 1 mole of gas on the left and 2 on the right.
@step A smaller volume favours the side with fewer gas molecules: the left.
@answer The equilibrium shifts to the left, so some brown $\ce{NO2}$ turns into colourless $\ce{N2O4}$. (The mixture first looks darker, because the same gas is squeezed into half the space, and then fades a little as the shift happens.)
:::

:::mistake Adding a gas that does not react
Adding argon to a sealed flask of fixed volume raises the total pressure, but it does not change the concentration of any gas in the equilibrium, so there is **no shift**. Only a change in volume changes the concentrations of all the gases at once. In the same way, adding more of a pure solid or liquid changes no concentration, so it causes no shift.
:::

:::

## Changing the temperature

To predict the effect of temperature, treat energy as if it were a reactant or a product. An exothermic forward reaction ($\Delta H < 0$) gives out energy, so energy is a product; an endothermic forward reaction ($\Delta H > 0$) takes energy in, so energy is a reactant.

$$\ce{N2(g) + 3H2(g) <=> 2NH3(g)} + \text{energy} \qquad \Delta H = -92\ \text{kJ}$$

**Raising** the temperature adds energy, so the system shifts in the direction that uses energy up: the **endothermic** direction. **Lowering** the temperature shifts it in the **exothermic** direction.

:::example Heating an endothermic system
For $\ce{N2O4(g) <=> 2NO2(g)}$, $\Delta H = +57\ \text{kJ}$. The flask is placed in hot water. How does the equilibrium respond, and what happens to the colour?
@step $\Delta H$ is positive, so the forward reaction is endothermic: energy $+ \ce{N2O4} \rightleftharpoons 2\ce{NO2}$.
@step Raising the temperature adds energy, so the system shifts to the right to use it up.
@answer The equilibrium shifts to the right: more brown $\ce{NO2}$ forms and the colour darkens.
:::

:::

## Temperature, K and catalysts

After a change in concentration or volume, the system shifts until the ratio in the expression is back at the **same** value of $K_c$, as the hydrogen iodide graph showed. A change in temperature is different: it changes the value of $K_c$ itself.

- If heating shifts an equilibrium to the **right**, there are more products and fewer reactants at the new equilibrium, so $K_c$ **increases**. This happens for endothermic reactions.
- If heating shifts it to the **left**, $K_c$ **decreases**. This happens for exothermic reactions.

:::definition Catalysts and equilibrium
A **catalyst** lowers the activation energy of the forward and the reverse reactions by the same amount, so it speeds both up equally. A system reaches equilibrium sooner, but the equilibrium position and the value of $K_c$ do not change.
:::

| Change | Shift | Effect on $K_c$ |
|---|---|---|
| Add a reactant, or remove a product | right | none |
| Add a product, or remove a reactant | left | none |
| Decrease the volume (increase the pressure) | towards fewer gas molecules | none |
| Add a non-reacting gas at constant volume | none | none |
| Raise the temperature | endothermic direction | changes |
| Add a catalyst | none | none |

:::example K at different temperatures
For $\ce{2SO2(g) + O2(g) <=> 2SO3(g)}$, $K_c$ is $2.5 \times 10^{3}$ at $300^\circ\text{C}$ and $34$ at $500^\circ\text{C}$ (values for this example). Is the forward reaction exothermic or endothermic?
@step $K_c$ gets smaller as the temperature rises, so heating shifts the equilibrium to the left.
@step Heating favours the endothermic direction, so the reverse reaction is endothermic.
@answer The forward reaction is exothermic. A larger yield of $\ce{SO3}$ needs a lower temperature.
:::

:::note Compromise in industry
For an exothermic reaction such as the synthesis of ammonia, a low temperature gives a larger yield at equilibrium, but the reaction is then too slow to be useful. Manufacturers choose a moderate temperature, a high pressure (which favours the side with fewer gas molecules) and a catalyst (which makes equilibrium arrive sooner), and they remove the ammonia as it forms, which shifts the equilibrium to the right.
:::

:::summary
- **Le Châtelier's principle**: a system at equilibrium shifts to partly counteract a stress, until a new equilibrium is reached.
- Adding a reactant or removing a product shifts the equilibrium **right**; adding a product or removing a reactant shifts it **left**.
- A smaller volume (higher pressure) shifts it towards the side with **fewer gas molecules**; equal numbers of gas molecules on each side means no shift. A non-reacting gas at constant volume, or more of a pure solid or liquid, causes no shift.
- Treat energy as a reactant (endothermic) or a product (exothermic): raising the temperature shifts the equilibrium in the endothermic direction.
- Only a change in **temperature** changes $K_c$: it increases with temperature for an endothermic reaction and decreases for an exothermic one.
- A **catalyst** speeds up both reactions equally: equilibrium arrives sooner, but its position and $K_c$ do not change.
:::
