How much iron is in an iron supplement tablet, or how strong is a bottle of hydrogen peroxide disinfectant? A quality-control lab can answer both with one deep purple solution, potassium permanganate. Added drop by drop from a burette, it reacts with the iron(II) ions or the hydrogen peroxide until they are used up, and the volume it takes gives the amount.

In this lesson you will use balanced redox equations and titration data to find amounts and concentrations, and see why a permanganate titration needs no indicator.

## Redox titrations

A redox titration works like the acid–base titrations of Grade 11 Chemistry, with the same burette, pipette and conical flask. Only the reaction is different: electrons are transferred instead of hydrogen ions.

:::definition Redox titration
A **redox titration** finds the amount of an oxidising or reducing agent in a sample by reacting it with a solution of a reducing or oxidising agent of known concentration.
:::

:::definition Titrant
The **titrant** is the solution added from the burette. It is a standard solution, whose concentration is known precisely. Potassium permanganate, $\ce{KMnO4}$, and potassium dichromate, $\ce{K2Cr2O7}$, are common oxidising titrants.
:::

:::definition Endpoint
The **endpoint** is the moment a colour change shows that the reaction is complete. A good titration has its endpoint at the equivalence point, when the titrant added has reacted exactly with the sample in the ratio of the balanced equation.
:::

Permanganate is a strong oxidising agent only in acidic solution, where it is reduced to manganese(II) ions:

$$\ce{MnO4^-(aq) + 8H+(aq) + 5e- -> Mn^2+(aq) + 4H2O(l)}$$

So the sample is acidified first, usually with dilute sulfuric acid. Hydrochloric acid is avoided, because permanganate would oxidise its chloride ions to chlorine and use up extra titrant.

## Why permanganate needs no indicator

Permanganate ions are a deep purple, while the manganese(II) ions they become are almost colourless. As the titrant runs into the flask, each drop meets reducing agent, reacts, and loses its colour at once. When the last of the reducing agent has reacted, the next drop has nothing to react with: its purple colour stays, and the whole solution turns a pale pink that lasts after swirling. That first lasting pink is the endpoint, so permanganate is its own indicator.

:::note Dichromate needs help
Dichromate ions are orange and chromium(III) ions are green, but the change from orange to green is gradual and hard to see. Dichromate titrations use a redox indicator, such as diphenylamine sulfonate, which turns violet at the endpoint.
:::

## From titration data to a concentration

The calculation is solution stoichiometry, just as for acid–base titrations:

$$c(\text{titrant}),\ V(\text{titrant}) \xrightarrow{\ n = cV\ } n(\text{titrant}) \xrightarrow{\ \text{mole ratio}\ } n(\text{sample}) \xrightarrow{\ c = n/V\ } c(\text{sample})$$

The mole ratio comes from the balanced redox equation, so balance it first, with half-reactions if necessary (lesson 2.4).

:::example Iron(II) ions
A $25.00\ \text{mL}$ sample of iron(II) sulfate solution is acidified and titrated with $0.0200\ \text{mol/L}$ potassium permanganate: $\ce{MnO4^- + 5Fe^2+ + 8H+ -> Mn^2+ + 5Fe^3+ + 4H2O}$. The endpoint is reached after $18.40\ \text{mL}$. What is the concentration of the iron(II) ions, to three significant figures?
@step $n(\ce{MnO4^-}) = cV = 0.0200 \times 0.01840 = 3.680 \times 10^{-4}\ \text{mol}$
@step $n(\ce{Fe^2+}) = 3.680 \times 10^{-4} \times \dfrac{5}{1} = 1.840 \times 10^{-3}\ \text{mol}$
@step $c = \dfrac{n}{V} = \dfrac{1.840 \times 10^{-3}}{0.02500} = 0.0736\ \text{mol/L}$
@answer $[\ce{Fe^2+}] = 0.0736\ \text{mol/L}$
:::

:::mistake Turning the mole ratio upside down
Each permanganate ion reacts with **five** iron(II) ions, so there are more moles of iron(II) than of permanganate. Dividing by 5 instead of multiplying gives $7.36 \times 10^{-5}$ mol of iron(II) and a concentration 25 times too small. Write the ratio as $\dfrac{\text{wanted coefficient}}{\text{given coefficient}}$.
:::

:::example Hydrogen peroxide
A $10.00\ \text{mL}$ sample of hydrogen peroxide solution is acidified and titrated with $0.0200\ \text{mol/L}$ potassium permanganate: $\ce{2MnO4^- + 5H2O2 + 6H+ -> 2Mn^2+ + 5O2 + 8H2O}$. The endpoint is reached after $17.30\ \text{mL}$. What is the concentration of the hydrogen peroxide?
@step $n(\ce{MnO4^-}) = 0.0200 \times 0.01730 = 3.460 \times 10^{-4}\ \text{mol}$
@step $n(\ce{H2O2}) = 3.460 \times 10^{-4} \times \dfrac{5}{2} = 8.650 \times 10^{-4}\ \text{mol}$
@step $c = \dfrac{8.650 \times 10^{-4}}{0.01000} = 0.0865\ \text{mol/L}$
@answer $[\ce{H2O2}] = 0.0865\ \text{mol/L}$
:::

## Several trials and a solid sample

As in any titration, the first trial is a quick, rough one, and careful trials are repeated until three agree within $0.20\ \text{mL}$. The volume used in each trial is the final burette reading minus the initial reading. To analyse a solid, such as an ore, a weighed sample is dissolved and every atom of the element is turned into the ion that reacts.

:::example Iron in an ore
A $0.500\ \text{g}$ sample of iron ore is dissolved in acid, and all of its iron is turned into $\ce{Fe^2+}$ ions. The solution is titrated with $0.0200\ \text{mol/L}$ potassium permanganate: $\ce{MnO4^- + 5Fe^2+ + 8H+ -> Mn^2+ + 5Fe^3+ + 4H2O}$. What is the percent by mass of iron in the ore, to the nearest tenth? Use the molar mass Fe $55.85\ \text{g/mol}$.

| Trial | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Final reading (mL) | 24.10 | 23.75 | 24.20 | 23.40 |
| Initial reading (mL) | 0.50 | 1.05 | 1.45 | 0.75 |
@step Volumes used: $23.60$, $22.70$, $22.75$ and $22.65\ \text{mL}$. Trial 1 is the rough trial; trials 2 to 4 agree, so the average is $\dfrac{22.70 + 22.75 + 22.65}{3} = 22.70\ \text{mL}$.
@step $n(\ce{MnO4^-}) = 0.0200 \times 0.02270 = 4.540 \times 10^{-4}\ \text{mol}$
@step $n(\ce{Fe^2+}) = 4.540 \times 10^{-4} \times 5 = 2.270 \times 10^{-3}\ \text{mol}$
@step $m(\ce{Fe}) = 2.270 \times 10^{-3} \times 55.85 = 0.1268\ \text{g}$
@step Percent by mass: $\dfrac{0.1268}{0.500} \times 100\% = 25.4\%$
@answer The ore is $25.4\%$ iron by mass.
:::

:::tip Diluted samples
A concentrated sample, such as a disinfectant, is often diluted before it is titrated. Find the concentration of the diluted solution first, then multiply by the dilution factor: diluting $10.00\ \text{mL}$ to $250.0\ \text{mL}$ makes the solution 25 times weaker, so the original was 25 times more concentrated.
:::

:::summary
- A **redox titration** finds an amount of an oxidising or reducing agent by titrating it with a standard solution, the **titrant**.
- Acidified **permanganate** is deep purple and is reduced to almost colourless $\ce{Mn^2+}$, so the **endpoint** is the first lasting pale pink: no indicator is needed. Sulfuric acid, not hydrochloric acid, is used to acidify.
- Calculation: $n = cV$ for the titrant, the mole ratio from the balanced redox equation, then $c = \dfrac{n}{V}$ for the sample.
- Average the trials that agree within $0.20\ \text{mL}$, leaving out the rough trial; for a solid, change the amount to a mass and a percent by mass.
:::
