Copper pipes can carry water for decades, but a zinc coating on a steel bucket slowly dissolves away, and gold stays bright for thousands of years. Some redox reactions happen on their own as soon as the reactants meet, and others do not happen at all. A table of reduction half-reactions, ranked from the strongest oxidising agent down, lets you predict which is which.

In this lesson you will use such a table to find the strongest oxidising and reducing agents in a mixture, and to predict whether a redox reaction is spontaneous.

## Comparing oxidising agents

A classic experiment compares two metals and their ions. A copper wire placed in colourless silver nitrate solution grows needles of silver, and the solution turns blue as copper(II) ions form: $\ce{Cu(s) + 2Ag^+(aq) -> Cu^2+(aq) + 2Ag(s)}$. A silver wire placed in blue copper(II) nitrate solution shows no change at all.

So silver ions can take electrons from copper atoms, but copper(II) ions cannot take electrons from silver atoms. Silver ions are the **stronger oxidising agent**, and copper atoms are the **stronger reducing agent**. Testing many pairs in this way puts oxidising agents in order of strength.

:::definition Spontaneous reaction
A **spontaneous** reaction happens on its own once the reactants are mixed, with no continuous supply of energy from outside. A non-spontaneous reaction does not happen unless energy is put in.
:::

## The redox table

A **redox table** lists reduction half-reactions in order of the strength of their oxidising agents. Each has a standard reduction potential, $E^\circ$, in volts: the higher the value, the stronger the oxidising agent. Lesson 2.7 explains where the values come from; for now, only the order matters.

| Reduction half-reaction | $E^\circ$ (V) |
|---|---|
| $\ce{F2(g) + 2e- -> 2F^-(aq)}$ | $+2.87$ |
| $\ce{Cl2(g) + 2e- -> 2Cl^-(aq)}$ | $+1.36$ |
| $\ce{O2(g) + 4H^+(aq) + 4e- -> 2H2O(l)}$ | $+1.23$ |
| $\ce{Br2(l) + 2e- -> 2Br^-(aq)}$ | $+1.07$ |
| $\ce{Ag^+(aq) + e- -> Ag(s)}$ | $+0.80$ |
| $\ce{Fe^3+(aq) + e- -> Fe^2+(aq)}$ | $+0.77$ |
| $\ce{I2(s) + 2e- -> 2I^-(aq)}$ | $+0.54$ |
| $\ce{Cu^2+(aq) + 2e- -> Cu(s)}$ | $+0.34$ |
| $\ce{2H^+(aq) + 2e- -> H2(g)}$ | $0.00$ |
| $\ce{Pb^2+(aq) + 2e- -> Pb(s)}$ | $-0.13$ |
| $\ce{Ni^2+(aq) + 2e- -> Ni(s)}$ | $-0.26$ |
| $\ce{Fe^2+(aq) + 2e- -> Fe(s)}$ | $-0.45$ |
| $\ce{Zn^2+(aq) + 2e- -> Zn(s)}$ | $-0.76$ |
| $\ce{2H2O(l) + 2e- -> H2(g) + 2OH^-(aq)}$ | $-0.83$ |
| $\ce{Al^3+(aq) + 3e- -> Al(s)}$ | $-1.66$ |
| $\ce{Mg^2+(aq) + 2e- -> Mg(s)}$ | $-2.37$ |
| $\ce{Na^+(aq) + e- -> Na(s)}$ | $-2.71$ |
| $\ce{Li^+(aq) + e- -> Li(s)}$ | $-3.04$ |

The values are standard reduction potentials at $25^\circ\text{C}$, to two decimal places; every question gives the part of the table it needs.

:::definition Strongest oxidising agent and strongest reducing agent
Oxidising agents are on the **left** of the half-reactions; the **strongest oxidising agent** is the highest one, here $\ce{F2}$. Reducing agents are on the **right**; the **strongest reducing agent** is the lowest one, here $\ce{Li}$.
:::

Strength runs in opposite directions on the two sides. Fluorine is the strongest oxidising agent, and fluoride ions, beside it, are the weakest reducing agent: they hold their electrons so tightly that almost nothing can take them.

:::example The strongest oxidising agent
Which is the strongest oxidising agent: $\ce{Zn^2+}$, $\ce{Ag^+}$, $\ce{Cu^2+}$ or $\ce{Ni^2+}$?
@step All four are on the left of the table, so all four are oxidising agents.
@step In order from the top: $\ce{Ag^+}$ ($+0.80$ V), $\ce{Cu^2+}$ ($+0.34$ V), $\ce{Ni^2+}$ ($-0.26$ V), $\ce{Zn^2+}$ ($-0.76$ V).
@answer $\ce{Ag^+}$, the highest in the table.
:::

## Spontaneous or not

A reaction needs an oxidising agent to take electrons and a reducing agent to give them. Whether it happens depends on where the two are in the table.

:::definition Predicting spontaneity
A redox reaction is **spontaneous** when the oxidising agent is **above** the reducing agent in the table. When the oxidising agent is below the reducing agent, no reaction occurs.
:::

:::example Copper in silver nitrate
Will copper react spontaneously with silver ions?
@step The oxidising agent is $\ce{Ag^+}$ ($+0.80$ V); the reducing agent is $\ce{Cu}$ ($+0.34$ V).
@step $\ce{Ag^+}$ is above $\ce{Cu}$, so the reaction is spontaneous.
@step Combine the half-reactions: $\ce{Cu(s) -> Cu^2+(aq) + 2e-}$ and $2 \times [\ce{Ag^+(aq) + e- -> Ag(s)}]$.
@answer Yes: $\ce{Cu(s) + 2Ag^+(aq) -> Cu^2+(aq) + 2Ag(s)}$, as the experiment showed.
:::

:::example Copper in hydrochloric acid
Will copper react with hydrochloric acid, which contains $\ce{H^+(aq)}$ and $\ce{Cl^-(aq)}$?
@step The oxidising agent is $\ce{H^+}$ ($0.00$ V); the reducing agent is $\ce{Cu}$ ($+0.34$ V).
@step $\ce{H^+}$ is below $\ce{Cu}$.
@answer No reaction: copper does not dissolve in hydrochloric acid. Metals below hydrogen in the table, such as zinc and iron, do react with acids, releasing hydrogen gas.
:::

:::mistake Two oxidising agents cannot react
"Will $\ce{Zn^2+}$ react with $\ce{Cu^2+}$?" Both are oxidising agents: each can only take electrons, and neither has electrons to give. A redox reaction always needs one oxidising agent and one reducing agent.
:::

## Mixtures

A beaker can hold several species at once. To predict what happens:

1. **List every species** present: atoms, molecules and the separate ions of ionic compounds. In an aqueous solution, include water.
2. **Label** each one as an oxidising agent (on the left of the table), a reducing agent (on the right), or both.
3. **Find the strongest oxidising agent** (highest on the left) and the **strongest reducing agent** (lowest on the right).
4. If the strongest oxidising agent is above the strongest reducing agent, they react. Combine their half-reactions.

:::example A strip of nickel in a mixture
A strip of nickel is placed in a solution containing copper(II) ions and zinc ions; the nitrate ions with them do not react. What reaction occurs?
@step Species: $\ce{Ni(s)}$, $\ce{Cu^2+(aq)}$, $\ce{Zn^2+(aq)}$, $\ce{H2O(l)}$.
@step Oxidising agents: $\ce{Cu^2+}$ ($+0.34$ V), $\ce{Zn^2+}$ ($-0.76$ V) and $\ce{H2O}$ ($-0.83$ V). The strongest is $\ce{Cu^2+}$.
@step Reducing agents: $\ce{Ni}$ ($-0.26$ V) and $\ce{H2O}$, on the right of the oxygen half-reaction ($+1.23$ V). The strongest is $\ce{Ni}$, the lower one.
@step $\ce{Cu^2+}$ is above $\ce{Ni}$, so they react.
@answer $\ce{Ni(s) + Cu^2+(aq) -> Ni^2+(aq) + Cu(s)}$. The zinc ions stay in solution unchanged.
:::

Some species appear on both sides of the table. Water is on the left of $\ce{2H2O + 2e- -> H2 + 2OH-}$, as an oxidising agent, and on the right of the oxygen half-reaction, as a reducing agent. Iron(II) ions are on the left of $\ce{Fe^2+ + 2e- -> Fe}$ and on the right of $\ce{Fe^3+ + e- -> Fe^2+}$. Check both places before deciding.

:::note Water in the table
The reduction of water is low in the table, so only reducing agents below it, such as sodium and lithium, react with water to release hydrogen gas. (Magnesium and aluminium are below it too, but a thin layer of oxide on their surface makes them react only very slowly.) The oxidation of water is high in the table, so only strong oxidising agents, such as fluorine, can take electrons from it.
:::

:::summary
- A **redox table** lists reduction half-reactions in order: the higher a half-reaction, the stronger its oxidising agent.
- Oxidising agents are on the left; the **strongest oxidising agent** is the highest. Reducing agents are on the right; the **strongest reducing agent** is the lowest.
- A redox reaction is **spontaneous** when the oxidising agent is **above** the reducing agent. Otherwise no reaction occurs.
- In a mixture, list every species (including water), find the strongest oxidising and reducing agents, and check their positions.
- Water, iron(II) ions and tin(II) ions can act as oxidising agents and as reducing agents.
:::
