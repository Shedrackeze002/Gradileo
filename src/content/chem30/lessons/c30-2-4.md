Early breath-alcohol testers held crystals of orange potassium dichromate. When a driver breathed through the tube, any ethanol in the breath reduced the orange dichromate ions to green chromium(III) ions, and the further the green colour spread, the more alcohol there was. The reaction, in acidic solution, is

$$\ce{2Cr2O7^2- + 3C2H5OH + 16H+ -> 4Cr^3+ + 3CH3COOH + 11H2O}$$

No one finds coefficients like these by trial and error. In this lesson you will balance half-reactions in acidic solution with water, hydrogen ions and electrons, and combine them into balanced redox equations: the **half-reaction method**.

## Balancing a half-reaction

In acidic solution there is plenty of water and plenty of hydrogen ions, so either can be added to a half-reaction as needed. Follow these steps in order:

1. **Write the skeleton**: the reactant and product that contain the element changing oxidation number.
2. **Balance the atoms other than O and H**, with coefficients.
3. **Balance oxygen** by adding $\ce{H2O}$ to the side that needs oxygen.
4. **Balance hydrogen** by adding $\ce{H+}$ to the side that needs hydrogen.
5. **Balance charge** by adding electrons to the side with the greater (more positive) total charge.
6. **Check** that the atoms and the total charge are the same on both sides.

:::definition Half-reaction method
The **half-reaction method** balances a redox equation by balancing the oxidation and the reduction separately, then combining them so the electrons lost equal the electrons gained (the **electron transfer** is the same on both sides).
:::

:::example Permanganate to manganese(II)
Balance $\ce{MnO4^- -> Mn^2+}$ in acidic solution.
@step Manganese is already balanced: one on each side.
@step Oxygen: four O on the left, none on the right. Add $\ce{4H2O}$ to the right: $\ce{MnO4^- -> Mn^2+ + 4H2O}$.
@step Hydrogen: eight H on the right. Add $\ce{8H+}$ to the left: $\ce{MnO4^- + 8H+ -> Mn^2+ + 4H2O}$.
@step Charge: $-1 + 8 = +7$ on the left, $+2$ on the right. Add 5 electrons to the left: $+7 - 5 = +2$.
@answer $\ce{MnO4^- + 8H+ + 5e- -> Mn^2+ + 4H2O}$. Electrons are gained, so it is a reduction: manganese goes from $+7$ to $+2$.
:::

The number of electrons always matches the change in oxidation number: manganese drops by 5, and 5 electrons are gained. That makes a quick check.

:::example Dichromate to chromium(III)
Balance $\ce{Cr2O7^2- -> Cr^3+}$ in acidic solution.
@step Chromium: two on the left, so put 2 in front of $\ce{Cr^3+}$: $\ce{Cr2O7^2- -> 2Cr^3+}$.
@step Oxygen: add $\ce{7H2O}$ to the right: $\ce{Cr2O7^2- -> 2Cr^3+ + 7H2O}$.
@step Hydrogen: add $\ce{14H+}$ to the left: $\ce{Cr2O7^2- + 14H+ -> 2Cr^3+ + 7H2O}$.
@step Charge: $-2 + 14 = +12$ on the left, $2(+3) = +6$ on the right. Add 6 electrons to the left.
@answer $\ce{Cr2O7^2- + 14H+ + 6e- -> 2Cr^3+ + 7H2O}$. Each chromium goes from $+6$ to $+3$, and there are two of them: 6 electrons.
:::

Oxidations balance the same way; the electrons simply end up on the right. For sulfur dioxide becoming sulfate ions: $\ce{SO2 + 2H2O -> SO4^2- + 4H+ + 2e-}$.

:::mistake Balancing charge with hydrogen ions
Once water and hydrogen ions have balanced the atoms, the only thing left to add is electrons. Adding more $\ce{H+}$ to fix the charge unbalances the hydrogen again. And electrons go on the **more positive** side: they carry a negative charge, so they lower it.
:::

:::

## Combining half-reactions

Once both half-reactions are balanced:

7. **Multiply** each half-reaction so the electrons lost equal the electrons gained.
8. **Add** the two half-reactions.
9. **Cancel** what appears on both sides: all the electrons, and any $\ce{H+}$ and $\ce{H2O}$ that appear on both sides.
10. **Check** the atoms and the charge.

:::example Permanganate and iron(II)
Combine $\ce{Fe^2+ -> Fe^3+ + e-}$ and $\ce{MnO4^- + 8H+ + 5e- -> Mn^2+ + 4H2O}$ into a balanced equation.
@step The oxidation gives 1 electron and the reduction takes 5, so multiply the oxidation by 5: $\ce{5Fe^2+ -> 5Fe^3+ + 5e-}$.
@step Add: $\ce{MnO4^- + 5Fe^2+ + 8H+ + 5e- -> Mn^2+ + 5Fe^3+ + 4H2O + 5e-}$.
@step Cancel the five electrons.
@answer $\ce{MnO4^- + 5Fe^2+ + 8H+ -> Mn^2+ + 5Fe^3+ + 4H2O}$. Check the charge: $-1 + 10 + 8 = +17$ on the left and $2 + 15 = +17$ on the right.
:::

Water and hydrogen ions often appear on both sides after adding, and only the difference is kept. Here is the breath-tester reaction.

:::example Dichromate and ethanol
Balance $\ce{Cr2O7^2- + C2H5OH -> Cr^3+ + CH3COOH}$ in acidic solution.
@step Reduction: $\ce{Cr2O7^2- + 14H+ + 6e- -> 2Cr^3+ + 7H2O}$
@step Oxidation: carbon is balanced; add $\ce{H2O}$ to the left for oxygen, then $\ce{4H+}$ to the right, then 4 electrons to the right: $\ce{C2H5OH + H2O -> CH3COOH + 4H+ + 4e-}$.
@step The least common multiple of 6 and 4 is 12: multiply the reduction by 2 and the oxidation by 3.
@step Add: $\ce{2Cr2O7^2- + 3C2H5OH + 28H+ + 3H2O -> 4Cr^3+ + 3CH3COOH + 14H2O + 12H+}$ (the 12 electrons cancel).
@step Cancel: $28 - 12 = 16$ $\ce{H+}$ on the left, and $14 - 3 = 11$ $\ce{H2O}$ on the right.
@answer $\ce{2Cr2O7^2- + 3C2H5OH + 16H+ -> 4Cr^3+ + 3CH3COOH + 11H2O}$. Charge: $-4 + 16 = +12$ on both sides.
:::

:::mistake Forgetting to multiply the whole half-reaction
When a half-reaction is multiplied, every coefficient in it is multiplied: the water and hydrogen ions too, not just the electrons. Multiplying only the electrons gives an equation whose atoms do not balance.
:::

:::note Basic solutions
In a basic solution there are almost no hydrogen ions. Balance the equation as if it were acidic, then add as many $\ce{OH-}$ ions to both sides as there are $\ce{H+}$ ions; each $\ce{H+}$ and $\ce{OH-}$ together make $\ce{H2O}$. The questions in this unit use acidic solutions.
:::

:::

:::summary
- Balance a half-reaction in acidic solution in this order: atoms other than O and H, then O with $\ce{H2O}$, H with $\ce{H+}$, and charge with electrons on the more positive side.
- Electrons on the left mean a reduction; on the right, an oxidation. Their number equals the change in oxidation number.
- To combine half-reactions, multiply them so the electrons match, add them, and cancel the electrons and any $\ce{H+}$ and $\ce{H2O}$ that appear on both sides.
- Check the final equation: the same atoms and the same total charge on both sides.
:::
