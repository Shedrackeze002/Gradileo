Aluminium is the most common metal in the Earth's crust, yet in the mid-1800s it was rarer and more precious than gold, because no affordable reducing agent could pull it out of its ore. Since 1886 it has been made by electrolysis: an electric current forces aluminium ions to take electrons they would never take on their own. The same idea refines copper, makes chlorine and sodium hydroxide, and coats taps with chromium and cutlery with silver.

In this lesson you will predict the products of an electrolytic cell and the minimum voltage it needs, and use Faraday's law to relate the current, the time and the amount of product.

## Electrolytic cells

:::definition Electrolysis and electrolytic cell
**Electrolysis** is the use of electrical energy to drive a non-spontaneous redox reaction. An **electrolytic cell** is the arrangement in which it happens: two electrodes in an electrolyte, connected to a power supply.
:::

The names of the electrodes keep their meaning: oxidation at the anode, reduction at the cathode. What changes is the push. The power supply pumps electrons onto the cathode, making it negative, and pulls them from the anode, making it positive.

| | Voltaic cell | Electrolytic cell |
|---|---|---|
| Reaction | spontaneous | non-spontaneous |
| $E^\circ_\text{cell}$ | positive | negative |
| Energy | chemical to electrical | electrical to chemical |
| Anode | oxidation, negative | oxidation, positive |
| Cathode | reduction, positive | reduction, negative |

## Molten salts

In a molten ionic compound the only species are the metal ions and the non-metal ions. The cathode reduces the metal ions to metal, and the anode oxidises the anions.

:::example Molten sodium chloride
Molten sodium chloride is electrolysed with inert electrodes. What forms at each electrode, and what is the minimum voltage needed? Use $\ce{Na^+ + e- -> Na}$, $E^\circ = -2.71\ \text{V}$, and $\ce{Cl2 + 2e- -> 2Cl^-}$, $E^\circ = +1.36\ \text{V}$.
@step Cathode (reduction): $\ce{Na^+ + e- -> Na}$. Sodium metal forms.
@step Anode (oxidation): $\ce{2Cl^- -> Cl2 + 2e-}$. Chlorine gas forms.
@step $E^\circ_\text{cell} = E^\circ_\text{cathode} - E^\circ_\text{anode} = -2.71 - (+1.36) = -4.07\ \text{V}$
@answer Sodium at the cathode and chlorine at the anode. The cell potential is $-4.07\ \text{V}$, so at least $4.07\ \text{V}$ must be applied.
:::

:::definition Minimum voltage
The **minimum voltage** of an electrolytic cell is the size of its negative cell potential: the power supply must push at least that hard to make the reaction run.
:::

## Aqueous solutions

In a solution, water competes with the ions. Water can be reduced at the cathode, $\ce{2H2O(l) + 2e- -> H2(g) + 2OH^-(aq)}$, $E^\circ = -0.83\ \text{V}$, and oxidised at the anode, the reverse of $\ce{O2(g) + 4H+(aq) + 4e- -> 2H2O(l)}$, $E^\circ = +1.23\ \text{V}$.

- At the **cathode**, the **strongest oxidising agent** present (highest on the left of the table) is reduced: either the metal ion or water.
- At the **anode**, the **strongest reducing agent** present (lowest on the right) is oxidised: either the anion or water. Sulfate and nitrate ions are not oxidised.

:::example Potassium iodide solution
Aqueous potassium iodide is electrolysed with inert electrodes. Predict the products and the minimum voltage. Use $\ce{K^+ + e- -> K}$, $-2.93\ \text{V}$; water, $-0.83\ \text{V}$ and $+1.23\ \text{V}$; $\ce{I2 + 2e- -> 2I^-}$, $+0.54\ \text{V}$.
@step Cathode: water ($-0.83$ V) is a stronger oxidising agent than $\ce{K^+}$ ($-2.93$ V), so water is reduced: $\ce{2H2O + 2e- -> H2 + 2OH^-}$. Hydrogen gas forms, and the solution around the cathode becomes basic.
@step Anode: $\ce{I^-}$ ($+0.54$ V) is a stronger reducing agent than water ($+1.23$ V), so iodide ions are oxidised: $\ce{2I^- -> I2 + 2e-}$. The solution around the anode turns brown with iodine.
@step $E^\circ_\text{cell} = -0.83 - (+0.54) = -1.37\ \text{V}$
@answer Hydrogen at the cathode and iodine at the anode; at least $1.37\ \text{V}$ is needed.
:::

:::example Copper(II) sulfate solution
Aqueous copper(II) sulfate is electrolysed with inert electrodes. Predict the products and the minimum voltage, using $\ce{Cu^2+ + 2e- -> Cu}$, $+0.34\ \text{V}$, and the potentials of water.
@step Cathode: $\ce{Cu^2+}$ ($+0.34$ V) is a stronger oxidising agent than water ($-0.83$ V), so copper plates onto the cathode.
@step Anode: sulfate ions are not oxidised, so water is: $\ce{2H2O -> O2 + 4H+ + 4e-}$. Oxygen gas bubbles off.
@step $E^\circ_\text{cell} = +0.34 - (+1.23) = -0.89\ \text{V}$
@answer Copper at the cathode and oxygen at the anode; at least $0.89\ \text{V}$ is needed.
:::

:::note Chloride solutions
By the table, water ($+1.23$ V) should be oxidised before chloride ions ($+1.36$ V). In practice, concentrated chloride solutions release chlorine instead, because oxygen needs a noticeably higher voltage than its $E^\circ$ suggests at most electrodes. This is how chlorine is made from brine. The questions here follow the table.
:::

## Electroplating

:::definition Electroplating
**Electroplating** uses electrolysis to coat an object with a thin layer of metal. The object is the cathode, the plating metal is the anode, and the electrolyte contains ions of the plating metal.
:::

The silver anode is not inert: silver atoms are the strongest reducing agent present, so they are oxidised, $\ce{Ag(s) -> Ag^+(aq) + e-}$, replacing the silver ions that are reduced onto the spoon, $\ce{Ag^+(aq) + e- -> Ag(s)}$. The concentration of the solution stays the same while silver moves from the bar to the spoon.

## Faraday's law

The more electrons pass through a cell, the more product forms. A current is a flow of charge: a current of $1\ \text{A}$ carries one coulomb of charge each second, so the charge is $Q = It$, with the time in seconds.

:::definition Faraday constant
The **Faraday constant**, $F$, is the charge of one mole of electrons: $F = 9.65 \times 10^4\ \text{C/mol}$.
:::

:::definition Faraday's law
The amount of electrons that passes through a cell is $n_{e^-} = \dfrac{It}{F}$, where $I$ is the current in amperes and $t$ is the time in seconds. The half-reaction then gives the amount of product.
:::

:::example Moles of electrons
A current of $2.50\ \text{A}$ flows through a cell for $30.0\ \text{min}$. What amount of electrons passes through it?
@step $t = 30.0 \times 60 = 1800\ \text{s}$, and $Q = It = 2.50 \times 1800 = 4500\ \text{C}$
@step $n_{e^-} = \dfrac{Q}{F} = \dfrac{4500}{9.65 \times 10^4}$
@answer $n_{e^-} = 0.0466\ \text{mol}$
:::

:::check c30-faraday 1
:::

:::example Mass of copper
Copper is plated from copper(II) sulfate solution with a current of $3.00\ \text{A}$ for $45.0\ \text{min}$: $\ce{Cu^2+ + 2e- -> Cu}$. What mass of copper forms? Use the molar mass Cu $63.55\ \text{g/mol}$.
@step $t = 45.0 \times 60 = 2700\ \text{s}$
@step $n_{e^-} = \dfrac{It}{F} = \dfrac{3.00 \times 2700}{9.65 \times 10^4} = 0.08394\ \text{mol}$
@step Each copper atom needs two electrons: $n(\ce{Cu}) = 0.08394 \times \dfrac{1}{2} = 0.04197\ \text{mol}$
@step $m = nM = 0.04197 \times 63.55 = 2.667\ \text{g}$
@answer $2.67\ \text{g}$ of copper
:::

:::mistake Forgetting the electrons per atom
Skipping the mole ratio from the half-reaction treats every electron as one copper atom: $0.08394 \times 63.55 = 5.33\ \text{g}$, twice the right answer. A $\ce{Cu^2+}$ ion needs two electrons, an $\ce{Ag^+}$ ion one, and an $\ce{Al^3+}$ ion three.
:::

:::check c30-faraday 2
:::

Faraday's law also works backwards, from a mass of product to the time or the current needed.

:::example Time to silver-plate
How long does it take to plate $2.00\ \text{g}$ of silver onto a spoon with a current of $0.500\ \text{A}$? $\ce{Ag^+ + e- -> Ag}$; use the molar mass Ag $107.87\ \text{g/mol}$.
@step $n(\ce{Ag}) = \dfrac{2.00}{107.87} = 0.01854\ \text{mol}$, and one electron per atom gives $n_{e^-} = 0.01854\ \text{mol}$.
@step $t = \dfrac{n_{e^-}F}{I} = \dfrac{0.01854 \times 9.65 \times 10^4}{0.500} = 3578\ \text{s}$
@answer $3578\ \text{s} \div 60 = 59.6\ \text{min}$, about an hour.
:::

:::check c30-faraday 3
:::

:::summary
- An **electrolytic cell** uses electrical energy to drive a non-spontaneous reaction. Oxidation still happens at the anode (now positive) and reduction at the cathode (now negative).
- Molten salts give the metal at the cathode and the non-metal at the anode.
- In solutions, water competes: the strongest oxidising agent is reduced at the cathode, and the strongest reducing agent is oxidised at the anode.
- The **minimum voltage** is the size of the negative cell potential, $E^\circ_\text{cathode} - E^\circ_\text{anode}$.
- In **electroplating**, the object is the cathode and the plating metal is the anode.
- **Faraday's law**: $n_{e^-} = \dfrac{It}{F}$, with $F = 9.65 \times 10^4\ \text{C/mol}$ and $t$ in seconds; then use the half-reaction to find the amount of product.
:::
