Vegetable oils are liquid at room temperature because their long molecules contain carbon–carbon double bonds. Bubble hydrogen through a warm oil with a nickel catalyst, and hydrogen atoms add across some of those double bonds: the oil becomes a soft solid, used to make some margarines. Chemists turn one organic compound into another in a handful of ways, and the three most common are addition, substitution and elimination.

In this lesson you will classify organic reactions as addition, substitution or elimination, and predict the products of each.

## Addition reactions

:::definition Addition reaction
In an **addition reaction**, a small molecule adds across a double or triple bond: the multiple bond opens, and one part of the small molecule joins each of its two carbons. Two reactants make one product. Only unsaturated compounds, such as alkenes and alkynes, undergo addition.
:::

The small molecule decides the product:

| Reagent | Name of the reaction | Example |
|---|---|---|
| hydrogen, $\ce{H2}$ (with a Pt or Ni catalyst) | **hydrogenation** | $\ce{CH2=CH-CH3 + H2 -> CH3-CH2-CH3}$ |
| a halogen, $\ce{Cl2}$ or $\ce{Br2}$ | **halogenation** | $\ce{CH2=CH2 + Br2 -> CH2Br-CH2Br}$ |
| a hydrogen halide, $\ce{HCl}$ or $\ce{HBr}$ | hydrohalogenation | $\ce{CH3-CH=CH-CH3 + HCl -> CH3-CHCl-CH2-CH3}$ |
| water, $\ce{H2O}$ (as steam, with an acid catalyst) | hydration | $\ce{CH2=CH2 + H2O -> CH3-CH2-OH}$ |

:::definition Hydrogenation and halogenation
**Hydrogenation** is the addition of hydrogen across a multiple bond, which turns an alkene into an alkane. **Halogenation** of an alkene is the addition of a halogen, which puts one halogen atom on each carbon of the double bond.
:::

Adding bromine is the laboratory test for a carbon–carbon double bond. Orange bromine water is shaken with the compound: an alkene turns it colourless at once, as the bromine adds across the double bond, while an alkane leaves it orange.

:::example Adding bromine
Ethene reacts with bromine. Write the equation and name the product.
@step The double bond opens, and one bromine atom adds to each carbon.
@step $\ce{CH2=CH2 + Br2 -> CH2Br-CH2Br}$
@answer The product is 1,2-dibromoethane.
:::

When hydrogen chloride, hydrogen bromide or water adds to an alkene whose two double-bond carbons are not alike, two products are possible. The major product follows a simple rule: **the hydrogen atom adds to the carbon that already has more hydrogen atoms**. So propene and hydrogen bromide give mainly 2-bromopropane: $\ce{CH2=CH-CH3 + HBr -> CH3-CHBr-CH3}$. Questions that need the rule state it.

An alkyne can add twice: one molecule of a reagent turns its triple bond into a double bond, and a second turns that into a single bond. For example, $\ce{CH#CH + H2 -> CH2=CH2}$, then $\ce{CH2=CH2 + H2 -> CH3-CH3}$.

## Substitution reactions

:::definition Substitution reaction
In a **substitution reaction**, an atom or group of atoms takes the place of another atom or group in a molecule. Two reactants make two products.
:::

**Alkanes** are saturated, so they cannot add anything. In ultraviolet light, a halogen replaces one of their hydrogen atoms, which leaves as a hydrogen halide:

$$\ce{CH4 + Cl2 ->[{light}] CH3Cl + HCl}$$

The reaction can go on, replacing more hydrogens, to give $\ce{CH2Cl2}$, $\ce{CHCl3}$ and $\ce{CCl4}$, so the product is usually a mixture.

**Haloalkanes** react with hydroxide ions in a warm aqueous solution: the $\ce{OH}$ takes the place of the halogen atom, making an alcohol.

$$\ce{CH3-CH2-CH2Br + OH- -> CH3-CH2-CH2-OH + Br-}$$

**Benzene** has a very stable ring, so it reacts by substitution rather than addition, keeping its ring. With bromine and an iron(III) bromide catalyst: $\ce{C6H6 + Br2 ->[FeBr3] C6H5Br + HBr}$.

:::example Chlorinating ethane
Ethane reacts with chlorine in ultraviolet light, and one hydrogen atom is replaced. Write the equation and name the organic product.
@step A chlorine atom replaces a hydrogen atom, which leaves with the other chlorine atom as $\ce{HCl}$.
@step $\ce{CH3-CH3 + Cl2 ->[{light}] CH3-CH2Cl + HCl}$
@answer Chloroethane, along with hydrogen chloride.
:::

## Elimination reactions

:::definition Elimination reaction
In an **elimination reaction**, a small molecule, such as water or a hydrogen halide, is removed from one molecule. The atoms are taken from two neighbouring carbon atoms, and a double bond forms between them. One reactant gives two products.
:::

**Alcohols** lose water when they are heated with concentrated sulfuric acid, a reaction called dehydration. The $\ce{-OH}$ group and a hydrogen atom from the next carbon leave together:

$$\ce{CH3-CH2-OH ->[H2SO4][{heat}] CH2=CH2 + H2O}$$

**Haloalkanes** heated with hydroxide ions dissolved in ethanol lose a hydrogen halide:

$$\ce{CH3-CHBr-CH3 + OH- ->[{ethanol, heat}] CH2=CH-CH3 + H2O + Br-}$$

:::example Dehydrating propan-2-ol
Propan-2-ol, $\ce{CH3-CH(OH)-CH3}$, is heated with concentrated sulfuric acid. Name the organic product.
@step The $\ce{-OH}$ group on carbon 2 leaves with a hydrogen atom from carbon 1 or carbon 3. Both choices give the same alkene.
@step $\ce{CH3-CH(OH)-CH3 ->[H2SO4][{heat}] CH2=CH-CH3 + H2O}$
@answer Propene.
:::

:::mistake Substitution or elimination?
A haloalkane and hydroxide ions can react in two ways. In a warm **aqueous** solution, the $\ce{OH}$ takes the place of the halogen: substitution, giving an alcohol. Heated in **ethanol**, a hydrogen halide is removed: elimination, giving an alkene. Read the conditions, then check the product: does it have an $\ce{-OH}$ group or a new double bond?
:::

## Telling the three apart

| Reaction | Reactants | Products | What changes |
|---|---|---|---|
| addition | two | one | a double or triple bond opens; atoms add to its two carbons |
| substitution | two | two | one atom or group swaps for another |
| elimination | one | two | a small molecule leaves; a double bond forms |

:::tip Count, then look at the bonds
Count the molecules on each side, then compare the organic reactant and product. A multiple bond that becomes a single bond means addition; a new multiple bond means elimination; the same bonds with one atom swapped means substitution.
:::

## Predicting products

To predict a product, first decide which kind of reaction happens: a multiple bond with $\ce{H2}$, a halogen, a hydrogen halide or water means addition; an alkane with a halogen in light, or a haloalkane with aqueous hydroxide, means substitution; an alcohol with hot concentrated sulfuric acid means elimination. Then move the atoms, and name the product.

:::example Bromine and but-2-ene
Name the product of but-2-ene, $\ce{CH3-CH=CH-CH3}$, with bromine.
@step An alkene and a halogen: addition. One bromine atom adds to each carbon of the double bond, carbons 2 and 3.
@step $\ce{CH3-CH=CH-CH3 + Br2 -> CH3-CHBr-CHBr-CH3}$
@answer 2,3-dibromobutane
:::

:::example Working backwards
Which alkene and which reagent form 1,2-dichloropropane, $\ce{CH2Cl-CHCl-CH3}$, by addition?
@step The two chlorine atoms are on neighbouring carbons 1 and 2, so the double bond was between carbons 1 and 2: the alkene is propene, $\ce{CH2=CH-CH3}$.
@step Two chlorine atoms were added, so the reagent is chlorine, $\ce{Cl2}$. (Hydrogen chloride adds only one chlorine atom.)
@answer Propene and chlorine: $\ce{CH2=CH-CH3 + Cl2 -> CH2Cl-CHCl-CH3}$
:::

:::summary
- **Addition**: two reactants give one product, as a small molecule adds across a double or triple bond. Hydrogenation adds $\ce{H2}$, halogenation adds $\ce{Cl2}$ or $\ce{Br2}$; $\ce{HCl}$, $\ce{HBr}$ and $\ce{H2O}$ add too. An alkene turns orange bromine water colourless.
- When HX or water adds to an unsymmetrical alkene, the hydrogen goes mainly to the carbon that already has more hydrogens.
- **Substitution**: two reactants give two products, as one atom or group replaces another. Alkanes with halogens in ultraviolet light; haloalkanes with aqueous hydroxide ions give alcohols; benzene keeps its ring.
- **Elimination**: one reactant gives two products, as a small molecule leaves and a double bond forms. Alcohols lose water with hot concentrated sulfuric acid; haloalkanes lose HX with hydroxide in hot ethanol.
:::
