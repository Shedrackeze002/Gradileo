When bottles of concentrated ammonia solution and concentrated hydrochloric acid are opened side by side in a fume cupboard, a white smoke forms in the air between them. Ammonia gas and hydrogen chloride gas meet and react to make tiny crystals of ammonium chloride: $\ce{NH3(g) + HCl(g) -> NH4Cl(s)}$. It is an acid–base reaction, yet no water is involved and no hydroxide ions form. To describe it, chemists need a broader idea of what acids and bases are.

In this lesson you will identify Brønsted–Lowry acids, bases and conjugate pairs in a reaction, and write the conjugate acid or base of a particle, including particles that can act both ways.

## Acids and bases as proton donors and acceptors

In earlier courses you met the **Arrhenius** definitions: an acid makes hydrogen ions in water, and a base makes hydroxide ions. They work for hydrochloric acid and sodium hydroxide, but not for ammonia, which is a base without containing any hydroxide, and not for reactions outside water.

A hydrogen atom that has lost its electron is just a proton, so a hydrogen ion, $\ce{H+}$, is a **proton**. The Brønsted–Lowry theory describes acid–base reactions as the transfer of a proton from one particle to another.

:::definition Brønsted–Lowry acid
A **Brønsted–Lowry acid** is a particle that **donates** a proton ($\ce{H+}$) to another particle.
:::

:::definition Brønsted–Lowry base
A **Brønsted–Lowry base** is a particle that **accepts** a proton from another particle.
:::

When an acid such as hydrogen fluoride dissolves, it gives a proton to a water molecule. The water molecule becomes a **hydronium ion**, $\ce{H3O+}$, the ion that makes a solution acidic.

$$\ce{HF(aq) + H2O(l) <=> H3O+(aq) + F-(aq)}$$

Here $\ce{HF}$ is the acid (it loses a proton) and $\ce{H2O}$ is the base (it gains one). In the smoke reaction, $\ce{HCl}$ is the acid and $\ce{NH3}$ is the base, even though there is no water at all.

:::definition Hydronium ion
The **hydronium ion**, $\ce{H3O+}$, is a water molecule that has accepted a proton. In water, a free $\ce{H+}$ ion is always attached to a water molecule, so chemists write $\ce{H3O+(aq)}$.
:::

:::example Ammonia in water
Identify the Brønsted–Lowry acid and base in $\ce{NH3(aq) + H2O(l) <=> NH4+(aq) + OH-(aq)}$.
@step $\ce{NH3}$ becomes $\ce{NH4+}$: it gains a proton, so it is the base.
@step $\ce{H2O}$ becomes $\ce{OH-}$: it loses a proton, so it is the acid.
@answer Acid: $\ce{H2O}$. Base: $\ce{NH3}$. Water acts as an acid here, which is why a solution of ammonia contains $\ce{OH-}$ ions.
:::

## Conjugate acid–base pairs

Look at the reverse reaction of $\ce{HF + H2O <=> H3O+ + F-}$. Now $\ce{H3O+}$ donates a proton (it is an acid) and $\ce{F-}$ accepts it (it is a base). Every acid–base equilibrium has an acid and a base on **each** side.

:::definition Conjugate acid–base pair
Two particles that differ by exactly one proton form a **conjugate acid–base pair**. The one with the extra proton is the **conjugate acid**; the other is the **conjugate base**. In every proton transfer there are two pairs, one member of each on each side of the equation.
:::

$$\underset{\text{acid}}{\ce{HF}} + \underset{\text{base}}{\ce{H2O}} \rightleftharpoons \underset{\text{conjugate acid}}{\ce{H3O+}} + \underset{\text{conjugate base}}{\ce{F-}}$$

The two pairs are $\ce{HF}$ / $\ce{F-}$ and $\ce{H3O+}$ / $\ce{H2O}$.

:::example Labelling a reaction
Label the acid, base, conjugate acid and conjugate base in $\ce{CH3COOH(aq) + CO3^2-(aq) <=> CH3COO-(aq) + HCO3-(aq)}$, and name the two conjugate pairs.
@step $\ce{CH3COOH}$ loses a proton and becomes $\ce{CH3COO-}$: acid, and its conjugate base.
@step $\ce{CO3^2-}$ gains the proton and becomes $\ce{HCO3-}$: base, and its conjugate acid.
@answer Pairs: $\ce{CH3COOH}$ / $\ce{CH3COO-}$ and $\ce{HCO3-}$ / $\ce{CO3^2-}$.
:::

:::mistake The two reactants are not a pair
The acid and the base on the same side of the equation never form a conjugate pair: $\ce{CH3COOH}$ and $\ce{CO3^2-}$ differ by much more than one proton. A conjugate pair always has one member on each side of the equation.
:::

:::

:::example The white smoke
Hydrogen chloride gas and ammonia gas react to form solid ammonium chloride: $\ce{HCl(g) + NH3(g) -> NH4Cl(s)}$. Show that this is a proton transfer, and name the conjugate pairs.
@step Solid ammonium chloride is made of $\ce{NH4+}$ and $\ce{Cl-}$ ions, so the reaction is $\ce{HCl + NH3 -> NH4+ + Cl-}$.
@step $\ce{HCl}$ loses a proton and becomes $\ce{Cl-}$: it is the acid. $\ce{NH3}$ gains the proton and becomes $\ce{NH4+}$: it is the base.
@answer A proton passes from $\ce{HCl}$ to $\ce{NH3}$. The conjugate pairs are $\ce{HCl}$ / $\ce{Cl-}$ and $\ce{NH4+}$ / $\ce{NH3}$.
:::

## Writing conjugates

To find a conjugate, move one proton, and keep track of the charge:

- **Conjugate base**: remove one $\ce{H}$ and lower the charge by 1. $\ce{H2SO4} \to \ce{HSO4-}$, $\ce{NH4+} \to \ce{NH3}$, $\ce{H2O} \to \ce{OH-}$.
- **Conjugate acid**: add one $\ce{H}$ and raise the charge by 1. $\ce{CO3^2-} \to \ce{HCO3-}$, $\ce{NH3} \to \ce{NH4+}$, $\ce{H2O} \to \ce{H3O+}$.

| Acid | Conjugate base | | Base | Conjugate acid |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| $\ce{HNO2}$ | $\ce{NO2-}$ | | $\ce{F-}$ | $\ce{HF}$ |
| $\ce{H2PO4-}$ | $\ce{HPO4^2-}$ | | $\ce{PO4^3-}$ | $\ce{HPO4^2-}$ |
| $\ce{HSO4-}$ | $\ce{SO4^2-}$ | | $\ce{CH3COO-}$ | $\ce{CH3COOH}$ |

:::example Two conjugates
Write the conjugate base of $\ce{H2CO3}$ and the conjugate acid of $\ce{OH-}$.
@step $\ce{H2CO3}$ loses one $\ce{H+}$: one hydrogen fewer, and the charge goes from 0 to −1.
@step $\ce{OH-}$ gains one $\ce{H+}$: one hydrogen more, and the charge goes from −1 to 0.
@answer The conjugate base of $\ce{H2CO3}$ is $\ce{HCO3-}$; the conjugate acid of $\ce{OH-}$ is $\ce{H2O}$.
:::

:::tip Typing a formula with a charge
Type the charge after the formula: `HCO3-` or `HCO3^-` for $\ce{HCO3-}$, `NH4+` for $\ce{NH4+}$, and `HPO4^2-` for $\ce{HPO4^2-}$.
:::

## Amphiprotic particles

Water was a base when hydrogen fluoride dissolved, and an acid when ammonia dissolved. Particles that can go either way are common.

:::definition Amphiprotic
An **amphiprotic** particle can act as a Brønsted–Lowry acid or as a base, depending on what it reacts with. It has a proton it can give away, and it can also accept one. Examples: $\ce{H2O}$, $\ce{HCO3-}$, $\ce{H2PO4-}$, $\ce{HPO4^2-}$ and $\ce{HSO3-}$.
:::

An amphiprotic particle has both a conjugate acid and a conjugate base. For the hydrogen carbonate ion, $\ce{HCO3-}$, the conjugate acid is $\ce{H2CO3}$ and the conjugate base is $\ce{CO3^2-}$.

:::example One ion, two roles
What does $\ce{HCO3-}$ do in each reaction?

Reaction 1: $\ce{HCO3-(aq) + OH-(aq) <=> CO3^2-(aq) + H2O(l)}$

Reaction 2: $\ce{HCO3-(aq) + H3O+(aq) <=> H2CO3(aq) + H2O(l)}$
@step Reaction 1: $\ce{HCO3-}$ becomes $\ce{CO3^2-}$; it gives its proton to $\ce{OH-}$, so it acts as an acid.
@step Reaction 2: $\ce{HCO3-}$ becomes $\ce{H2CO3}$; it takes a proton from $\ce{H3O+}$, so it acts as a base.
@answer An acid in reaction 1 and a base in reaction 2: $\ce{HCO3-}$ is amphiprotic.
:::

:::note Why baking soda neutralises acids
Because $\ce{HCO3-}$ can accept protons, sodium hydrogen carbonate neutralises acids, releasing carbon dioxide as the $\ce{H2CO3}$ it forms breaks down: $\ce{H2CO3(aq) -> H2O(l) + CO2(g)}$. You will predict reactions like this in lesson 4.5.
:::

:::summary
- A **Brønsted–Lowry acid** donates a proton ($\ce{H+}$); a **Brønsted–Lowry base** accepts one. Acid–base reactions are proton transfers, in water or not.
- In water, a proton joins a water molecule to form the **hydronium ion**, $\ce{H3O+}$.
- A **conjugate acid–base pair** is two particles that differ by one proton, one on each side of the equation; every proton transfer has two pairs.
- Conjugate base: remove one H and lower the charge by 1. Conjugate acid: add one H and raise the charge by 1.
- An **amphiprotic** particle, such as $\ce{H2O}$, $\ce{HCO3-}$ or $\ce{H2PO4-}$, can act as an acid or a base, and has both a conjugate acid and a conjugate base.
:::
