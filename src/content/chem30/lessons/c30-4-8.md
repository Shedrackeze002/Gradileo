Your blood stays between about pH 7.35 and 7.45, even though your muscles pour acids into it whenever you exercise. A change of a few tenths of a pH unit would be dangerous, so blood carries a **buffer**: a mixture of carbonic acid and hydrogen carbonate ions that soaks up added acid or base. Buffers keep the pH steady in living things, in swimming pools and in many industrial processes.

In this lesson you will identify buffers and explain how they resist changes in pH, and read the titration curves of strong, weak and polyprotic acids and bases, using them to find concentrations and to choose an indicator.

## Buffers

:::definition Buffer
A **buffer** is a solution that keeps its pH nearly constant when a small amount of a strong acid or a strong base is added. It contains a **weak acid and its conjugate base**, in similar amounts.
:::

| Buffer | Weak acid | Conjugate base |
|---|---|---|
| acetic acid and sodium acetate | $\ce{CH3COOH}$ | $\ce{CH3COO-}$ |
| ammonium chloride and ammonia | $\ce{NH4+}$ | $\ce{NH3}$ |
| sodium dihydrogen phosphate and sodium hydrogen phosphate | $\ce{H2PO4-}$ | $\ce{HPO4^2-}$ |
| carbonic acid and sodium hydrogen carbonate (in blood) | $\ce{H2CO3}$ | $\ce{HCO3-}$ |

A mixture of hydrochloric acid and sodium chloride is **not** a buffer: $\ce{Cl-}$ is the conjugate base of a strong acid, far too weak a base to take a proton from added acid. Two weak acids together, such as acetic acid and hydrofluoric acid, are not a buffer either: there is no base to react with added acid.

:::example Spotting a buffer
Which of these, each with equal amounts of the two substances, is a buffer: (a) nitric acid and potassium nitrate; (b) hydrofluoric acid and sodium fluoride; (c) sodium hydroxide and sodium chloride?
@step (a) $\ce{HNO3}$ is a strong acid, and $\ce{NO3-}$ is too weak a base to matter: not a buffer.
@step (b) $\ce{HF}$ is a weak acid, and $\ce{F-}$ from sodium fluoride is its conjugate base: a buffer.
@step (c) A strong base and a neutral salt, with no weak acid: not a buffer.
@answer (b), hydrofluoric acid and sodium fluoride.
:::

## How a buffer works

Each part of a buffer deals with one kind of addition. In an acetic acid–acetate buffer:

- **Added acid** ($\ce{H3O+}$) is used up by the **base** of the buffer: $\ce{CH3COO-(aq) + H3O+(aq) -> CH3COOH(aq) + H2O(l)}$
- **Added base** ($\ce{OH-}$) is used up by the **acid** of the buffer: $\ce{CH3COOH(aq) + OH-(aq) -> CH3COO-(aq) + H2O(l)}$

Both reactions go almost to completion, so the added ion disappears. All that changes is the ratio of acid to conjugate base, and only a little, so the pH hardly changes.

:::example Adding a little strong acid
A buffer contains $0.100\ \text{mol/L}$ of acetic acid and $0.100\ \text{mol/L}$ of sodium acetate. A small amount of hydrochloric acid is added. Write the equation for the reaction that keeps the pH steady, and say what happens to the two concentrations.
@step The added acid is $\ce{H3O+}$; the buffer's base, $\ce{CH3COO-}$, takes its proton.
@step $\ce{CH3COO-(aq) + H3O+(aq) -> CH3COOH(aq) + H2O(l)}$
@answer $[\ce{CH3COO-}]$ decreases a little and $[\ce{CH3COOH}]$ increases a little; the pH falls only slightly.
:::

:::mistake A buffer is not a wall
A buffer reduces a change in pH; it does not prevent it, and it can be used up. Once nearly all of its base has reacted with added acid (or all of its acid with added base), the next addition changes the pH sharply. The amount of acid or base a buffer can absorb is its **capacity**: more concentrated buffers have a larger capacity.
:::

## The pH of a buffer

Rearranging the $K_a$ expression of the buffer's weak acid gives
$$[\ce{H3O+}] = K_a \times \dfrac{[\text{acid}]}{[\text{conjugate base}]}$$
When the acid and its conjugate base are equal, $[\ce{H3O+}] = K_a$, so the pH equals $\text{p}K_a = -\log K_a$. A buffer works best within about one pH unit of the $\text{p}K_a$ of its weak acid, so to hold a chosen pH, pick a weak acid whose $\text{p}K_a$ is close to it.

:::example How well a buffer resists
A buffer contains $0.100\ \text{mol/L}$ each of acetic acid and sodium acetate ($K_a = 1.8 \times 10^{-5}$ at $25^\circ\text{C}$). What is its pH? What is the pH after $0.010\ \text{mol}$ of $\ce{HCl}$ is added to $1.00\ \text{L}$ of it, and after the same amount is added to $1.00\ \text{L}$ of pure water?
@step Buffer: $[\ce{H3O+}] = 1.8 \times 10^{-5} \times \dfrac{0.100}{0.100} = 1.8 \times 10^{-5}$, so $\text{pH} = 4.74$.
@step After the acid: $0.010\ \text{mol/L}$ of acetate becomes acetic acid, giving $0.110$ and $0.090\ \text{mol/L}$. $[\ce{H3O+}] = 1.8 \times 10^{-5} \times \dfrac{0.110}{0.090} = 2.2 \times 10^{-5}$, so $\text{pH} = 4.66$.
@step Pure water: $[\ce{H3O+}] = 0.010\ \text{mol/L}$, so $\text{pH} = 2.00$.
@answer The buffer's pH falls from 4.74 to 4.66, by less than 0.1; the water's pH falls from 7.00 to 2.00.
:::

:::definition Equivalence point
The **equivalence point** is the point in a titration where the amount of titrant added has reacted exactly with the sample, in the mole ratio of the balanced equation. On a titration curve it is the middle of the steep part.
:::

A weak base titrated with a strong acid gives the mirror image: $0.100\ \text{mol/L}$ ammonia with hydrochloric acid starts near pH 11.1, falls through a buffer region, and has its equivalence point near pH 5.3, below 7, because ammonium ions are a weak acid.

:::example Reading a titration curve
In the graph, find the volume of sodium hydroxide at the equivalence point of the acetic acid, its concentration, and $K_a$ from the pH halfway to the equivalence point.
@step The steep part is centred on $25.0\ \text{mL}$, at about pH 8.7.
@step $n(\ce{NaOH}) = 0.100 \times 0.0250 = 0.00250\ \text{mol} = n(\ce{CH3COOH})$, so $c = \dfrac{0.00250}{0.02500} = 0.100\ \text{mol/L}$.
@step Halfway, at $12.5\ \text{mL}$, the pH is 4.75, so $K_a = 10^{-4.75} = 1.8 \times 10^{-5}$.
@answer $25.0\ \text{mL}$; $0.100\ \text{mol/L}$; $K_a = 1.8 \times 10^{-5}$.
:::

## Choosing an indicator

An indicator is itself a weak acid whose conjugate base has a different colour, so it changes colour over a range of one and a half to two pH units around its own $\text{p}K_a$. For the colour change (the endpoint) to mark the equivalence point, choose the indicator whose **range includes the pH at the equivalence point**.

| Indicator | Colour change | pH range |
|---|---|---|
| methyl orange | red to yellow | 3.2 to 4.4 |
| bromocresol green | yellow to blue | 3.8 to 5.4 |
| methyl red | red to yellow | 4.8 to 6.0 |
| bromothymol blue | yellow to blue | 6.0 to 7.6 |
| phenolphthalein | colourless to pink | 8.2 to 10.0 |
| alizarin yellow R | yellow to red | 10.1 to 12.0 |

:::example An indicator for each titration
Using the table, choose an indicator for (a) acetic acid titrated with sodium hydroxide, (b) hydrochloric acid titrated with sodium hydroxide, and (c) ammonia titrated with hydrochloric acid.
@step (a) Equivalence point about pH 8.7: phenolphthalein, 8.2 to 10.0.
@step (b) Equivalence point pH 7.0: bromothymol blue, 6.0 to 7.6.
@step (c) Equivalence point about pH 5.3: methyl red, 4.8 to 6.0.
@answer (a) phenolphthalein; (b) bromothymol blue; (c) methyl red.
:::

:::mistake Phenolphthalein is not always right
Phenolphthalein suits a weak acid titrated with a strong base, but for a weak base titrated with a strong acid it changes colour long before the equivalence point, while the solution is still basic. Read the pH at the equivalence point first, then choose.
:::

## Polyprotic acids

A **polyprotic acid** has more than one proton to give, and it gives them one at a time, each with its own $K_a$. Phosphoric acid, $\ce{H3PO4}$, loses its first proton easily ($K_a = 6.9 \times 10^{-3}$) and its second much less easily ($K_a = 6.2 \times 10^{-8}$):

$$\ce{H3PO4 + OH- -> H2PO4- + H2O} \qquad\text{then}\qquad \ce{H2PO4- + OH- -> HPO4^2- + H2O}$$

:::definition Polyprotic acid
An acid with two or more protons that it donates one at a time, such as sulfurous acid, $\ce{H2SO3}$, or phosphoric acid, $\ce{H3PO4}$. Its titration curve has one steep rise for each proton that is removed separately.
:::

:::summary
- A **buffer** contains a weak acid and its conjugate base in similar amounts. Its base uses up added $\ce{H3O+}$ and its acid uses up added $\ce{OH-}$, so the pH changes only a little, until the buffer's capacity is used up.
- In a buffer, $[\ce{H3O+}] = K_a \times \dfrac{[\text{acid}]}{[\text{conjugate base}]}$; with equal amounts, $\text{pH} = \text{p}K_a$. Choose a weak acid whose $\text{p}K_a$ is close to the pH wanted.
- On a **titration curve**, the **equivalence point** is the middle of the steep part. Strong acid with strong base: equivalence at pH 7. Weak acid with strong base: higher start, buffer region, $\text{pH} = \text{p}K_a$ halfway, equivalence above 7. Weak base with strong acid: equivalence below 7.
- Choose the **indicator** whose colour-change range includes the pH at the equivalence point.
- A **polyprotic acid** loses its protons one at a time and gives one steep rise for each; the first equivalence point gives the moles of acid.
:::
