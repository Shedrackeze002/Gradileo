Vinegar poured onto baking soda fizzes furiously; vinegar poured onto table salt does nothing at all. Both are white powders made of sodium ions and an anion, so why does one react with an acid and not the other? The answer lies in how strongly each anion holds on to a proton, and a table of relative acid strengths lets you predict it.

In this lesson you will use a table of relative acid strengths to find the strongest acid and the strongest base in a mixture, write the reaction between them, and decide whether products or reactants are favoured at equilibrium.

## A table of relative acid strengths

In lesson 4.4 you saw that every acid has a conjugate base. Acids differ in how readily they give up a proton, and a table lists them in order, with the acid ionisation constant $K_a$ for each (you will meet $K_a$ properly in lesson 4.6; for now, a larger $K_a$ means a stronger acid).

| Acid | Conjugate base | $K_a$ at $25^\circ\text{C}$ |
|---|---|---|
| $\ce{HCl}$ | $\ce{Cl-}$ | very large |
| $\ce{HNO3}$ | $\ce{NO3-}$ | very large |
| $\ce{H3O+}$ | $\ce{H2O}$ | 1 |
| $\ce{HSO4-}$ | $\ce{SO4^2-}$ | $1.0 \times 10^{-2}$ |
| $\ce{H3PO4}$ | $\ce{H2PO4-}$ | $6.9 \times 10^{-3}$ |
| $\ce{HF}$ | $\ce{F-}$ | $6.6 \times 10^{-4}$ |
| $\ce{HNO2}$ | $\ce{NO2-}$ | $5.6 \times 10^{-4}$ |
| $\ce{CH3COOH}$ | $\ce{CH3COO-}$ | $1.8 \times 10^{-5}$ |
| $\ce{H2CO3}$ | $\ce{HCO3-}$ | $4.5 \times 10^{-7}$ |
| $\ce{H2PO4-}$ | $\ce{HPO4^2-}$ | $6.2 \times 10^{-8}$ |
| $\ce{HClO}$ | $\ce{ClO-}$ | $4.0 \times 10^{-8}$ |
| $\ce{HCN}$ | $\ce{CN-}$ | $6.2 \times 10^{-10}$ |
| $\ce{NH4+}$ | $\ce{NH3}$ | $5.6 \times 10^{-10}$ |
| $\ce{HCO3-}$ | $\ce{CO3^2-}$ | $4.7 \times 10^{-11}$ |
| $\ce{HPO4^2-}$ | $\ce{PO4^3-}$ | $4.8 \times 10^{-13}$ |
| $\ce{H2O}$ | $\ce{OH-}$ | $1.0 \times 10^{-14}$ |

:::definition Relative strength
Acids get **weaker going down** the left column. Bases get **stronger going down** the right column: the stronger an acid, the weaker its conjugate base, because an acid that gives its proton away easily leaves behind a base that does not want it back.
:::

The strong acids at the top ionise completely in water, so in a solution they are present as $\ce{H3O+}$ ions: the strongest acid that can exist in water. Their conjugate bases, such as $\ce{Cl-}$ and $\ce{NO3-}$, are so weak that they take no part in acid–base reactions. At the bottom, $\ce{OH-}$ is the strongest base that can exist in water.

:::example Comparing strengths
Use the table. Which is the strongest base: $\ce{F-}$, $\ce{CN-}$, $\ce{CH3COO-}$ or $\ce{HCO3-}$?
@step The strongest base is the one lowest in the right column, the conjugate base of the weakest acid.
@step Their acids have $K_a$ of $6.6 \times 10^{-4}$ ($\ce{HF}$), $6.2 \times 10^{-10}$ ($\ce{HCN}$), $1.8 \times 10^{-5}$ ($\ce{CH3COOH}$) and $4.5 \times 10^{-7}$ ($\ce{H2CO3}$). The smallest is that of $\ce{HCN}$.
@answer $\ce{CN-}$ is the strongest base.
:::

## Listing the particles in a mixture

Before predicting a reaction, list every particle actually present:

- **Ionic compounds** are present as separate ions: sodium acetate is $\ce{Na+}$ and $\ce{CH3COO-}$.
- **Strong acids** are present as $\ce{H3O+}$ and their anion: hydrochloric acid is $\ce{H3O+}$ and $\ce{Cl-}$.
- **Weak acids** and molecular bases are present mostly as molecules: $\ce{CH3COOH}$, $\ce{NH3}$.
- **Water** is always there, and it can act as an acid or a base.

Then find the **strongest acid** (highest in the left column) and the **strongest base** (lowest in the right column). Sodium and potassium ions are spectators: they are neither acids nor bases. An amphiprotic particle, such as $\ce{HCO3-}$, appears in both columns, so check it as an acid and as a base.

:::example The strongest acid and base
Solutions of acetic acid and sodium hydrogen carbonate are mixed. Which particle is the strongest acid, and which is the strongest base?
@step Particles: $\ce{CH3COOH}$, $\ce{Na+}$, $\ce{HCO3-}$ and $\ce{H2O}$.
@step Acids among them: $\ce{CH3COOH}$ ($1.8 \times 10^{-5}$), $\ce{HCO3-}$ ($4.7 \times 10^{-11}$) and $\ce{H2O}$ ($1.0 \times 10^{-14}$). The highest is $\ce{CH3COOH}$.
@step Bases among them: $\ce{HCO3-}$ (conjugate base of $\ce{H2CO3}$) and $\ce{H2O}$ (conjugate base of $\ce{H3O+}$). The lowest in the right column is $\ce{HCO3-}$.
@answer Strongest acid: $\ce{CH3COOH}$. Strongest base: $\ce{HCO3-}$.
:::

:::mistake Forgetting water, or counting spectators
Water must always be on the list: in a solution of a weak acid alone, water is the strongest base present. And $\ce{Na+}$ is not an acid, even though it is positive: it has no proton to give.
:::

## Writing the reaction

The main reaction in the mixture is the transfer of **one proton** from the strongest acid to the strongest base. The acid becomes its conjugate base, and the base becomes its conjugate acid.

$$\ce{CH3COOH(aq) + HCO3-(aq) <=> CH3COO-(aq) + H2CO3(aq)}$$

The carbonic acid formed breaks down into water and carbon dioxide, $\ce{H2CO3(aq) -> H2O(l) + CO2(g)}$: that is the fizz when vinegar meets baking soda.

## Products or reactants favoured?

Every proton transfer is an equilibrium with an acid on each side. The reaction runs mostly in the direction in which the **stronger acid gives its proton away**:

- If the acid on the left is **stronger** than the acid formed on the right, **products are favoured**.
- If the acid formed on the right is stronger, **reactants are favoured**.

On the table, products are favoured when the reacting acid is **above** the reacting base: the proton moves "downhill" from the upper left to the lower right.

:::example Vinegar and baking soda
Are products or reactants favoured in $\ce{CH3COOH(aq) + HCO3-(aq) <=> CH3COO-(aq) + H2CO3(aq)}$?
@step Compare the acids: $\ce{CH3COOH}$ on the left ($K_a = 1.8 \times 10^{-5}$) and $\ce{H2CO3}$ on the right ($K_a = 4.5 \times 10^{-7}$).
@step The acid on the left is stronger, so it gives up its proton.
@answer Products are favoured.
:::

:::example A reaction that hardly goes
Solutions of acetic acid and sodium fluoride are mixed. Write the reaction between the strongest acid and the strongest base, and say whether products or reactants are favoured.
@step Particles: $\ce{CH3COOH}$, $\ce{Na+}$, $\ce{F-}$ and $\ce{H2O}$. Strongest acid: $\ce{CH3COOH}$. Strongest base: $\ce{F-}$ (below $\ce{H2O}$ in the right column).
@step $\ce{CH3COOH(aq) + F-(aq) <=> CH3COO-(aq) + HF(aq)}$
@step Compare the acids: $\ce{HF}$ ($6.6 \times 10^{-4}$) is stronger than $\ce{CH3COOH}$ ($1.8 \times 10^{-5}$).
@answer Reactants are favoured: only a little $\ce{HF}$ forms.
:::

:::example A strong acid as H3O+
Hydrochloric acid is added to a solution of sodium acetate. Write the reaction between the strongest acid and the strongest base, and say whether products or reactants are favoured.
@step Particles: $\ce{H3O+}$ and $\ce{Cl-}$ (hydrochloric acid ionises completely), $\ce{Na+}$, $\ce{CH3COO-}$ and $\ce{H2O}$.
@step Strongest acid: $\ce{H3O+}$. Strongest base: $\ce{CH3COO-}$ ($\ce{Cl-}$ and $\ce{H2O}$ are much weaker bases).
@step $\ce{H3O+(aq) + CH3COO-(aq) <=> H2O(l) + CH3COOH(aq)}$
@answer Products are favoured: $\ce{H3O+}$ ($K_a = 1$) is a far stronger acid than $\ce{CH3COOH}$. A strong acid turns the anion of a weak acid back into the weak acid.
:::

:::mistake The strongest base is low on the right
The strongest base is the conjugate base of the **weakest** acid, so look for it near the **bottom of the right column**. Picking the particle with the largest $K_a$ beside it finds the weakest base instead.
:::

:::note Why salt does not fizz
In vinegar and table salt, the particles are $\ce{CH3COOH}$, $\ce{Na+}$, $\ce{Cl-}$ and $\ce{H2O}$. $\ce{Cl-}$ is the conjugate base of a strong acid, an even weaker base than water, so the strongest base is water. The only reaction is the slight ionisation of acetic acid, $\ce{CH3COOH + H2O <=> CH3COO- + H3O+}$, and nothing new forms.
:::

:::summary
- In a **table of relative acid strengths**, acids get weaker going down the left column and their conjugate bases get stronger going down the right column. A larger $K_a$ means a stronger acid.
- List every particle in the mixture: ions of ionic compounds, $\ce{H3O+}$ for a strong acid, molecules of weak acids and bases, and water. $\ce{Na+}$ and $\ce{K+}$ are spectators.
- The **strongest acid** is highest in the left column; the **strongest base** is lowest in the right column. Check amphiprotic particles both ways.
- The main reaction transfers one proton from the strongest acid to the strongest base.
- **Products are favoured** when the acid on the left is stronger than the acid formed on the right (the acid is above the base in the table); otherwise reactants are favoured.
:::
