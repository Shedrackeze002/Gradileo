Most of the energy used worldwide, to run vehicles, heat homes and make electricity, still comes from burning coal, oil and natural gas. These fuels are mixtures of hydrocarbons that took millions of years to form, and burning them releases the energy stored in their bonds, along with carbon dioxide, water and some less welcome products.

In this lesson you will see how crude oil is separated into useful fractions and how cracking turns large molecules into smaller ones, write balanced equations for the complete and incomplete combustion of hydrocarbons and alcohols, and explain the effects of burning fossil fuels.

## Fossil fuels

:::definition Fossil fuel
A **fossil fuel** is a fuel formed over millions of years from the remains of ancient organisms, buried and changed by heat and pressure: coal, crude oil (petroleum) and natural gas. Fossil fuels are **non-renewable**, because they form far more slowly than they are used.
:::

Natural gas is mostly methane, $\ce{CH4}$. Crude oil is a thick mixture of hundreds of hydrocarbons, from small molecules with one to four carbon atoms to large ones with more than forty. As it comes out of the ground it has few uses; it must first be separated.

## Fractional distillation

:::definition Fractional distillation
**Fractional distillation** separates a mixture of liquids into parts, called **fractions**, with different ranges of boiling points. Each fraction is a mixture of hydrocarbons with molecules of a similar size.
:::

In a refinery, crude oil is heated to about $400^\circ\text{C}$, so that most of it vaporises, and the vapour enters the bottom of a tall fractionating column. The column is hottest at the bottom and coolest at the top. As the vapour rises it cools, and each hydrocarbon condenses at the level where the temperature falls below its boiling point; trays at different heights collect the liquids. Large molecules, with strong London dispersion forces between them and high boiling points, condense near the bottom. Small molecules stay as gases and rise to the top.

| Fraction | Carbon atoms per molecule | Boiling range (approximate) | Uses |
|---|---|---|---|
| refinery gases | 1 to 4 | below $25^\circ\text{C}$ | fuel gas, bottled gas |
| petrol | 5 to 10 | $25$ to $180^\circ\text{C}$ | fuel for cars |
| kerosene | 10 to 16 | $180$ to $260^\circ\text{C}$ | fuel for aircraft |
| diesel oil | 14 to 20 | $260$ to $340^\circ\text{C}$ | fuel for diesel engines |
| fuel oil and lubricating oil | 20 to 40 | $340$ to $500^\circ\text{C}$ | fuel for ships, lubricants |
| residue (bitumen) | more than 40 | above $500^\circ\text{C}$ | road surfaces, roofing |

:::example Where does a hydrocarbon collect?
Octane, $\ce{C8H18}$, boils at about $126^\circ\text{C}$, and hexadecane, $\ce{C16H34}$, at about $287^\circ\text{C}$. Which collects higher in the column?
@step Octane has smaller molecules, with weaker dispersion forces, so it boils at a lower temperature.
@step It stays a gas until it reaches the cooler upper part of the column before it condenses.
@answer Octane collects higher, in the petrol fraction; hexadecane condenses lower down, in the diesel fraction.
:::

## Cracking

Crude oil holds more large molecules than are needed, and fewer of the small ones in demand for petrol. Cracking fixes the mismatch.

:::definition Cracking
**Cracking** breaks large alkane molecules into smaller ones by heating them, often with a catalyst. The products are a smaller alkane and one or more alkenes, such as ethene and propene, which are used to make polymers and other chemicals.
:::

Atoms are conserved, so the products of cracking contain all the carbon and hydrogen atoms of the large molecule:

$$\ce{C10H22 -> C8H18 + C2H4}$$

:::example A missing product
Complete the cracking equation $\ce{C14H30 -> C8H18 + 2X}$.
@step Carbon: $14 - 8 = 6$, shared between 2 molecules: 3 each.
@step Hydrogen: $30 - 18 = 12$, shared between 2 molecules: 6 each.
@answer X is $\ce{C3H6}$, propene: $\ce{C14H30 -> C8H18 + 2C3H6}$.
:::

## Complete and incomplete combustion

:::definition Complete combustion
In **complete combustion**, a fuel burns in plenty of oxygen. Every carbon atom ends up in carbon dioxide and every hydrogen atom in water: hydrocarbon (or alcohol) + oxygen → carbon dioxide + water.
:::

:::definition Incomplete combustion
In **incomplete combustion**, there is not enough oxygen. Some carbon ends up as carbon monoxide, $\ce{CO}$, or as carbon itself, $\ce{C}$, which is soot. The flame is yellow and smoky, and less energy is released.
:::

To balance a combustion equation, balance the carbon first, then the hydrogen, and the oxygen last. If the oxygen needs half a molecule, double every coefficient.

:::example Complete combustion of octane
Balance $\ce{C8H18 + O2 -> CO2 + H2O}$.
@step Carbon: 8 on the left, so $\ce{8CO2}$. Hydrogen: 18 on the left, so $\ce{9H2O}$.
@step Oxygen on the right: $8 \times 2 + 9 = 25$ atoms, which needs $12\tfrac{1}{2}$ molecules of $\ce{O2}$.
@step Double everything to clear the half.
@answer $\ce{2C8H18 + 25O2 -> 16CO2 + 18H2O}$
:::

:::example Burning an alcohol
Balance the complete combustion of ethanol: $\ce{C2H5OH + O2 -> CO2 + H2O}$.
@step Carbon: $\ce{2CO2}$. Hydrogen: 6 atoms in all, so $\ce{3H2O}$.
@step Oxygen on the right: $2 \times 2 + 3 = 7$ atoms. Ethanol already brings one, so $\ce{O2}$ must bring 6: $\ce{3O2}$.
@answer $\ce{C2H5OH + 3O2 -> 2CO2 + 3H2O}$
:::

:::mistake The oxygen in an alcohol
An alcohol molecule contains an oxygen atom. Forgetting it when you count the oxygen gives too much $\ce{O2}$: for ethanol, $3\tfrac{1}{2}$ molecules of $\ce{O2}$ would put 8 oxygen atoms on the left and only 7 on the right.
:::

:::example Incomplete combustion of methane
Methane burns in a limited supply of oxygen to form carbon monoxide and water. Balance the equation.
@step Carbon: $\ce{CO}$. Hydrogen: 4 atoms, so $\ce{2H2O}$.
@step Oxygen on the right: $1 + 2 = 3$ atoms, which needs $1\tfrac{1}{2}$ molecules of $\ce{O2}$. Double everything.
@answer $\ce{2CH4 + 3O2 -> 2CO + 4H2O}$
:::

## Burning fossil fuels and the environment

| Product | Where it comes from | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| carbon dioxide, $\ce{CO2}$ | complete combustion of every fossil fuel | absorbs infrared radiation and traps heat in the atmosphere: the enhanced greenhouse effect and climate change |
| carbon monoxide, $\ce{CO}$ | incomplete combustion | poisonous: it binds to haemoglobin in the blood more strongly than oxygen does |
| soot (carbon particles) | incomplete combustion | blackens buildings and damages the lungs |
| sulfur dioxide, $\ce{SO2}$ | sulfur impurities in coal and oil | dissolves in water in the air to form acids: acid deposition (acid rain) |
| nitrogen oxides, $\ce{NO}$ and $\ce{NO2}$ | nitrogen and oxygen from the air, reacting at the high temperature of an engine | acid deposition and smog |

These effects can be reduced: sulfur is removed from fuels before they are sold, and a catalytic converter in a car's exhaust turns carbon monoxide and nitrogen oxides into carbon dioxide and nitrogen. Carbon dioxide cannot be removed this way, because every carbon atom in the fuel ends up in it. Burning less fuel, or using energy sources that release no carbon dioxide, is the only way to make less.

:::example Carbon dioxide from a fuel
What mass of carbon dioxide forms when $1.00\ \text{kg}$ of octane burns completely: $\ce{2C8H18 + 25O2 -> 16CO2 + 18H2O}$? Use the molar masses $M(\ce{C8H18}) = 114.26\ \text{g/mol}$ and $M(\ce{CO2}) = 44.01\ \text{g/mol}$.
@step $n(\ce{C8H18}) = \dfrac{1000\ \text{g}}{114.26\ \text{g/mol}} = 8.752\ \text{mol}$
@step $n(\ce{CO2}) = 8.752 \times \dfrac{16}{2} = 70.02\ \text{mol}$
@step $m = 70.02 \times 44.01 = 3081\ \text{g}$
@answer About $3.08\ \text{kg}$ of carbon dioxide: three times the mass of the fuel, because each carbon atom picks up two oxygen atoms from the air.
:::

:::summary
- **Fossil fuels** (coal, crude oil, natural gas) formed over millions of years from ancient organisms and are non-renewable.
- **Fractional distillation** separates crude oil into fractions by boiling point: small molecules rise to the cool top of the column, large ones condense near the hot bottom.
- **Cracking** breaks large alkanes into smaller alkanes and alkenes; the atoms are conserved.
- **Complete combustion** gives carbon dioxide and water; **incomplete combustion** gives carbon monoxide or soot as well. Balance carbon, then hydrogen, then oxygen, counting any oxygen in the fuel.
- Burning fossil fuels releases carbon dioxide (climate change), carbon monoxide (poisonous), soot, sulfur dioxide and nitrogen oxides (acid deposition and smog).
:::
