An opened bottle of sparkling water fizzes as carbon dioxide escapes. With the cap left on, though, the water stays fizzy for months, even though carbon dioxide molecules are leaving the liquid all the time. Inside the sealed bottle, gas molecules dissolve back into the water exactly as fast as dissolved ones escape, so nothing seems to change. This balance, with change still going on underneath, is called **dynamic equilibrium**, and it is the subject of this whole unit.

In this lesson you will meet reversible reactions, learn the conditions for dynamic equilibrium and what stays the same once a system reaches it, and read graphs of concentration and of reaction rate as a system settles into equilibrium.

## Reversible reactions

Most of the reactions you have balanced so far go one way: the reactants turn into products until one of them runs out. Many reactions can also run backwards. Dinitrogen tetroxide, a colourless gas, splits into brown nitrogen dioxide, and nitrogen dioxide molecules pair up again to make dinitrogen tetroxide:

$$\ce{N2O4(g) <=> 2NO2(g)}$$

:::definition Reversible reaction
A **reversible reaction** can go in both directions: the reactants form products (the **forward reaction**) and the products react to re-form the reactants (the **reverse reaction**). The double arrow, $\rightleftharpoons$, shows that both happen.
:::

When colourless $\ce{N2O4}$ is sealed in a glass tube at constant temperature, a brown colour slowly appears as $\ce{NO2}$ forms. After a while the colour stops changing. The tube does not turn completely brown: some $\ce{N2O4}$ is always left.

## Dynamic equilibrium

At the start only $\ce{N2O4}$ is present, so only the forward reaction can happen, and it happens quickly. As $\ce{N2O4}$ is used up, the forward reaction slows down. Meanwhile $\ce{NO2}$ builds up, so the reverse reaction speeds up. Eventually the two rates are equal: $\ce{NO2}$ is made exactly as fast as it is used up, and so is $\ce{N2O4}$.

:::definition Dynamic equilibrium
A system is at **dynamic equilibrium** when the forward and reverse reactions happen at equal rates, so the amounts of reactants and products stay constant. It is *dynamic* because both reactions keep going; it is an *equilibrium* because nothing changes overall.
:::

Three conditions are needed for a system to reach dynamic equilibrium:

- The reaction must be **reversible**.
- The system must be **closed**: no matter can enter or leave. In an open beaker, a gas product escapes and the reverse reaction can never catch up.
- The **temperature** must stay constant.

:::definition Closed system
A **closed system** can exchange energy with its surroundings but not matter. A sealed flask is closed; an open beaker is not.
:::

Once a system is at equilibrium, it has these features:

| At dynamic equilibrium | Not true at equilibrium |
|---|---|
| The forward and reverse rates are equal. | The reactions have stopped. |
| The concentrations of reactants and products are **constant**. | The concentrations are **equal** to each other. |
| Observable properties, such as colour and pressure, stay constant. | The concentrations are in the ratio of the coefficients. |
| The same equilibrium can be reached from the reactants or from the products. | Equilibrium can only be reached from the reactants. |

How do we know the reactions are still going if nothing seems to change? In one experiment, a little solid lead(II) iodide made with radioactive iodine is added to a saturated solution of lead(II) iodide in a sealed flask. The mass of solid at the bottom stays the same, yet radioactive iodide ions soon turn up in the solution. Solid keeps dissolving and ions keep crystallising, at the same rate.

:::mistake Equilibrium does not mean equal amounts
At equilibrium the concentrations are **constant**, not equal. In the tube of $\ce{N2O4}$ and $\ce{NO2}$, there might be $0.50\ \text{mol/L}$ of one and $0.60\ \text{mol/L}$ of the other. What is equal is the **rate** of the forward and reverse reactions.
:::

:::example Is it at equilibrium?
Liquid bromine is sealed in a flask at constant temperature: $\ce{Br2(l) <=> Br2(g)}$. After a few minutes, the orange-brown colour of the vapour above the liquid stops getting darker. Explain what is happening, and whether the system is at equilibrium.
@step The flask is closed and the temperature is constant, and evaporation and condensation are reverse processes.
@step The colour of the vapour is constant, so the concentration of $\ce{Br2(g)}$ is constant.
@step Molecules still evaporate and still condense; they now do so at equal rates.
@answer Yes: it is a dynamic equilibrium, with evaporation and condensation happening at the same rate.
:::

Equilibria come in three broad kinds. In a **phase equilibrium** one substance is present in two states, as in the bromine flask. In a **solubility equilibrium** a solid is in contact with its saturated solution, as with the lead(II) iodide. In a **chemical reaction equilibrium** new substances form and react back, as with $\ce{N2O4}$ and $\ce{NO2}$.

:::example Approaching from either side
Flask 1 starts with $1.00\ \text{mol}$ of $\ce{N2O4}$. Flask 2, the same size and at the same temperature, starts with $2.00\ \text{mol}$ of $\ce{NO2}$. How do the two mixtures compare once each reaches equilibrium?
@step $2.00\ \text{mol}$ of $\ce{NO2}$ is exactly what $1.00\ \text{mol}$ of $\ce{N2O4}$ would make, so both flasks hold the same atoms.
@step Flask 1 reaches equilibrium by the forward reaction, and flask 2 by the reverse reaction.
@answer The two equilibrium mixtures are the same: an equilibrium can be approached from either side.
:::

## The position of equilibrium

Some reversible reactions stop with mostly products; others hardly get started before the reverse reaction catches up. Chemists describe this with the **position of equilibrium**.

:::definition Equilibrium position
The **equilibrium position** describes the relative amounts of reactants and products at equilibrium. If the equilibrium mixture is mostly products, the equilibrium **lies to the right** and products are **favoured**; if it is mostly reactants, it lies to the left and reactants are favoured.
:::

In lesson 4.2 you will put a number on the equilibrium position, the equilibrium constant, and in lesson 4.3 you will see how changing the conditions moves it.

Three things can be read from a graph like this:

- **When equilibrium is reached**: where the curves become flat, here at $40\ \text{s}$ (the dashed line). From then on the concentrations stay constant.
- **The equilibrium concentrations**: the levels of the flat parts, here $[\ce{N2O4}] = 0.50\ \text{mol/L}$ and $[\ce{NO2}] = 0.60\ \text{mol/L}$. Square brackets mean "the concentration of", in moles per litre.
- **The mole ratio**: the changes in concentration are in the ratio of the coefficients. $[\ce{N2O4}]$ fell by $0.30\ \text{mol/L}$ while $[\ce{NO2}]$ rose by $0.60\ \text{mol/L}$, a $1 : 2$ ratio that matches $\ce{N2O4 <=> 2NO2}$.

:::example Finding the equation from a graph
Gases A and B react in a sealed flask to form gas C. A graph shows $[\text{A}]$ falling from $0.90$ to $0.30\ \text{mol/L}$, $[\text{B}]$ falling from $0.50$ to $0.20\ \text{mol/L}$, and $[\text{C}]$ rising from $0$ to $0.60\ \text{mol/L}$, all levelling off at $30\ \text{s}$. Write the balanced equation.
@step Changes: $\Delta[\text{A}] = -0.60$, $\Delta[\text{B}] = -0.30$ and $\Delta[\text{C}] = +0.60\ \text{mol/L}$.
@step The ratio $0.60 : 0.30 : 0.60$ simplifies to $2 : 1 : 2$.
@answer $\ce{2A + B <=> 2C}$, at equilibrium from $30\ \text{s}$.
:::

:::mistake The changes, not the final values
The coefficients come from how much each concentration **changes**, not from the equilibrium concentrations themselves. In the example, the final values $0.30$, $0.20$ and $0.60$ are not in the ratio $2 : 1 : 2$; the changes are.
:::

The forward rate starts high and falls as $\ce{N2O4}$ is used up. The reverse rate starts at zero, because there is no $\ce{NO2}$ at first, and rises as $\ce{NO2}$ builds up. Equilibrium is reached where the two curves meet, at $40\ \text{s}$, and from then on they stay together: both reactions continue, at the same rate.

:::example Reading a rate graph
Use the rate–time graph above. Describe the system at $20\ \text{s}$ and at $60\ \text{s}$.
@step At $20\ \text{s}$ the forward rate (about $0.43$) is greater than the reverse rate (about $0.23$), so $\ce{NO2}$ is still forming overall and the system is not at equilibrium.
@step At $60\ \text{s}$ both rates are $0.30$: equal, so the concentrations are constant.
@answer At $20\ \text{s}$, not at equilibrium (products still forming overall); at $60\ \text{s}$, at dynamic equilibrium, with both reactions still going.
:::

:::note Starting from the other side
If the flask started with $\ce{NO2}$ only, the graphs would be the mirror image: the reverse rate would start high and fall, the forward rate would start at zero and rise, and $[\ce{N2O4}]$ would rise while $[\ce{NO2}]$ fell, until the rates met.
:::

:::summary
- A **reversible reaction** goes both ways; the double arrow $\rightleftharpoons$ shows the forward and reverse reactions.
- **Dynamic equilibrium** needs a reversible reaction in a **closed system** at constant temperature. The forward and reverse rates are equal, so concentrations and observable properties stay constant, though both reactions continue.
- Constant does not mean equal: equilibrium concentrations are usually different from each other, and not in the ratio of the coefficients.
- The same equilibrium can be reached from the reactants or the products. Equilibria can be phase, solubility or chemical reaction equilibria.
- The **equilibrium position** says whether reactants or products are favoured.
- On a **concentration–time graph**, equilibrium starts where the curves become flat; the changes in concentration are in the ratio of the coefficients.
- On a **rate–time graph**, the forward rate falls and the reverse rate rises until they meet; equilibrium is reached where they become equal.
:::
