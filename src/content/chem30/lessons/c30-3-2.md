Two bottles in a chemical store are both labelled $\ce{C6H14}$. One holds a liquid that boils at $69^\circ\text{C}$, the other one that boils at $50^\circ\text{C}$: they are different compounds with the same formula. A formula is not enough to say which compound is meant, so chemists everywhere use one set of naming rules, agreed by IUPAC, the International Union of Pure and Applied Chemistry. From a name you can draw the structure, and from a structure you can work out the one name that belongs to it.

In this lesson you will name straight-chain and branched alkanes, draw them from their names, and name alkenes and alkynes.

## Straight-chain alkanes

The name of an alkane has two parts: a **stem** that gives the number of carbon atoms in the chain, and the ending **-ane**.

| Carbons | Stem | Alkane | Condensed formula | Molecular formula |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | meth- | methane | $\ce{CH4}$ | $\ce{CH4}$ |
| 2 | eth- | ethane | $\ce{CH3-CH3}$ | $\ce{C2H6}$ |
| 3 | prop- | propane | $\ce{CH3-CH2-CH3}$ | $\ce{C3H8}$ |
| 4 | but- | butane | $\ce{CH3-CH2-CH2-CH3}$ | $\ce{C4H10}$ |
| 5 | pent- | pentane | $\ce{CH3-(CH2)3-CH3}$ | $\ce{C5H12}$ |
| 6 | hex- | hexane | $\ce{CH3-(CH2)4-CH3}$ | $\ce{C6H14}$ |
| 7 | hept- | heptane | $\ce{CH3-(CH2)5-CH3}$ | $\ce{C7H16}$ |
| 8 | oct- | octane | $\ce{CH3-(CH2)6-CH3}$ | $\ce{C8H18}$ |
| 9 | non- | nonane | $\ce{CH3-(CH2)7-CH3}$ | $\ce{C9H20}$ |
| 10 | dec- | decane | $\ce{CH3-(CH2)8-CH3}$ | $\ce{C10H22}$ |

From pentane on, the stems are the Greek number words. A run of $\ce{CH2}$ groups can be shortened: $\ce{CH3-(CH2)4-CH3}$ means four $\ce{CH2}$ groups in a row.

## Branched alkanes

:::definition Parent chain
The **parent chain** is the longest continuous chain of carbon atoms in the molecule. It gives the stem of the name. The longest chain does not have to be written in a straight line: it can bend through a branch.
:::

:::definition Alkyl group
An **alkyl group** is an alkane with one hydrogen atom removed, so it can join to a chain: methyl, $\ce{-CH3}$; ethyl, $\ce{-CH2CH3}$; propyl, $\ce{-CH2CH2CH3}$. The ending -ane becomes -yl.
:::

:::definition Substituent
A **substituent** is an atom or group that takes the place of a hydrogen atom on the parent chain, such as an alkyl group or a halogen atom. Each substituent is named as a prefix, with the number of the carbon it is on.
:::

To name a branched alkane:

1. **Find the parent chain**, the longest continuous chain, and name it.
2. **Number the chain** from the end nearer the first branch, so the numbers are as low as possible.
3. **Name each branch** with the number of its carbon. Use di-, tri- or tetra- for two, three or four of the same branch, and give each one a number: 2,2-dimethyl.
4. **List the branches in alphabetical order**, ignoring di-, tri- and tetra-: ethyl comes before methyl.
5. **Write it as one word**: commas between numbers, hyphens between numbers and letters, and the parent name joined straight on: 3-ethyl-2-methylhexane.

:::example One branch
Name $\ce{CH3-CH2-CH2-CH(CH3)-CH3}$.
@step The longest chain has 5 carbons: the parent is pentane.
@step The branch is nearer the right-hand end, so number from the right. The methyl group is on carbon 2 (numbering from the left would put it on carbon 4).
@answer 2-methylpentane
:::

:::mistake A chain that bends
$\ce{CH3-CH(CH2CH3)-CH3}$ looks like propane with an ethyl branch, "2-ethylpropane". But the longest chain runs from one $\ce{CH3}$ through the middle carbon and on into the ethyl group: four carbons. With the other $\ce{CH3}$ as a methyl branch on carbon 2, the compound is 2-methylbutane. An ethyl group on carbon 2, or a methyl group on carbon 1, always means the chain is longer than it looks.
:::

:::mistake Numbering from the wrong end
"4-methylpentane" numbers the chain from the end farther from the branch. Number from the nearer end and the same compound is 2-methylpentane. Always check both directions, and choose the one that gives the lower number at the first point of difference.
:::

## Drawing a structure from its name

Work from the end of the name back to the start:

1. Draw the parent chain and number its carbons.
2. Attach each branch to the carbon its number gives.
3. Add hydrogens so that every carbon has four bonds.

:::example Drawing 2,3-dimethylpentane
Write the condensed formula and the molecular formula of 2,3-dimethylpentane.
@step Pentane: a chain of five carbons, $\ce{C-C-C-C-C}$.
@step A methyl group on carbon 2 and another on carbon 3.
@step Fill in the hydrogens: $\ce{CH3-CH(CH3)-CH(CH3)-CH2-CH3}$
@answer $\ce{CH3-CH(CH3)-CH(CH3)-CH2-CH3}$, which is $\ce{C7H16}$.
:::

:::

## Alkenes and alkynes

The name of an alkene ends in **-ene**, and the name of an alkyne in **-yne**. The multiple bond decides how the chain is chosen and numbered:

1. The parent chain is the longest chain that **contains the multiple bond**.
2. Number the chain from the end **nearer the multiple bond**.
3. The position of the bond is the lower of the two numbers of its carbons. Write it in front of -ene or -yne: but-2-ene has its double bond between carbons 2 and 3. An older style puts the number in front of the whole name, 2-butene, and you will see both.
4. Then number and name the branches as for an alkane, using the same numbering.

Ethene, $\ce{CH2=CH2}$, propene, $\ce{CH2=CH-CH3}$, ethyne, $\ce{CH#CH}$, and propyne, $\ce{CH#C-CH3}$, need no number: their multiple bond can only be in one place.

:::example Numbering from the nearer end
Name $\ce{CH3-CH2-CH=CH-CH3}$.
@step The chain has 5 carbons with a double bond: a pentene.
@step The double bond is nearer the right-hand end. Numbering from the right, it joins carbons 2 and 3.
@answer pent-2-ene (or 2-pentene), not pent-3-ene.
:::

:::check c30-name-unsaturated 1
:::

:::example An alkene with a branch
Name $\ce{CH2=CH-CH2-CH(CH3)-CH3}$.
@step The longest chain holding the double bond has 5 carbons: a pentene.
@step Number from the left, nearer the double bond: the double bond starts at carbon 1, so the parent is pent-1-ene.
@step In that numbering the methyl group is on carbon 4.
@answer 4-methylpent-1-ene
:::

:::mistake The bond comes first
Numbering $\ce{CH2=CH-CH2-CH(CH3)-CH3}$ from the right gives the methyl group the lower number, 2, but puts the double bond at 4: "2-methylpent-4-ene" is wrong. The multiple bond always gets the lowest number possible; the branches take whatever numbers that leaves.
:::

:::example An alkyne with a branch
Name $\ce{CH#C-CH(CH3)-CH3}$.
@step The longest chain holding the triple bond has 4 carbons: a butyne.
@step Number from the left, nearer the triple bond: but-1-yne, with a methyl group on carbon 3.
@answer 3-methylbut-1-yne
:::

:::check c30-name-unsaturated 2
:::

Drawing an alkene or alkyne from its name works the same way: draw the chain, put the multiple bond after the carbon its number gives, add the branches, then the hydrogens. For hex-3-yne, the triple bond joins carbons 3 and 4: $\ce{CH3-CH2-C#C-CH2-CH3}$, which is $\ce{C6H10}$.

:::check c30-name-unsaturated 3
:::

:::summary
- Stems: meth 1, eth 2, prop 3, but 4, pent 5, hex 6, hept 7, oct 8, non 9, dec 10. Alkanes end in -ane, alkenes in -ene, alkynes in -yne.
- The **parent chain** is the longest continuous chain (for an alkene or alkyne, the longest one containing the multiple bond). It may bend through a branch.
- Number from the end nearer the multiple bond, if there is one, and otherwise nearer the first branch.
- Branches are **alkyl groups** (methyl, ethyl, propyl), each with its carbon number; di-, tri- and tetra- count repeats, and the prefixes go in alphabetical order.
- Commas between numbers, hyphens between numbers and letters: 3-ethyl-2-methylhexane, 4-methylpent-1-ene (or 4-methyl-1-pentene).
- To draw from a name: chain, multiple bond, branches, then hydrogens.
:::
