A zinc–copper cell makes about $1.1\ \text{V}$, a lead-acid cell in a car battery about $2\ \text{V}$, and a lithium cell more than $3\ \text{V}$. The voltage of a cell measures how strongly the cathode pulls electrons compared with the anode, and it can be predicted from a table before the cell is ever built.

In this lesson you will calculate standard cell potentials from standard reduction potentials, and use the sign of a cell potential to decide whether a redox reaction is spontaneous.

## Standard reduction potentials

A voltmeter can only measure the difference between two half-cells, never one half-cell on its own. So chemists choose one half-cell as a reference and give it a potential of exactly zero.

:::definition Standard hydrogen electrode
The **standard hydrogen electrode** is a platinum electrode in a solution of $1.0\ \text{mol/L}$ hydrogen ions, with hydrogen gas at $100\ \text{kPa}$ bubbling over it, at $25^\circ\text{C}$. Its half-reaction, $\ce{2H+(aq) + 2e- -> H2(g)}$, is given a potential of exactly $0.00\ \text{V}$.
:::

:::definition Standard reduction potential
The **standard reduction potential**, $E^\circ$, of a half-cell is the voltage it produces with a standard hydrogen electrode, under standard conditions: $1.0\ \text{mol/L}$ solutions, gases at $100\ \text{kPa}$, and $25^\circ\text{C}$. It is positive when the half-cell's oxidising agent is stronger than hydrogen ions, and negative when it is weaker.
:::

In this cell, zinc is oxidised and hydrogen ions are reduced, and the voltmeter reads $0.76\ \text{V}$. Zinc ions are weaker oxidising agents than hydrogen ions, so the zinc half-cell has $E^\circ = -0.76\ \text{V}$. Measuring every half-cell this way builds the redox table of lesson 2.3.

## Calculating a cell potential

The potential of a cell is the difference between the reduction potentials of its two half-cells.

:::definition Standard cell potential
The **standard cell potential** is $E^\circ_\text{cell} = E^\circ_\text{cathode} - E^\circ_\text{anode}$, where both values are standard **reduction** potentials, exactly as the table gives them.
:::

On a number line of reduction potentials, the cell potential is the distance from the anode up to the cathode.

:::figure

:::

:::example The zinc–copper cell
What is the standard cell potential of $\ce{Zn(s)} \mid \ce{Zn^2+(aq)} \parallel \ce{Cu^2+(aq)} \mid \ce{Cu(s)}$? Use $\ce{Cu^2+(aq) + 2e- -> Cu(s)}$, $E^\circ = +0.34\ \text{V}$, and $\ce{Zn^2+(aq) + 2e- -> Zn(s)}$, $E^\circ = -0.76\ \text{V}$.
@step Copper(II) ions are reduced at the cathode, and zinc is oxidised at the anode.
@step $E^\circ_\text{cell} = E^\circ_\text{cathode} - E^\circ_\text{anode} = +0.34 - (-0.76)$
@answer $E^\circ_\text{cell} = +1.10\ \text{V}$
:::

:::

When you are given two half-cells but not told which is which, the one higher in the table is the cathode: its oxidising agent is the stronger, so it is reduced. A voltaic cell always has a positive potential.

:::example A silver–aluminium cell
A standard cell is made from an aluminium half-cell, $\ce{Al^3+(aq) + 3e- -> Al(s)}$, $E^\circ = -1.66\ \text{V}$, and a silver half-cell, $\ce{Ag^+(aq) + e- -> Ag(s)}$, $E^\circ = +0.80\ \text{V}$. What is its standard cell potential?
@step Silver is higher in the table, so silver ions are reduced at the cathode; aluminium is oxidised at the anode.
@step The cell reaction is $\ce{Al(s) + 3Ag^+(aq) -> Al^3+(aq) + 3Ag(s)}$, but the potentials are not multiplied by the coefficients.
@step $E^\circ_\text{cell} = +0.80 - (-1.66)$
@answer $E^\circ_\text{cell} = +2.46\ \text{V}$
:::

:::mistake Multiplying potentials by coefficients
A potential measures how strongly each electron is pushed, not how many electrons move, so it does not change when a half-reaction is multiplied. Working out $3(+0.80) - (-1.66) = 4.06\ \text{V}$ for the silver–aluminium cell is wrong. Do not change the sign of the anode's potential by hand either: the subtraction in the formula does that.
:::

:::check c30-cell-potential 2
:::

The same formula can be run backwards to find an unknown reduction potential from a measured cell potential.

:::example An unknown metal
A cell is made from a standard copper half-cell ($E^\circ = +0.34\ \text{V}$) and a half-cell of a metal M in a solution of $\ce{M^2+}$ ions. The cell potential is $0.74\ \text{V}$, and electrons flow through the wire from M to copper. What is $E^\circ$ for $\ce{M^2+(aq) + 2e- -> M(s)}$?
@step Electrons leave the anode, so M is the anode and copper is the cathode.
@step $0.74 = +0.34 - E^\circ_\text{M}$
@answer $E^\circ_\text{M} = 0.34 - 0.74 = -0.40\ \text{V}$, the value for cadmium.
:::

## Potentials and spontaneity

Any redox reaction can be treated as a cell, whether or not anyone builds one. For the reaction as written, the species reduced is at the cathode and the species oxidised is at the anode, and $E^\circ = E^\circ_\text{cathode} - E^\circ_\text{anode}$.

:::definition Spontaneity from the cell potential
A redox reaction is **spontaneous** when its $E^\circ$ is **positive**. When $E^\circ$ is **negative**, the reaction is non-spontaneous: its reverse is the spontaneous one, and it runs only if energy is supplied, as in the electrolytic cells of lesson 2.8.
:::

This is the rule of lesson 2.3 in numbers: an oxidising agent above the reducing agent in the table means a cathode potential higher than the anode potential, and a positive difference.

:::example Copper in acid
Is $\ce{Cu(s) + 2H+(aq) -> Cu^2+(aq) + H2(g)}$ spontaneous? Use $E^\circ = +0.34\ \text{V}$ for $\ce{Cu^2+/Cu}$ and $0.00\ \text{V}$ for $\ce{H+/H2}$.
@step Hydrogen ions are reduced (cathode); copper is oxidised (anode).
@step $E^\circ = 0.00 - (+0.34) = -0.34\ \text{V}$
@answer The potential is negative, so the reaction is non-spontaneous: copper does not dissolve in acids such as hydrochloric acid.
:::

:::check c30-cell-spontaneous 1
:::

:::example Permanganate and chloride
Is $\ce{2MnO4^-(aq) + 10Cl^-(aq) + 16H+(aq) -> 2Mn^2+(aq) + 5Cl2(g) + 8H2O(l)}$ spontaneous? Use $E^\circ = +1.51\ \text{V}$ for the permanganate half-reaction and $+1.36\ \text{V}$ for $\ce{Cl2/Cl-}$.
@step Permanganate is reduced (cathode); chloride ions are oxidised (anode).
@step $E^\circ = +1.51 - (+1.36) = +0.15\ \text{V}$
@answer Positive, so it is spontaneous. This is why permanganate titrations are acidified with sulfuric acid, not hydrochloric acid.
:::

:::note Not standard conditions
Real cells rarely run at standard conditions. As a battery is used, the concentrations in it change and its voltage slowly falls. Standard potentials describe the start, with every solution at $1.0\ \text{mol/L}$.
:::

:::summary
- Reduction potentials are measured against the **standard hydrogen electrode**, defined as $0.00\ \text{V}$, at $1.0\ \text{mol/L}$, $100\ \text{kPa}$ and $25^\circ\text{C}$.
- $E^\circ_\text{cell} = E^\circ_\text{cathode} - E^\circ_\text{anode}$, using reduction potentials straight from the table. Never multiply a potential by a coefficient.
- In a voltaic cell the half-cell higher in the table is the cathode, and $E^\circ_\text{cell}$ is positive.
- A redox reaction is **spontaneous** when $E^\circ$ for the reaction as written is positive, and non-spontaneous when it is negative.
:::
