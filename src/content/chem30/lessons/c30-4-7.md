Vinegar is roughly a $0.8\ \text{mol/L}$ solution of acetic acid, yet its pH is about 2.4. A hydrochloric acid solution of the same concentration would have a pH of about 0.1, more than two hundred times as much hydronium ion. Because a weak acid ionises only partly, its pH cannot be read straight from its concentration: it has to be worked out from the equilibrium.

In this lesson you will calculate the pH of solutions of weak acids and weak bases from their concentration and $K_a$ or $K_b$, and work backwards from a measured pH to find $K_a$ and the percent ionisation.

## Setting up the calculation

For a weak acid HA with an initial concentration $c$, an ICE table (lesson 4.2) gives the equilibrium concentrations, with $x = [\ce{H3O+}]$:

| | $[\ce{HA}]$ | $[\ce{H3O+}]$ | $[\ce{A-}]$ |
|---|---|---|---|
| Initial | $c$ | 0 | 0 |
| Change | $-x$ | $+x$ | $+x$ |
| Equilibrium | $c - x$ | $x$ | $x$ |

(Pure water contains a tiny $1.0 \times 10^{-7}\ \text{mol/L}$ of $\ce{H3O+}$, far too little to matter here.) Substituting into the $K_a$ expression gives
$$K_a = \dfrac{x^2}{c - x}$$

## The approximation

For most weak acids, so little ionises that $x$ is tiny compared with $c$. Then $c - x \approx c$, and the equation becomes simple:
$$K_a \approx \dfrac{x^2}{c} \qquad\text{so}\qquad x = [\ce{H3O+}] = \sqrt{K_ac}$$

:::definition The approximation for weak acids and bases
When the ionisation is small, the concentration of the un-ionised acid (or base) is taken to stay at its initial value: $c - x \approx c$. As a rule, it works well when $c$ is more than 1000 times $K_a$; then less than about 3% ionises, and the pH comes out within 0.01 of the exact value. Questions say when to use it.
:::

:::example pH of a weak acid
What is the pH of a $0.100\ \text{mol/L}$ solution of acetic acid, to two decimal places? At $25^\circ\text{C}$, $K_a = 1.8 \times 10^{-5}$. Use the approximation.
@step $\ce{CH3COOH(aq) + H2O(l) <=> H3O+(aq) + CH3COO-(aq)}$, and $K_a = \dfrac{x^2}{0.100 - x} \approx \dfrac{x^2}{0.100}$
@step $x = \sqrt{1.8 \times 10^{-5} \times 0.100} = 1.34 \times 10^{-3}\ \text{mol/L}$
@step $\text{pH} = -\log(1.34 \times 10^{-3}) = 2.87$
@answer $\text{pH} = 2.87$. Check: $x$ is 1.3% of $0.100$, so the approximation was fine.
:::

## Weak bases

A weak base works the same way, except that it makes hydroxide ions. With $x = [\ce{OH-}]$:
$$K_b = \dfrac{x^2}{c - x} \approx \dfrac{x^2}{c} \qquad\text{so}\qquad [\ce{OH-}] = \sqrt{K_bc}$$
Then find the pOH, and the pH from $\text{pH} + \text{pOH} = 14.00$ at $25^\circ\text{C}$.

:::example pH of a weak base
What is the pH of a $0.200\ \text{mol/L}$ solution of ammonia, to two decimal places? At $25^\circ\text{C}$, $K_b = 1.8 \times 10^{-5}$. Use the approximation.
@step $\ce{NH3(aq) + H2O(l) <=> NH4+(aq) + OH-(aq)}$
@step $[\ce{OH-}] = \sqrt{1.8 \times 10^{-5} \times 0.200} = 1.90 \times 10^{-3}\ \text{mol/L}$
@step $\text{pOH} = -\log(1.90 \times 10^{-3}) = 2.72$
@answer $\text{pH} = 14.00 - 2.72 = 11.28$
:::

:::mistake Stopping at the pOH
A weak base calculation gives $[\ce{OH-}]$ first, and $-\log[\ce{OH-}]$ is the **pOH**. A basic solution must have a pH above 7: $2.72$ cannot be the pH of ammonia. Finish with $\text{pH} = 14.00 - \text{pOH}$.
:::

## Salt solutions and the quadratic

The salt of a weak acid, such as sodium acetate, makes a basic solution: the acetate ion is a weak base, with $K_b = \dfrac{K_w}{K_a}$ (lesson 4.6). The salt of a weak base, such as ammonium chloride, makes an acidic solution: the ammonium ion is a weak acid, with $K_a = \dfrac{K_w}{K_b}$.

:::example A basic salt
What is the pH of a $0.100\ \text{mol/L}$ solution of sodium acetate, to two decimal places? At $25^\circ\text{C}$, $K_a$ for acetic acid is $1.8 \times 10^{-5}$ and $K_w = 1.0 \times 10^{-14}$. Use the approximation.
@step The base is $\ce{CH3COO-}$: $\ce{CH3COO-(aq) + H2O(l) <=> CH3COOH(aq) + OH-(aq)}$. $\ce{Na+}$ is a spectator.
@step $K_b = \dfrac{1.0 \times 10^{-14}}{1.8 \times 10^{-5}} = 5.56 \times 10^{-10}$
@step $[\ce{OH-}] = \sqrt{5.56 \times 10^{-10} \times 0.100} = 7.45 \times 10^{-6}\ \text{mol/L}$, so $\text{pOH} = 5.13$
@answer $\text{pH} = 14.00 - 5.13 = 8.87$: slightly basic.
:::

:::example An acidic salt
What is the pH of a $0.250\ \text{mol/L}$ solution of ammonium chloride, to two decimal places? At $25^\circ\text{C}$, $K_b$ for ammonia is $1.8 \times 10^{-5}$ and $K_w = 1.0 \times 10^{-14}$. Use the approximation.
@step The acid is $\ce{NH4+}$: $\ce{NH4+(aq) + H2O(l) <=> NH3(aq) + H3O+(aq)}$. $\ce{Cl-}$ is too weak a base to matter.
@step $K_a = \dfrac{1.0 \times 10^{-14}}{1.8 \times 10^{-5}} = 5.56 \times 10^{-10}$
@step $[\ce{H3O+}] = \sqrt{5.56 \times 10^{-10} \times 0.250} = 1.18 \times 10^{-5}\ \text{mol/L}$
@answer $\text{pH} = -\log(1.18 \times 10^{-5}) = 4.93$: slightly acidic.
:::

When an acid is fairly strong or very dilute, too much of it ionises for the approximation. Keep $c - x$, rearrange into a quadratic equation, and solve it:
$$x^2 + K_ax - K_ac = 0 \qquad\text{so}\qquad x = \dfrac{-K_a + \sqrt{K_a^2 + 4K_ac}}{2}$$
(The other root of the quadratic is negative, and a concentration cannot be negative.)

:::example When the approximation fails
What is the pH of a $0.0100\ \text{mol/L}$ solution of hydrofluoric acid, to two decimal places? At $25^\circ\text{C}$, $K_a = 6.6 \times 10^{-4}$. Solve the quadratic.
@step $6.6 \times 10^{-4} = \dfrac{x^2}{0.0100 - x}$, so $x^2 + 6.6 \times 10^{-4}x - 6.6 \times 10^{-6} = 0$
@step $x = \dfrac{-6.6 \times 10^{-4} + \sqrt{(6.6 \times 10^{-4})^2 + 4(6.6 \times 10^{-6})}}{2} = 2.26 \times 10^{-3}\ \text{mol/L}$
@step $\text{pH} = -\log(2.26 \times 10^{-3}) = 2.65$
@answer $\text{pH} = 2.65$. The approximation would have given 2.59: 23% of this acid ionises, far too much to ignore.
:::

## Ka and percent ionisation from the pH

A pH meter measures $[\ce{H3O+}]$ directly, so measuring the pH of a weak acid of known concentration gives $x$, and from it $K_a$.

:::definition Percent ionisation
The **percent ionisation** of a weak acid is the fraction of its molecules that have ionised:
$$\text{percent ionisation} = \dfrac{[\ce{H3O+}]_{eq}}{c_{\text{initial}}} \times 100\%$$
:::

:::example Ka from a measured pH
A $0.100\ \text{mol/L}$ solution of methanoic acid, $\ce{HCOOH}$, has a pH of 2.38 at $25^\circ\text{C}$. Find $K_a$, to two significant figures, and the percent ionisation, to three significant figures.
@step $x = [\ce{H3O+}] = [\ce{HCOO-}] = 10^{-2.38} = 4.17 \times 10^{-3}\ \text{mol/L}$
@step $[\ce{HCOOH}] = 0.100 - 0.00417 = 0.0958\ \text{mol/L}$
@step $K_a = \dfrac{(4.17 \times 10^{-3})^2}{0.0958} = 1.8 \times 10^{-4}$
@answer $K_a = 1.8 \times 10^{-4}$; percent ionisation $= \dfrac{4.17 \times 10^{-3}}{0.100} \times 100\% = 4.17\%$
:::

:::tip Keep the extra digits
Carry the unrounded $[\ce{H3O+}]$ through to the end. Rounding $4.17 \times 10^{-3}$ to $4 \times 10^{-3}$ before squaring changes $K_a$ by about 8%.
:::

Percent ionisation depends on the concentration as well as on $K_a$: diluting a weak acid makes a **larger** fraction of it ionise. A $0.100\ \text{mol/L}$ acetic acid solution is 1.3% ionised, but a $0.0100\ \text{mol/L}$ solution is 4.2% ionised, as Le Châtelier's principle predicts: diluting favours the side with more dissolved particles.

:::example Working backwards from the percent ionisation
In a $0.500\ \text{mol/L}$ solution of acetic acid, 0.600% of the acid molecules are ionised at $25^\circ\text{C}$. What are the pH, to two decimal places, and $K_a$, to two significant figures?
@step $x = [\ce{H3O+}] = \dfrac{0.600}{100} \times 0.500 = 3.00 \times 10^{-3}\ \text{mol/L}$
@step $\text{pH} = -\log(3.00 \times 10^{-3}) = 2.52$
@answer $\text{pH} = 2.52$ and $K_a = \dfrac{(3.00 \times 10^{-3})^2}{0.500 - 0.00300} = 1.8 \times 10^{-5}$
:::

:::summary
- For a weak acid, an ICE table gives $K_a = \dfrac{x^2}{c - x}$ with $x = [\ce{H3O+}]$; for a weak base, $K_b = \dfrac{x^2}{c - x}$ with $x = [\ce{OH-}]$.
- **The approximation**: when little ionises, $c - x \approx c$, so $[\ce{H3O+}] = \sqrt{K_ac}$ or $[\ce{OH-}] = \sqrt{K_bc}$. For a base, find the pOH and then $\text{pH} = 14.00 - \text{pOH}$.
- The anion of a weak acid is a weak base ($K_b = K_w/K_a$); the cation of a weak base is a weak acid ($K_a = K_w/K_b$).
- When too much ionises for the approximation, solve $x^2 + K_ax - K_ac = 0$ and keep the positive root.
- From a measured pH: $x = 10^{-\text{pH}}$, $K_a = \dfrac{x^2}{c - x}$, and percent ionisation $= \dfrac{x}{c} \times 100\%$. Diluting a weak acid raises its percent ionisation.
:::
