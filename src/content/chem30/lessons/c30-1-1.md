Light a camping stove, set a pot of water on it, and within minutes the water boils. The energy that heats it was not sitting in the flame. It was stored in the propane and the oxygen, and the reaction between them released it. Other changes run the other way: an instant cold pack turns icy because the substances inside take energy from everything around them.

In this lesson you will describe a chemical system and its surroundings, and the kinds of energy they hold. You will classify changes as **exothermic** or **endothermic** from what you observe, and explain the energy change of a reaction in terms of the bonds that break and the bonds that form.

## Systems and surroundings

To follow an energy change, chemists divide the world in two.

:::definition Chemical system
The **chemical system** is the set of substances being studied: the reactants and the products they form.
:::

:::definition Surroundings
The **surroundings** are everything else that can exchange energy with the system: the water the chemicals are dissolved in, the container, the thermometer and the air around them.
:::

When zinc powder is stirred into copper(II) sulfate solution in a polystyrene cup, the system is the zinc atoms and copper(II) ions that react, and the zinc ions and copper metal they form. The water that holds the ions in solution belongs to the surroundings, along with the cup, the thermometer and the air. A thermometer in the solution therefore reads the temperature of the surroundings.

Systems are also described by what can cross their boundary:

| Kind of system | Matter crosses the boundary? | Energy crosses the boundary? | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| open | yes | yes | zinc reacting with acid in an open beaker, hydrogen bubbling away |
| closed | no | yes | a reaction in a sealed flask that becomes warm |
| isolated | no | no | an ideal sealed and perfectly insulated container |

No real container is perfectly isolated, but a good calorimeter (lesson 1.2) comes close.

:::example Naming the system and the surroundings
Methane burns in a laboratory burner under a metal can of water. Name the chemical system, and three parts of the surroundings.
@step The system is the substances that react and form: methane and oxygen, and the carbon dioxide and water vapour they make.
@step Everything else that can exchange energy with them is the surroundings.
@answer System: $\ce{CH4}$, $\ce{O2}$, $\ce{CO2}$ and $\ce{H2O}$ vapour. Surroundings: for example the water in the can, the can itself and the air in the room.
:::

## Kinetic and potential energy

The energy of a system comes in two kinds.

:::definition Kinetic energy
**Kinetic energy** is the energy of motion. The particles of a substance move from place to place, spin and vibrate. Temperature measures their **average** kinetic energy: the hotter a substance, the faster its particles move.
:::

:::definition Potential energy
**Potential energy** is stored energy that depends on position. In chemistry it is held in the chemical bonds within particles and in the attractions between particles. It changes when bonds break and form, or when particles move apart or come together.
:::

:::definition Enthalpy
The **enthalpy**, $H$, of a system is its total kinetic and potential energy at constant pressure. The total enthalpy of a substance cannot be measured, but its change, $\Delta H$, can: it is the energy the system gains or loses.
:::

In a reaction, the atoms of the reactants are rearranged into the products, so the chemical potential energy of the system changes. Energy is conserved: whatever potential energy the system loses becomes kinetic energy of the particles around it, and whatever it gains comes from them.

A change of state shows the two kinds of energy apart. While ice melts at $0^\circ\text{C}$, the temperature stays at $0^\circ\text{C}$, so the average kinetic energy of the water molecules does not change. The energy absorbed pulls the molecules partly apart against their attractions, which raises their potential energy.

:::note Energy in a stove
Before propane burns, the energy it will release is potential energy, held in the bonds and the arrangement of the propane and oxygen molecules. Burning rearranges the atoms into carbon dioxide and water, which have less potential energy. The difference raises the kinetic energy of the hot gases in the flame, and then of the pot and the water.
:::

## Exothermic and endothermic changes

:::definition Exothermic
An **exothermic** change releases energy from the system to the surroundings. The surroundings get warmer, and $\Delta H$ is negative.
:::

:::definition Endothermic
An **endothermic** change absorbs energy from the surroundings into the system. The surroundings get colder, or the change keeps going only while energy is supplied, and $\Delta H$ is positive.
:::

You can classify a change from what you observe:

| Observation | Energy flows | The change is |
|---|---|---|
| The solution, the container or the air warms up | out of the system | exothermic |
| The change gives out heat and light, as burning does | out of the system | exothermic |
| The solution or the container cools down | into the system | endothermic |
| The change stops when heating, light or electricity stops | into the system | endothermic |

Changes of state follow the same rule. Melting, evaporating and subliming absorb energy, so they are endothermic. Freezing and condensing release energy, so they are exothermic.

:::example Reading the surroundings
Ammonium nitrate is dissolved in water in a polystyrene cup, and the temperature of the water falls from $21.4^\circ\text{C}$ to $14.9^\circ\text{C}$. Is the change exothermic or endothermic, and what is the sign of $\Delta H$?
@step The thermometer is in the water, which is part of the surroundings. The surroundings got colder.
@step So energy flowed from the water into the dissolving ammonium nitrate, the system.
@answer Endothermic, with a positive $\Delta H$.
:::

:::mistake A cold pack does not give out cold
"Cold" is not something that flows. An instant cold pack feels cold because the dissolving solid takes energy **from** your skin. Always describe the flow of energy: into the system for an endothermic change, out of the system for an exothermic one.
:::

## Bonds broken and bonds formed

Why do some changes release energy and others absorb it? A reaction breaks the bonds of the reactants and forms the bonds of the products.

- **Breaking a bond absorbs energy**: the atoms must be pulled apart against the attraction that holds them.
- **Forming a bond releases energy**: the atoms pull together.

The enthalpy change is the balance of the two:

$$\Delta H = \text{energy absorbed breaking bonds} - \text{energy released forming bonds}$$

If forming the new bonds releases more energy than breaking the old ones absorbs, $\Delta H$ is negative and the reaction is exothermic. If breaking the old bonds absorbs more, $\Delta H$ is positive and the reaction is endothermic.

The **bond energy** is the energy needed to break one mole of a particular bond. Tables give average values, such as $436\ \text{kJ/mol}$ for $\ce{H-H}$, $243\ \text{kJ/mol}$ for $\ce{Cl-Cl}$ and $432\ \text{kJ/mol}$ for $\ce{H-Cl}$. :::example Hydrogen and chlorine
Use the bond energies $\ce{H-H}$ $436\ \text{kJ/mol}$, $\ce{Cl-Cl}$ $243\ \text{kJ/mol}$ and $\ce{H-Cl}$ $432\ \text{kJ/mol}$ to estimate $\Delta_r H$ for $\ce{H2(g) + Cl2(g) -> 2HCl(g)}$.
@step Bonds broken: one $\ce{H-H}$ and one $\ce{Cl-Cl}$, so $436 + 243 = 679\ \text{kJ}$ is absorbed.
@step Bonds formed: two $\ce{H-Cl}$, so $2(432) = 864\ \text{kJ}$ is released.
@step $\Delta_r H = 679 - 864 = -185\ \text{kJ}$
@answer $\Delta_r H \approx -185\ \text{kJ}$. More energy is released than absorbed, so the reaction is exothermic.
:::

:::note Bond energies are averages
The energy of a bond such as $\ce{C-H}$ differs a little from one molecule to another, so tables give averages and the result is an estimate. Measured by experiment, this reaction has $\Delta_r H = -184.6\ \text{kJ}$, very close to the estimate.
:::

:::example Making ammonia
Each $\ce{N2}$ molecule has one $\ce{N#N}$ triple bond ($945\ \text{kJ/mol}$), each $\ce{H2}$ has one $\ce{H-H}$ bond ($436\ \text{kJ/mol}$) and each $\ce{NH3}$ has three $\ce{N-H}$ bonds ($391\ \text{kJ/mol}$). Estimate $\Delta_r H$ for $\ce{N2(g) + 3H2(g) -> 2NH3(g)}$.
@step Bonds broken: one $\ce{N#N}$ and three $\ce{H-H}$, so $945 + 3(436) = 2253\ \text{kJ}$ is absorbed.
@step Bonds formed: two $\ce{NH3}$ molecules have $2 \times 3 = 6$ $\ce{N-H}$ bonds, so $6(391) = 2346\ \text{kJ}$ is released.
@step $\Delta_r H = 2253 - 2346 = -93\ \text{kJ}$
@answer $\Delta_r H \approx -93\ \text{kJ}$: exothermic.
:::

:::mistake Breaking bonds never releases energy
It is tempting to say that a fuel "releases the energy stored in its bonds" as they break. Breaking any bond **absorbs** energy. A fuel releases energy because the bonds of its products, carbon dioxide and water, are stronger than the bonds broken in the fuel and oxygen, so forming them releases more energy than breaking the old bonds took.
:::

:::summary
- The **chemical system** is the reacting substances; the **surroundings** are everything else, including the water they are dissolved in. An open system exchanges matter and energy, a closed system only energy, and an isolated system neither.
- **Kinetic energy** is the energy of moving particles, and temperature measures its average. **Potential energy** is held in bonds and in the attractions between particles. **Enthalpy**, $H$, is the total; only its change, $\Delta H$, can be measured.
- **Exothermic**: energy flows out of the system, the surroundings warm up and $\Delta H$ is negative. **Endothermic**: energy flows in, the surroundings cool down or energy must be supplied, and $\Delta H$ is positive.
- Breaking bonds absorbs energy and forming bonds releases it: $\Delta H$ = energy absorbed − energy released.
:::
