In a common laboratory demonstration, a clean iron nail is placed in blue copper(II) sulfate solution. Within minutes the nail wears a coat of reddish-brown copper, and the blue colour of the solution slowly fades. Iron rusting, a battery running a torch, bleach whitening a shirt and the reactions that release energy from food in living cells are the same kind of chemistry: electrons move from one substance to another.

In this lesson you will see what oxidation and reduction mean, identify the substances oxidised and reduced and the oxidising and reducing agents, and split a redox reaction into its two half-reactions.

## Oxidation and reduction

The words are older than the electron. Oxidation first meant combining with oxygen, as when magnesium burns: $\ce{2Mg + O2 -> 2MgO}$. Reduction meant taking the oxygen out of a metal ore to leave the metal, as in a blast furnace, where the solid loses mass: it is "reduced".

Magnesium oxide is an ionic compound of $\ce{Mg^2+}$ and $\ce{O^2-}$ ions, so when magnesium burns, each magnesium atom gives two electrons to an oxygen atom. Chemists now define oxidation and reduction by electrons, which covers many reactions with no oxygen at all.

:::definition Oxidation
**Oxidation** is a loss of electrons. A substance that loses electrons is **oxidised**.
:::

:::definition Reduction
**Reduction** is a gain of electrons. A substance that gains electrons is **reduced**.
:::

:::definition Redox reaction
A **redox reaction** is a reaction in which electrons are transferred from one substance to another. Oxidation and reduction always happen together: the electrons lost by one substance are gained by another.
:::

:::tip OIL RIG
**O**xidation **I**s **L**oss, **R**eduction **I**s **G**ain (of electrons).
:::

The iron nail in copper(II) sulfate is a redox reaction:

$$\ce{Fe(s) + Cu^2+(aq) -> Fe^2+(aq) + Cu(s)}$$

Each iron atom, with no charge, becomes an $\ce{Fe^2+}$ ion: it has lost two electrons, so iron is oxidised. Each copper(II) ion becomes a neutral copper atom: it has gained two electrons, so the copper(II) ions are reduced. The sulfate ions are spectators and are left out of the equation.

:::example Which is oxidised?
A strip of zinc is placed in a solution of lead(II) nitrate: $\ce{Zn(s) + Pb^2+(aq) -> Zn^2+(aq) + Pb(s)}$. Which substance is oxidised, and which is reduced?
@step Zinc atoms (charge 0) become zinc ions (charge 2+): each zinc atom loses two electrons.
@step Lead(II) ions (charge 2+) become lead atoms (charge 0): each lead(II) ion gains two electrons.
@answer Zinc is oxidised; the lead(II) ions are reduced.
:::

## Half-reactions

A redox equation hides the electrons, because every electron lost on one side is gained on the other. Writing each part separately shows them.

:::definition Half-reaction
A **half-reaction** shows either the oxidation or the reduction part of a redox reaction, with the electrons written as $\ce{e-}$. In an oxidation half-reaction the electrons are products; in a reduction half-reaction they are reactants.
:::

For the iron nail:

| Half-reaction | Equation |
|---|---|
| Oxidation | $\ce{Fe(s) -> Fe^2+(aq) + 2e-}$ |
| Reduction | $\ce{Cu^2+(aq) + 2e- -> Cu(s)}$ |

Each half-reaction balances, both in atoms and in charge. In the oxidation the charge is 0 on the left and $+2 - 2 = 0$ on the right. Adding the two half-reactions, the two electrons cancel, and the redox equation is left.

:::example Half-reactions with a diatomic element
Chlorine gas is bubbled through a solution of sodium bromide: $\ce{Cl2(g) + 2Br^-(aq) -> 2Cl^-(aq) + Br2(l)}$. Write the oxidation and the reduction half-reactions.
@step Bromide ions (charge $-1$) become bromine molecules (charge 0), so they lose electrons. Two bromide ions make one $\ce{Br2}$ molecule: $\ce{2Br^-(aq) -> Br2(l) + 2e-}$. Charge check: $-2$ on the left, $0 - 2 = -2$ on the right.
@step Chlorine molecules (charge 0) become chloride ions (charge $-1$), so they gain electrons: $\ce{Cl2(g) + 2e- -> 2Cl^-(aq)}$. Charge check: $-2$ on both sides.
@answer Oxidation: $\ce{2Br^-(aq) -> Br2(l) + 2e-}$. Reduction: $\ce{Cl2(g) + 2e- -> 2Cl^-(aq)}$.
:::

:::mistake Electrons on the wrong side
$\ce{Zn(s) + 2e- -> Zn^2+(aq)}$ does not balance: the charge is $-2$ on the left and $+2$ on the right. Zinc loses electrons, so they are products: $\ce{Zn(s) -> Zn^2+(aq) + 2e-}$. Always check that the total charge is the same on both sides.
:::

## Oxidising and reducing agents

In a redox reaction each reactant does something to the other. The one that takes electrons causes the other to be oxidised; the one that gives electrons causes the other to be reduced.

:::definition Oxidising agent
An **oxidising agent** takes electrons from another substance, causing it to be oxidised. The oxidising agent is itself **reduced**.
:::

:::definition Reducing agent
A **reducing agent** gives electrons to another substance, causing it to be reduced. The reducing agent is itself **oxidised**.
:::

| In $\ce{Fe + Cu^2+ -> Fe^2+ + Cu}$ | Electrons | Change | Role |
|---|---|---|---|
| $\ce{Fe}$ | loses 2 | oxidised | reducing agent |
| $\ce{Cu^2+}$ | gains 2 | reduced | oxidising agent |

:::example Agents in an acid
Magnesium ribbon reacts with hydrochloric acid: $\ce{Mg(s) + 2H^+(aq) -> Mg^2+(aq) + H2(g)}$. Identify the oxidising agent and the reducing agent.
@step Oxidation: $\ce{Mg(s) -> Mg^2+(aq) + 2e-}$. Magnesium loses electrons, so it is oxidised.
@step Reduction: $\ce{2H^+(aq) + 2e- -> H2(g)}$. The hydrogen ions gain electrons, so they are reduced.
@answer The oxidising agent is $\ce{H^+(aq)}$, which is reduced. The reducing agent is $\ce{Mg(s)}$, which is oxidised. The chloride ions of the acid are spectators.
:::

:::mistake The oxidising agent is not oxidised
The names describe what a substance does to the other reactant, not what happens to it. The oxidising agent oxidises the other substance and is itself reduced. Saying "copper(II) ions are the oxidising agent, so they are oxidised" mixes the two up: copper(II) ions are reduced.
:::

## Balancing the electrons

In a redox reaction the electrons lost must equal the electrons gained. When the two half-reactions transfer different numbers of electrons, each is multiplied until they match.

Aluminium foil placed in copper(II) chloride solution is oxidised to aluminium ions while copper metal forms:

| | Half-reaction | Multiply by | Electrons |
|---|---|---|---|
| Oxidation | $\ce{Al -> Al^3+ + 3e-}$ | 2 | 6 |
| Reduction | $\ce{Cu^2+ + 2e- -> Cu}$ | 3 | 6 |

Adding them and cancelling the six electrons gives $\ce{2Al(s) + 3Cu^2+(aq) -> 2Al^3+(aq) + 3Cu(s)}$, in which six electrons are transferred. Check the charge: $3 \times (+2) = +6$ on the left and $2 \times (+3) = +6$ on the right.

:::example Silver from copper
A copper wire is placed in silver nitrate solution: $\ce{Cu(s) + 2Ag^+(aq) -> Cu^2+(aq) + 2Ag(s)}$. Write the half-reactions and find the number of electrons transferred in the equation.
@step Oxidation: $\ce{Cu(s) -> Cu^2+(aq) + 2e-}$
@step Reduction: $\ce{Ag^+(aq) + e- -> Ag(s)}$. Each silver ion gains only one electron, so the reduction is used twice.
@answer Two electrons are transferred: one copper atom gives one electron to each of two silver ions.
:::

:::summary
- **Oxidation** is a loss of electrons; **reduction** is a gain of electrons (OIL RIG). They always happen together in a **redox reaction**.
- A **half-reaction** shows one part with its electrons: products in an oxidation, reactants in a reduction. Each half-reaction balances in atoms and in charge.
- The **oxidising agent** takes electrons and is reduced; the **reducing agent** gives electrons and is oxidised.
- Follow the charges: a species whose charge goes up has lost electrons.
- The electrons lost equal the electrons gained: multiply the half-reactions until they match, then add them so the electrons cancel.
:::
