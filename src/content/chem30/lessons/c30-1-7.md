The fuel in a car's tank and the oxygen in the air could react with a huge release of energy, yet they sit side by side for months without doing anything. A spark from the spark plug is needed to start them. Many exothermic reactions are like this: before the reactants can become products, they must first climb an energy barrier. A catalyst lowers that barrier, which is how a catalytic converter cleans exhaust gases and how the enzymes in your cells run reactions at body temperature.

In this lesson you will read the activation energies of the forward and reverse reactions from a potential energy diagram, and explain how a catalyst speeds up a reaction without changing its enthalpy change.

## Activation energy and the activated complex

For particles to react, they must collide, in the right orientation and with enough energy. As they meet, their bonds stretch and begin to break while the new bonds begin to form. For a moment the atoms make an unstable arrangement with a high potential energy, partway between reactants and products.

:::definition Activated complex
The **activated complex** is the short-lived, unstable arrangement of atoms at the point of highest potential energy during a collision. It can go on to form the products or fall back to the reactants.
:::

:::definition Activation energy
The **activation energy**, $E_a$, is the minimum energy that colliding particles must have to form the activated complex and react: the height of the energy barrier above the reactants.
:::

Collisions with less energy than $E_a$ simply bounce apart. At room temperature, only a small fraction of collisions have enough energy, which is why reactions with a large activation energy are slow, or do not start at all until something, such as a spark, supplies the energy.

## Potential energy diagrams

:::definition Potential energy diagram
A **potential energy diagram** plots the potential energy of the reacting particles against the progress of the reaction: the reactants, a hump at the activated complex, and the products.
:::

Read three quantities from the diagram:

- $E_a(\text{forward})$ = activated complex − reactants $= 225 - 150 = 75\ \text{kJ}$
- $E_a(\text{reverse})$ = activated complex − products $= 225 - 50 = 175\ \text{kJ}$
- $\Delta H$ = products − reactants $= 50 - 150 = -100\ \text{kJ}$

The reverse reaction, from products back to reactants, climbs the same hump from the other side. Because the hump is shared, the three quantities are linked:

$$\Delta H = E_a(\text{forward}) - E_a(\text{reverse})$$

Here $75 - 175 = -100\ \text{kJ}$. In an exothermic reaction the reverse activation energy is the larger one; in an endothermic reaction the forward one is.

:::example Reading an endothermic reaction
A potential energy diagram shows the reactants at $50\ \text{kJ}$, the activated complex at $200\ \text{kJ}$ and the products at $125\ \text{kJ}$. Find $E_a(\text{forward})$, $E_a(\text{reverse})$ and $\Delta H$.
@step $E_a(\text{forward}) = 200 - 50 = 150\ \text{kJ}$
@step $E_a(\text{reverse}) = 200 - 125 = 75\ \text{kJ}$
@step $\Delta H = 125 - 50 = +75\ \text{kJ}$; check: $150 - 75 = 75$.
@answer $E_a(\text{forward}) = 150\ \text{kJ}$, $E_a(\text{reverse}) = 75\ \text{kJ}$, $\Delta H = +75\ \text{kJ}$: endothermic.
:::

:::mistake Measure each climb from its own starting level
The activation energy is not the height of the hump above zero. The forward reaction starts at the reactants and the reverse reaction starts at the products, so measure each climb from its own level. In the endothermic example, the hump is at $200\ \text{kJ}$, but neither activation energy is $200\ \text{kJ}$.

The relationship also works without a diagram.

:::example Without a diagram
For a reaction, $E_a(\text{forward}) = 75\ \text{kJ}$ and $\Delta H = -40\ \text{kJ}$. What is the activation energy of the reverse reaction?
@step Sketch it: the products are $40\ \text{kJ}$ below the reactants, and the top of the hump is $75\ \text{kJ}$ above the reactants.
@step From the products, the climb is $75 + 40 = 115\ \text{kJ}$. With the formula: $E_a(\text{reverse}) = E_a(\text{forward}) - \Delta H = 75 - (-40)$.
@answer $E_a(\text{reverse}) = 115\ \text{kJ}$
:::

:::note Two kinds of diagram
An enthalpy diagram (lesson 1.4) shows only the reactant and product levels and $\Delta H$. A potential energy diagram also shows the path between them, so it shows the activation energies. Both give the same $\Delta H$.
:::

## Catalysts

:::definition Catalyst
A **catalyst** is a substance that speeds up a reaction without being used up. It provides a different pathway, through a different activated complex, with a lower activation energy.
:::

:::figure

:::

On the diagram, the catalysed pathway (coloured) has a lower hump than the uncatalysed one (grey), but it starts and finishes at the same levels. So:

- **Both activation energies fall, by the same amount.** Here the forward one falls from $75\ \text{kJ}$ to $175 - 150 = 25\ \text{kJ}$, and the reverse one from $175\ \text{kJ}$ to $175 - 50 = 125\ \text{kJ}$: each by $50\ \text{kJ}$. A catalyst speeds up the forward and the reverse reactions alike.
- **$\Delta H$ does not change.** The reactants and products are the same substances with the same enthalpies, so $\Delta H = -100\ \text{kJ}$ by either path.
- **The catalyst is not consumed.** It takes part in the pathway but is regenerated at the end.

Why is the reaction faster? At the same temperature, the particles have the same spread of energies as before, but with a lower barrier, a much larger fraction of collisions have enough energy to react.

Catalysts are everywhere. In the laboratory, a pinch of manganese(IV) oxide makes hydrogen peroxide decompose rapidly into water and oxygen, and the black powder can be filtered off unchanged afterwards. In living cells, proteins called **enzymes** catalyse almost every reaction; one enzyme in the liver breaks down hydrogen peroxide millions of times faster than it would on its own. In a car's catalytic converter, harmful exhaust gases react on the surface of hot metals such as platinum.

:::example A catalysed reaction
A reaction has $E_a(\text{forward}) = 120\ \text{kJ}$ and $\Delta H = +30\ \text{kJ}$. A catalyst lowers $E_a(\text{forward})$ to $70\ \text{kJ}$. Find $E_a(\text{reverse})$ with and without the catalyst.
@step Without the catalyst: $E_a(\text{reverse}) = 120 - 30 = 90\ \text{kJ}$.
@step The catalyst does not change $\Delta H$, so with it: $E_a(\text{reverse}) = 70 - 30 = 40\ \text{kJ}$.
@answer $90\ \text{kJ}$ without the catalyst and $40\ \text{kJ}$ with it: lowered by $50\ \text{kJ}$, the same amount as the forward activation energy.
:::

:::mistake A catalyst does not add energy
A catalyst does not heat the reactants or give the particles more kinetic energy, and it does not change how much energy the reaction releases or absorbs. It only lowers the barrier, so that more of the collisions that already happen are energetic enough to succeed.
:::

:::summary
- Reacting particles must collide with at least the **activation energy**, $E_a$, to form the **activated complex**, the unstable arrangement at the top of the energy barrier.
- On a **potential energy diagram**: $E_a(\text{forward})$ = activated complex − reactants, $E_a(\text{reverse})$ = activated complex − products, and $\Delta H$ = products − reactants $= E_a(\text{forward}) - E_a(\text{reverse})$.
- A **catalyst** provides a pathway with a lower activation energy. Both activation energies fall by the same amount, $\Delta H$ is unchanged, and the catalyst is not used up.
- With a lower barrier, a larger fraction of collisions succeed at the same temperature, so the reaction is faster.
:::
