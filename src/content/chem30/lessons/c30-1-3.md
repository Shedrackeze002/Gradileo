An instant cold pack holds a few spoonfuls of ammonium nitrate and a pouch of water. Squeeze it, the pouch breaks, the salt dissolves, and the pack turns cold enough to soothe a sprained ankle. To design one, an engineer needs to know how much energy each mole of salt absorbs as it dissolves. That single number, the **molar enthalpy**, also tells you how much energy a mole of fuel releases when it burns, or a mole of acid when it is neutralised.

In this lesson you will find a molar enthalpy from calorimetry data, and use a molar enthalpy to calculate the enthalpy change for any amount or mass of a substance.

## Enthalpy change and molar enthalpy

In lesson 1.2, $Q = mc\Delta t$ gave the heat gained or lost by the water in a calorimeter. The water is the surroundings. The chemical system changes by the same amount of energy in the opposite direction:

- If the water **warms up**, the system released that energy: $\Delta H = -Q$, and the change is exothermic.
- If the water **cools down**, the system absorbed that energy: $\Delta H = +Q$, and the change is endothermic.

:::definition Enthalpy change
The **enthalpy change**, $\Delta H$, is the energy the chemical system gains (positive) or loses (negative) as a change takes place, in kilojoules.
:::

:::definition Molar enthalpy
The **molar enthalpy**, $\Delta_r H_m$, is the enthalpy change per mole of a stated substance, in $\text{kJ/mol}$:

$$\Delta_r H_m = \frac{\Delta H}{n} \qquad\text{and so}\qquad \Delta H = n\,\Delta_r H_m$$
:::

A molar enthalpy is named after the change it describes. The **molar enthalpy of combustion**, $\Delta_c H_m$, is per mole of fuel burned; the **molar enthalpy of solution** is per mole of solute dissolved; there are molar enthalpies of neutralisation, and of changes of state such as fusion (melting) and vaporisation. Each one carries a sign: negative when energy is released, positive when it is absorbed.

## Finding a molar enthalpy from calorimetry

1. **Find the heat** absorbed or released by the water: $Q = mc\Delta t$.
2. **Find the amount**, in moles, of the substance that reacted: $n = \dfrac{m}{M}$ for a mass, or $n = cV$ for a solution.
3. **Give $\Delta H$ its sign**: negative if the water warmed, positive if it cooled.
4. **Divide**: $\Delta_r H_m = \dfrac{\Delta H}{n}$.

:::example Dissolving ammonium nitrate
$8.00\ \text{g}$ of ammonium nitrate, $\ce{NH4NO3}$, is dissolved in $100.0\ \text{g}$ of water in a polystyrene cup calorimeter. The temperature of the water falls from $21.0^\circ\text{C}$ to $15.0^\circ\text{C}$. Find the molar enthalpy of solution of ammonium nitrate. Use $c = 4.19\ \text{J/(g·°C)}$ and $M(\ce{NH4NO3}) = 80.06\ \text{g/mol}$.
@step $Q = mc\Delta t = 100.0 \times 4.19 \times 6.0 = 2514\ \text{J} = 2.514\ \text{kJ}$
@step $n = \dfrac{8.00}{80.06} = 0.09993\ \text{mol}$
@step The water cooled, so the dissolving absorbed energy: $\Delta H = +2.514\ \text{kJ}$.
@step $\Delta_r H_m = \dfrac{+2.514\ \text{kJ}}{0.09993\ \text{mol}} = +25.2\ \text{kJ/mol}$
@answer The molar enthalpy of solution of ammonium nitrate is $+25.2\ \text{kJ/mol}$: endothermic.
:::

For a reaction between two solutions, the mass of water is the total volume of the mixture times its density. A dilute solution is assumed to have the density, $1.00\ \text{g/mL}$, and the specific heat capacity of water.

:::example Neutralisation
$50.0\ \text{mL}$ of $1.00\ \text{mol/L}$ hydrochloric acid and $50.0\ \text{mL}$ of $1.00\ \text{mol/L}$ sodium hydroxide, both at $21.0^\circ\text{C}$, are mixed in a polystyrene cup calorimeter: $\ce{HCl(aq) + NaOH(aq) -> NaCl(aq) + H2O(l)}$. The highest temperature reached is $27.7^\circ\text{C}$. Find the molar enthalpy of neutralisation of the acid.
@step $m = (50.0 + 50.0)\ \text{mL} \times 1.00\ \text{g/mL} = 100.0\ \text{g}$
@step $Q = 100.0 \times 4.19 \times 6.7 = 2807\ \text{J} = 2.807\ \text{kJ}$
@step $n(\ce{HCl}) = 1.00\ \text{mol/L} \times 0.0500\ \text{L} = 0.0500\ \text{mol}$
@step The mixture warmed, so $\Delta H = -2.807\ \text{kJ}$, and $\Delta_r H_m = \dfrac{-2.807}{0.0500} = -56.1\ \text{kJ/mol}$
@answer $-56.1\ \text{kJ/mol}$: exothermic.
:::

:::mistake The sign comes from the water
Once you have worked out $Q$, a positive number, decide the sign of $\Delta H$ from what the **water** did. A rise in temperature means the reaction released energy, so $\Delta H$ is **negative**. Reporting $+56.1\ \text{kJ/mol}$ for the neutralisation above would claim that it absorbs energy, while the thermometer showed the opposite.
:::

## The bomb calorimeter

Combustion is studied in a **bomb calorimeter**: a sealed steel container (the "bomb") filled with oxygen and set in a bucket of water inside an insulated jacket. The sample is ignited electrically, and the temperature of the whole instrument is measured. Because the steel bomb, the bucket and the water all warm together, the calorimeter is described by one **heat capacity**, $C$, in $\text{kJ/°C}$, found beforehand by burning a sample with a known energy. Then

$$Q = C\Delta t$$

:::example Burning glucose
A $1.200\ \text{g}$ sample of glucose, $\ce{C6H12O6}$, burns completely in a bomb calorimeter whose heat capacity is $9.25\ \text{kJ/°C}$. The temperature rises by $2.02^\circ\text{C}$. Find the molar enthalpy of combustion of glucose. $M(\ce{C6H12O6}) = 180.18\ \text{g/mol}$.
@step $Q = C\Delta t = 9.25 \times 2.02 = 18.69\ \text{kJ}$
@step $n = \dfrac{1.200}{180.18} = 0.006660\ \text{mol}$
@step The calorimeter warmed, so $\Delta H = -18.69\ \text{kJ}$, and $\Delta_c H_m = \dfrac{-18.69}{0.006660} = -2806\ \text{kJ/mol}$
@answer $\Delta_c H_m = -2806\ \text{kJ/mol}$, about $-2.81 \times 10^3\ \text{kJ/mol}$.
:::

:::note A simple calorimeter for fuels
A fuel can also be burned in a small burner under a metal can of water. This is quick, but much of the heat of the flame warms the air instead of the water, so the molar enthalpy found this way is usually far smaller in size than the bomb calorimeter value.
:::

## Using a molar enthalpy: $\Delta H = n\Delta_r H_m$

Once a molar enthalpy is known, the enthalpy change for any amount follows from $\Delta H = n\Delta_r H_m$. Change a mass to moles first.

:::example Burning propane
The molar enthalpy of combustion of propane, $\ce{C3H8}$, is $-2043.9\ \text{kJ/mol}$ (with water vapour as a product). What is the enthalpy change when $50.0\ \text{g}$ of propane burns? $M(\ce{C3H8}) = 44.11\ \text{g/mol}$.
@step $n = \dfrac{50.0}{44.11} = 1.1335\ \text{mol}$
@step $\Delta H = n\Delta_c H_m = 1.1335 \times (-2043.9) = -2317\ \text{kJ}$
@answer $\Delta H = -2317\ \text{kJ}$, or about $-2.32 \times 10^3\ \text{kJ}$: $2317\ \text{kJ}$ is released.
:::

:::example Melting ice
The molar enthalpy of fusion of ice is $+6.01\ \text{kJ/mol}$. What is the enthalpy change when $100.0\ \text{g}$ of ice melts? $M(\ce{H2O}) = 18.02\ \text{g/mol}$.
@step $n = \dfrac{100.0}{18.02} = 5.5494\ \text{mol}$
@step $\Delta H = 5.5494 \times (+6.01) = +33.35\ \text{kJ}$
@answer $\Delta H = +33.4\ \text{kJ}$: melting absorbs energy.
:::

The same relationship works backwards: from the energy needed, find the amount of fuel.

:::example How much fuel?
What mass of methane must burn to heat $2.00\ \text{L}$ of water from $20.0^\circ\text{C}$ to $100.0^\circ\text{C}$, if all the heat goes into the water? $\Delta_c H_m(\ce{CH4}) = -802.5\ \text{kJ/mol}$, $c = 4.19\ \text{J/(g·°C)}$, and $M(\ce{CH4}) = 16.05\ \text{g/mol}$.
@step Heat the water needs: $Q = 2000 \times 4.19 \times 80.0 = 670\,400\ \text{J} = 670.4\ \text{kJ}$
@step The methane must release $670.4\ \text{kJ}$: $n = \dfrac{670.4}{802.5} = 0.8354\ \text{mol}$
@step $m = nM = 0.8354 \times 16.05 = 13.41\ \text{g}$
@answer About $13.4\ \text{g}$ of methane.
:::

:::summary
- The water in a calorimeter is the surroundings. If it warms, the system released energy and $\Delta H = -Q$; if it cools, the system absorbed energy and $\Delta H = +Q$.
- **Molar enthalpy**: $\Delta_r H_m = \dfrac{\Delta H}{n}$, in $\text{kJ/mol}$, per mole of a stated substance, with its sign.
- From calorimetry: find $Q = mc\Delta t$ (or $Q = C\Delta t$ in a bomb calorimeter), find $n$, give $\Delta H$ its sign, and divide.
- **Enthalpy change** for any amount: $\Delta H = n\Delta_r H_m$. Change masses to moles first, and keep the sign.
:::
