Batteries power phones, torches, hearing aids and cars, and every one of them runs on a spontaneous redox reaction. When a strip of zinc stands in copper(II) sulfate solution, electrons pass straight from zinc atoms to copper(II) ions, and the energy of the reaction only warms the solution. If the two half-reactions are kept in separate containers, though, the electrons must travel through a wire to get from one to the other. A flow of electrons through a wire is an electric current.

In this lesson you will name the parts of a voltaic cell, describe which way electrons and ions move, and write and read the shorthand notation of a cell.

## The parts of a voltaic cell

:::definition Voltaic cell
A **voltaic cell** (or galvanic cell) changes the chemical energy of a spontaneous redox reaction into electrical energy, by making the electrons travel through an external circuit.
:::

A voltaic cell is made of two **half-cells**. Each has an electrode standing in an electrolyte, and the two are joined in two ways: by a wire between the electrodes, and by a salt bridge between the solutions.

:::definition Electrode and electrolyte
An **electrode** is a solid conductor where electrons enter or leave the cell, often a strip of metal. An **electrolyte** is a solution of ions that conducts electricity.
:::

:::definition Anode and cathode
The **anode** is the electrode where **oxidation** happens. The **cathode** is the electrode where **reduction** happens. Remember "**An Ox**, **Red Cat**": anode oxidation, reduction cathode.
:::

:::definition Salt bridge and porous cup
A **salt bridge** is a tube of an electrolyte whose ions do not react, such as potassium nitrate solution, joining the two half-cells. Its ions move into the half-cells to keep each solution electrically neutral, which completes the circuit. A **porous cup** does the same job: one half-cell stands inside an unglazed porous pot placed in the other, and ions pass through the pores.
:::

The table shows the two half-reactions, with copper(II) ions above zinc, so the reaction is spontaneous (lesson 2.3).

| Reduction half-reaction | $E^\circ$ (V) |
|---|---|
| $\ce{Cu^2+(aq) + 2e- -> Cu(s)}$ | $+0.34$ |
| $\ce{Zn^2+(aq) + 2e- -> Zn(s)}$ | $-0.76$ |

The stronger reducing agent, zinc, is oxidised: the zinc electrode is the **anode**, $\ce{Zn(s) -> Zn^2+(aq) + 2e-}$. The stronger oxidising agent, copper(II) ions, is reduced on the copper electrode: it is the **cathode**, $\ce{Cu^2+(aq) + 2e- -> Cu(s)}$. The cell reaction is $\ce{Zn(s) + Cu^2+(aq) -> Zn^2+(aq) + Cu(s)}$.

## What moves where

As the cell runs:

- **Electrons** flow through the wire, from the anode to the cathode. They never travel through the solutions or the salt bridge.
- At the anode, zinc atoms become ions: the **anode loses mass**, and the concentration of zinc ions rises.
- At the cathode, copper(II) ions become copper atoms: the **cathode gains mass**, and the concentration of copper(II) ions falls, so the blue colour fades.
- In the salt bridge, **anions** (here nitrate ions) move toward the anode, to balance the new positive zinc ions, and **cations** (potassium ions) move toward the cathode, to replace the copper(II) ions removed.

:::example A nickel–silver cell
A voltaic cell is made from a nickel electrode in nickel(II) nitrate solution and a silver electrode in silver nitrate solution, joined by a salt bridge of potassium nitrate. Silver ions ($+0.80$ V) are above nickel ($-0.26$ V) in the redox table. Describe the cell.
@step Nickel is the stronger reducing agent, so the nickel electrode is the anode: $\ce{Ni(s) -> Ni^2+(aq) + 2e-}$.
@step Silver ions are reduced at the silver electrode, the cathode: $\ce{Ag^+(aq) + e- -> Ag(s)}$.
@step Electrons flow through the wire from nickel to silver. Nitrate ions in the salt bridge move toward the nickel half-cell, and potassium ions toward the silver half-cell.
@answer Cell reaction: $\ce{Ni(s) + 2Ag^+(aq) -> Ni^2+(aq) + 2Ag(s)}$. The silver electrode gains mass and the nickel electrode loses mass.
:::

:::mistake Electrons in the salt bridge
Electrons cannot travel through a solution: they flow only through the wire and the electrodes. The salt bridge carries charge by moving **ions**. Without it, positive charge would build up around the anode and negative charge around the cathode, and the current would stop almost at once.
:::

## Inert electrodes

Sometimes neither the oxidising agent nor the reducing agent of a half-cell is a metal that can be an electrode, as when iron(III) ions are reduced to iron(II) ions, or chlorine to chloride ions. Then an **inert electrode**, usually platinum or graphite, stands in the solution. It carries electrons to or from the reacting species but does not react itself.

## Cell notation

Drawing a whole cell takes time, so chemists use a shorthand.

:::definition Cell notation
**Cell notation** writes the anode on the left and the cathode on the right. A single line, $\mid$, separates two phases, such as a solid electrode and a solution, and a double line, $\parallel$, stands for the salt bridge. Species in the same solution are separated by commas.
:::

The zinc–copper cell is written

$$\ce{Zn(s)} \mid \ce{Zn^2+(aq)} \parallel \ce{Cu^2+(aq)} \mid \ce{Cu(s)}$$

Read from left to right, it follows the electrons' story: zinc is oxidised to zinc ions at the anode, and copper(II) ions are reduced to copper at the cathode.

:::example Reading a cell
A cell is written $\ce{Pb(s)} \mid \ce{Pb^2+(aq)} \parallel \ce{Ag^+(aq)} \mid \ce{Ag(s)}$. Name the anode and the cathode, and write the cell reaction.
@step The left side is the anode: lead is oxidised, $\ce{Pb(s) -> Pb^2+(aq) + 2e-}$.
@step The right side is the cathode: silver ions are reduced, $\ce{Ag^+(aq) + e- -> Ag(s)}$.
@answer Anode: lead; cathode: silver. Cell reaction: $\ce{Pb(s) + 2Ag^+(aq) -> Pb^2+(aq) + 2Ag(s)}$.
:::

A cell is written $\ce{Ni(s)} \mid \ce{Ni^2+(aq)} \parallel \ce{Fe^3+(aq), Fe^2+(aq)} \mid \ce{Pt(s)}$. Write the balanced cell reaction.

:::summary
- A **voltaic cell** turns the energy of a spontaneous redox reaction into electrical energy. Two half-cells are joined by a wire and by a **salt bridge** or a **porous cup**.
- Oxidation happens at the **anode** and reduction at the **cathode** ("An Ox, Red Cat"). The anode is the reducing agent lower in the redox table.
- Electrons flow through the wire from the anode to the cathode. In the salt bridge, anions move toward the anode and cations toward the cathode.
- The anode loses mass; the cathode gains mass, and the concentration of the ions reduced there falls.
- An **inert electrode**, such as platinum, carries electrons when no metal takes part in a half-reaction.
- **Cell notation**: anode $\mid$ anode solution $\parallel$ cathode solution $\mid$ cathode.
:::
