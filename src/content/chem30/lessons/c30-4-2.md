A factory that makes ammonia from nitrogen and hydrogen needs to know more than whether the reaction reaches equilibrium. It needs to know **how far** the reaction goes: will the equilibrium mixture be mostly ammonia, or mostly unreacted gas? A single number, the equilibrium constant, answers that question for any reversible reaction at a given temperature.

In this lesson you will write the equilibrium law expression for a reaction, leaving out pure solids and liquids, use the size of the equilibrium constant to say which side is favoured, and calculate equilibrium constants and concentrations, using ICE tables when you need them.

## The equilibrium law

Hydrogen and iodine react to form hydrogen iodide: $\ce{H2(g) + I2(g) <=> 2HI(g)}$. The table shows three equilibrium mixtures, all at the same temperature, that started with different amounts of gas.

| Experiment | $[\ce{H2}]$ (mol/L) | $[\ce{I2}]$ (mol/L) | $[\ce{HI}]$ (mol/L) | $\dfrac{[\ce{HI}]}{[\ce{H2}][\ce{I2}]}$ | $\dfrac{[\ce{HI}]^2}{[\ce{H2}][\ce{I2}]}$ |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 0.100 | 0.100 | 0.700 | 70.0 | 49.0 |
| 2 | 0.250 | 0.0360 | 0.664 | 73.8 | 49.0 |
| 3 | 0.0400 | 0.160 | 0.560 | 87.5 | 49.0 |

The equilibrium concentrations are different every time, but one ratio is always the same: the concentration of the product **squared**, the power being its coefficient, divided by the concentrations of the reactants. That constant ratio is the equilibrium constant.

:::definition Equilibrium law expression
For the reaction $a\text{A} + b\text{B} \rightleftharpoons c\text{C} + d\text{D}$ at equilibrium,
$$K_c = \dfrac{[\text{C}]^c[\text{D}]^d}{[\text{A}]^a[\text{B}]^b}$$
Products go in the numerator and reactants in the denominator, each concentration raised to the power of its coefficient.
:::

:::definition Equilibrium constant
The **equilibrium constant**, $K_c$, is the value of the equilibrium law expression for a reaction at equilibrium. It has the same value for every equilibrium mixture of that reaction **at the same temperature**. In these lessons $K_c$ is written without units.
:::

:::example Writing an expression
Write the equilibrium law expression for $\ce{N2(g) + 3H2(g) <=> 2NH3(g)}$.
@step The product, $\ce{NH3}$, goes on top, raised to the power 2.
@step The reactants go on the bottom: $\ce{N2}$ to the power 1 and $\ce{H2}$ to the power 3, multiplied together.
@answer $K_c = \dfrac{[\ce{NH3}]^2}{[\ce{N2}][\ce{H2}]^3}$
:::

:::mistake Powers, not multipliers
The coefficient 3 in front of $\ce{H2}$ becomes the power in $[\ce{H2}]^3$. Writing $3[\ce{H2}]$, or adding the concentrations instead of multiplying them, gives an expression that is not constant.
:::

## Pure solids and liquids

The concentration of a pure solid or a pure liquid cannot change: a lump of calcium carbonate has the same number of moles per litre of solid however big it is. Because these concentrations are constant, they are left out of the equilibrium law expression (they are absorbed into the value of $K_c$).

:::definition What appears in K
Gases, $(g)$, and dissolved particles, $(aq)$, appear in the equilibrium law expression. **Pure solids, $(s)$, and pure liquids, $(l)$, are left out**, including water when it is the solvent.
:::

:::example A solid and a gas
Write the equilibrium law expression for $\ce{CaCO3(s) <=> CaO(s) + CO2(g)}$.
@step $\ce{CaCO3(s)}$ and $\ce{CaO(s)}$ are pure solids: leave them out.
@step Only the gas remains, in the numerator, and nothing is left for the denominator.
@answer $K_c = [\ce{CO2}]$
:::

:::example Water as the solvent
Write the equilibrium law expression for $\ce{CH3COOH(aq) + H2O(l) <=> H3O+(aq) + CH3COO-(aq)}$.
@step $\ce{H2O(l)}$ is the solvent, a pure liquid: leave it out.
@answer $K_c = \dfrac{[\ce{H3O+}][\ce{CH3COO-}]}{[\ce{CH3COOH}]}$
:::

:::

## What the size of K tells you

Because products are on top, a large $K_c$ means that at equilibrium the products far outweigh the reactants.

| Value of $K_c$ | At equilibrium |
|---|---|
| $K_c \gg 1$ (such as $10^{5}$) | Mostly products: **products are favoured**. |
| $K_c \approx 1$ | Significant amounts of both. |
| $K_c \ll 1$ (such as $10^{-5}$) | Mostly reactants: **reactants are favoured**. |

Writing the equation differently changes the value of $K_c$ in a predictable way:

- **Reversing** the equation turns the expression upside down: $K_c' = \dfrac{1}{K_c}$.
- **Multiplying** every coefficient by $n$ raises every power to $n$ times its value: $K_c' = K_c^n$.

:::example K for a related equation
At a certain temperature, $\ce{2SO2(g) + O2(g) <=> 2SO3(g)}$ has $K_c = 2.5 \times 10^{2}$. Are products or reactants favoured? What is $K_c$ for $\ce{2SO3(g) <=> 2SO2(g) + O2(g)}$ at the same temperature?
@step $K_c = 250$ is much greater than 1, so products are favoured.
@step The second equation is the first one reversed, so its expression is upside down.
@answer Products are favoured; $K_c' = \dfrac{1}{2.5 \times 10^{2}} = 4.0 \times 10^{-3}$, much less than 1, as expected for the reverse reaction.
:::

## Calculating K

When every equilibrium concentration is known, from measurements or read from the flat ends of a concentration–time graph (lesson 4.1), substitute them into the expression.

:::example K from equilibrium concentrations
At equilibrium at a certain temperature, a flask holds $[\ce{PCl5}] = 0.0500\ \text{mol/L}$, $[\ce{PCl3}] = 0.0300\ \text{mol/L}$ and $[\ce{Cl2}] = 0.0700\ \text{mol/L}$: $\ce{PCl5(g) <=> PCl3(g) + Cl2(g)}$. What is $K_c$, to three significant figures?
@step $K_c = \dfrac{[\ce{PCl3}][\ce{Cl2}]}{[\ce{PCl5}]}$
@step $K_c = \dfrac{(0.0300)(0.0700)}{0.0500} = 0.0420$
@answer $K_c = 0.0420$: reactants are favoured.
:::

## ICE tables

Usually only the starting amounts are known, plus one equilibrium concentration. An **ICE table** organises the rest: one row each for the **I**nitial concentrations, the **C**hange, and the **E**quilibrium concentrations.

:::definition ICE table
A table of the **initial** concentrations, the **change** in each concentration, and the **equilibrium** concentrations. The changes are in the ratio of the coefficients, written with an unknown $x$; reactants decrease and products increase.
:::

Follow these steps:

1. Change any amounts in moles to concentrations: $c = \dfrac{n}{V}$.
2. Fill in the initial row. Products not yet present start at 0.
3. Write the changes in terms of $x$, using the coefficients.
4. Use the one known equilibrium concentration to find $x$, then complete the equilibrium row.
5. Substitute the **equilibrium** concentrations into the expression.

:::example An ICE table
$0.600\ \text{mol}$ of $\ce{SO2}$ and $0.400\ \text{mol}$ of $\ce{O2}$ are put into an empty $2.00\ \text{L}$ flask at constant temperature: $\ce{2SO2(g) + O2(g) <=> 2SO3(g)}$. At equilibrium the flask holds $0.200\ \text{mol}$ of $\ce{SO3}$. What is $K_c$, to three significant figures?

| | $[\ce{SO2}]$ | $[\ce{O2}]$ | $[\ce{SO3}]$ |
|---|---|---|---|
| Initial | 0.300 | 0.200 | 0 |
| Change | $-2x$ | $-x$ | $+2x$ |
| Equilibrium | $0.300 - 2x$ | $0.200 - x$ | $2x$ |
@step Concentrations: $[\ce{SO2}]_0 = \dfrac{0.600}{2.00} = 0.300$, $[\ce{O2}]_0 = 0.200$ and $[\ce{SO3}]_{eq} = \dfrac{0.200}{2.00} = 0.100\ \text{mol/L}$.
@step $2x = 0.100$, so $x = 0.0500\ \text{mol/L}$.
@step Equilibrium: $[\ce{SO2}] = 0.300 - 0.100 = 0.200$, $[\ce{O2}] = 0.200 - 0.0500 = 0.150$, $[\ce{SO3}] = 0.100$.
@step $K_c = \dfrac{[\ce{SO3}]^2}{[\ce{SO2}]^2[\ce{O2}]} = \dfrac{(0.100)^2}{(0.200)^2(0.150)}$
@answer $K_c = 1.67$
:::

:::mistake Initial values in the expression
$K_c$ is always calculated from **equilibrium** concentrations. Substituting the initial concentrations, or amounts in moles instead of concentrations, gives a wrong value.
:::

## Working backwards

If $K_c$ is known, the expression can be solved for an unknown concentration. Two kinds of problem keep the algebra short.

**A perfect square.** When the expression is a square on top and bottom, take the square root of both sides.

:::example Taking the square root
$0.500\ \text{mol/L}$ each of $\ce{H2}$ and $\ce{I2}$ are put into an empty flask at a temperature where $K_c = 49.0$ for $\ce{H2(g) + I2(g) <=> 2HI(g)}$. What is the equilibrium concentration of $\ce{HI}$, to three significant figures?
@step ICE: at equilibrium $[\ce{H2}] = [\ce{I2}] = 0.500 - x$ and $[\ce{HI}] = 2x$.
@step $49.0 = \dfrac{(2x)^2}{(0.500 - x)^2}$. Square root of both sides: $7.00 = \dfrac{2x}{0.500 - x}$
@step $2x = 3.50 - 7.00x$, so $9.00x = 3.50$ and $x = 0.3889\ \text{mol/L}$.
@answer $[\ce{HI}] = 2x = 0.778\ \text{mol/L}$ (and $[\ce{H2}] = [\ce{I2}] = 0.111\ \text{mol/L}$).
:::

**A very small K.** When $K_c$ is tiny, so little reacts that the change $x$ is negligible next to the starting concentration. Replace $c - x$ by $c$, then check afterwards that $x$ really is tiny.

:::example A small equilibrium constant
$0.500\ \text{mol/L}$ of $\ce{COCl2}$ is put into an empty flask at a temperature where $K_c = 8.0 \times 10^{-7}$ for $\ce{COCl2(g) <=> CO(g) + Cl2(g)}$. What is the equilibrium concentration of $\ce{CO}$, to two significant figures?
@step ICE: $[\ce{COCl2}] = 0.500 - x$ and $[\ce{CO}] = [\ce{Cl2}] = x$.
@step $K_c$ is tiny, so $0.500 - x \approx 0.500$: $8.0 \times 10^{-7} = \dfrac{x^2}{0.500}$
@step $x = \sqrt{8.0 \times 10^{-7} \times 0.500} = 6.3 \times 10^{-4}\ \text{mol/L}$
@answer $[\ce{CO}] = 6.3 \times 10^{-4}\ \text{mol/L}$. Check: $x$ is about 0.1% of $0.500$, so the approximation was fine.
:::

:::check c30-k-calculate 3
:::

:::summary
- For $a\text{A} + b\text{B} \rightleftharpoons c\text{C} + d\text{D}$: $K_c = \dfrac{[\text{C}]^c[\text{D}]^d}{[\text{A}]^a[\text{B}]^b}$, products over reactants, each to the power of its coefficient.
- $K_c$ is constant for a reaction at a given temperature, whatever the starting amounts.
- Leave pure solids and pure liquids, including water as the solvent, out of the expression.
- $K_c \gg 1$: products favoured. $K_c \ll 1$: reactants favoured. Reversing an equation inverts $K_c$; multiplying the coefficients by $n$ raises $K_c$ to the power $n$.
- **ICE tables**: initial, change (in the ratio of the coefficients) and equilibrium concentrations. Convert moles to mol/L first, and always use equilibrium concentrations in $K_c$.
- Working backwards: take square roots when both sides are perfect squares; when $K_c$ is very small, treat $x$ as negligible next to the starting concentration and check afterwards.
:::
