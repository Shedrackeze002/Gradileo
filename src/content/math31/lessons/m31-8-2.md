At a steady speed, a car's distance is speed × time: the area of a rectangle under a flat velocity graph. When the speed changes, the area under the velocity curve still gives the distance, but now its top is curved. Riemann sums estimate that area with rectangles.

## Area under a rate graph

A cyclist riding at a steady $6$ m/s for $10$ s travels $6 \times 10 = 60$ m, the area of a rectangle of height $6$ and width $10$ under her velocity graph. The same holds for any rate: the area under a graph of (rate) against (time) is the total amount, whether it is metres from a velocity, litres from a flow rate or barrels of oil from a production rate.

Under a curve, we cut the interval into thin strips, replace each strip by a rectangle, and add the areas of the rectangles.

:::definition Riemann sum
Divide $[a, b]$ into $n$ subintervals of equal width $\Delta x = \dfrac{b - a}{n}$ and choose a sample point $x_{i}^{*}$ in each one. The **Riemann sum** is
$$\sum_{i=1}^{n} f(x_{i}^{*})\,\Delta x = \Delta x\left[f(x_{1}^{*}) + f(x_{2}^{*}) + \dots + f(x_{n}^{*})\right].$$
Each term is the area of a rectangle: height $f(x_{i}^{*})$, width $\Delta x$.
:::

## Left and right sums

A **left sum** $L_n$ takes each height at the left end of its subinterval; a **right sum** $R_n$ takes it at the right end.

:::example Left and right sums
Estimate the area under $f(x) = x^{2} + 1$ on $[0, 2]$ with $n = 4$, using left endpoints and then right endpoints.
@step $\Delta x = \dfrac{2 - 0}{4} = 0.5$, so the subintervals are $[0, 0.5]$, $[0.5, 1]$, $[1, 1.5]$ and $[1.5, 2]$.
@step Left endpoints $0, 0.5, 1, 1.5$ give heights $1, 1.25, 2, 3.25$: $L_{4} = 0.5(1 + 1.25 + 2 + 3.25) = 0.5(7.5) = 3.75$.
@step Right endpoints $0.5, 1, 1.5, 2$ give heights $1.25, 2, 3.25, 5$: $R_{4} = 0.5(11.5) = 5.75$.
@answer $L_{4} = 3.75$ and $R_{4} = 5.75$ square units
:::

:::mistake Counting endpoints
With $n$ subintervals there are $n + 1$ endpoints. A left sum uses the first $n$ of them (not $b$), and a right sum uses the last $n$ (not $a$). Using all $n + 1$ adds an extra rectangle.
:::

## Midpoint sums

A **midpoint sum** $M_n$ takes each height at the middle of its subinterval. Part of each rectangle sticks out above the curve and part falls short, so the errors partly cancel.

:::example A midpoint sum
Estimate the area under $f(x) = x^{2} + 1$ on $[0, 2]$ with $n = 4$, using midpoints.
@step The midpoints are $0.25, 0.75, 1.25, 1.75$, with heights $1.0625, 1.5625, 2.5625, 4.0625$.
@step $M_{4} = 0.5(1.0625 + 1.5625 + 2.5625 + 4.0625) = 0.5(9.25) = 4.625$
@answer $M_{4} = 4.625$ square units
:::

## Over or under?

When $f$ is increasing, the left end of each subinterval is its lowest point, so every left rectangle fits under the curve: $L_n$ is an **underestimate**. Every right rectangle reaches above the curve, so $R_n$ is an **overestimate**. For a decreasing function the roles swap. If $f$ rises and then falls on the interval, a left or right sum could go either way.

As $n$ grows, the rectangles get thinner and all three sums close in on the same number:

:::figure

:::

:::definition Definite integral
The limit of the Riemann sums as $n \to \infty$ is the **definite integral** of $f$ from $a$ to $b$:
$$\int_a^b f(x)\,dx = \lim_{n \to \infty} \sum_{i=1}^{n} f(x_{i}^{*})\,\Delta x.$$
For a continuous function the limit exists and is the same for every choice of sample points.
:::

## Sums from data

Measured data give a rate only at certain times, so a Riemann sum is the natural way to estimate the total. A car merging onto a highway records these velocities:

:::figure
{"type":"table","headers":["$t$ (s)","$v$ (m/s)"],"rows":[["0","0"],["2","6"],["4","10"],["6","13"],["8","15"]],"horizontal":true}
:::

:::example Distance from a speedometer
Estimate the distance the car travels in the first $8$ s with a left sum and with a right sum.
@step The readings are $2$ s apart, so there are $4$ subintervals with $\Delta t = 2$.
@step $L_{4} = 2(0 + 6 + 10 + 13) = 2(29) = 58$ m and $R_{4} = 2(6 + 10 + 13 + 15) = 2(44) = 88$ m.
@step The car speeds up the whole time, so the left sum is too low and the right sum is too high.
@answer The distance is between $58$ m and $88$ m; their average, $73$ m, is a reasonable estimate.
:::

:::tip Units
The units of a Riemann sum are (units of the rate) × (units of the variable): (m/s) × s = m, and (L/min) × min = L.
:::

:::check m31-riemann 3
:::

:::summary
- The area under a rate graph is the total amount; Riemann sums estimate it with rectangles of width $\Delta x = \frac{b - a}{n}$.
- Left, right and midpoint sums take each height at the left end, right end or middle of its subinterval.
- On an increasing function, left sums underestimate and right sums overestimate; on a decreasing function it is the reverse.
- Midpoint sums are usually the most accurate of the three.
- The definite integral $\int_a^b f(x)\,dx$ is the limit of the Riemann sums as $n \to \infty$.
:::
