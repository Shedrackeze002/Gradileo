When you blow up a balloon, its radius and its volume both grow, but not at the same rate: they are linked by $V = \frac{4}{3}\pi r^{3}$. If you know how fast one is changing, the chain rule tells you how fast the other is changing. Problems like this are called **related rates** problems.

## Rates linked by an equation

In a related rates problem every changing quantity is a function of time $t$. Differentiating an equation that links the quantities, with respect to $t$, gives an equation that links their **rates**. This is implicit differentiation with $t$ as the variable:
$$\frac{d}{dt}\left(r^{2}\right) = 2r\frac{dr}{dt}, \qquad \frac{d}{dt}\left(x^{2} + y^{2}\right) = 2x\frac{dx}{dt} + 2y\frac{dy}{dt}.$$

:::definition Related rates
Two or more rates of change, such as $\dfrac{dr}{dt}$ and $\dfrac{dA}{dt}$, that are connected because the quantities themselves are connected by an equation.
:::

:::example A spreading ripple
A pebble dropped into a calm lake makes a circular ripple whose radius grows at $30$ cm/s. How fast is the area inside the ripple growing when the radius is $50$ cm?
@step $A = \pi r^{2}$, so differentiating with respect to $t$: $\dfrac{dA}{dt} = 2\pi r\dfrac{dr}{dt}$.
@step Substitute $r = 50$ and $\dfrac{dr}{dt} = 30$: $\dfrac{dA}{dt} = 2\pi(50)(30) = 3000\pi$.
@answer The area grows at $3000\pi \approx 9425$ cm²/s.
:::

Notice that the area grows faster when the ripple is bigger, even though the radius grows at a steady rate.

## A strategy

1. Draw a diagram. Name every quantity that changes with a variable (a function of $t$); label constants with numbers.
2. Write down the given rates and the rate you want, with signs: a decreasing quantity has a **negative** rate.
3. Write an equation that relates the variables at **any** time.
4. Differentiate both sides with respect to $t$.
5. Substitute the values at the given instant, and solve for the unknown rate.
6. Answer in words, with units.

:::example A sliding ladder
A $10$ m ladder leans against a vertical wall. The bottom is pulled away from the wall at $0.5$ m/s. How fast is the top sliding down the wall when the bottom is $6$ m from the wall?
@step Let $x$ be the distance from the wall to the bottom and $y$ the height of the top: $x^{2} + y^{2} = 100$, with $\dfrac{dx}{dt} = 0.5$.
@step Differentiate: $2x\dfrac{dx}{dt} + 2y\dfrac{dy}{dt} = 0$.
@step When $x = 6$: $y = \sqrt{100 - 36} = 8$, so $2(6)(0.5) + 2(8)\dfrac{dy}{dt} = 0$.
@step $\dfrac{dy}{dt} = -\dfrac{6}{16} = -0.375$. The negative sign means $y$ is decreasing.
@answer The top slides down the wall at $0.375$ m/s.
:::

If you put $x = 6$ into $x^{2} + y^{2} = 100$ **before** differentiating, you get $36 + y^{2} = 100$, which says $y$ never changes, and $\dfrac{dy}{dt} = 0$. Values at one instant go in only after you differentiate.

## Similar triangles: shadows and cones

When a problem has two quantities in a fixed ratio, similar triangles give an equation that eliminates one of them.

:::example A lengthening shadow
A street light is at the top of a $6$ m pole. Dakota, who is $1.8$ m tall, walks away from the pole at $1.4$ m/s. How fast is Dakota's shadow lengthening?
@step Let $x$ be Dakota's distance from the pole and $s$ the length of the shadow. Similar triangles: $\dfrac{6}{x + s} = \dfrac{1.8}{s}$.
@step Cross-multiply: $6s = 1.8x + 1.8s$, so $4.2s = 1.8x$ and $s = \dfrac{3}{7}x$.
@step $\dfrac{ds}{dt} = \dfrac{3}{7}\cdot\dfrac{dx}{dt} = \dfrac{3}{7}(1.4) = 0.6$
@answer The shadow lengthens at $0.6$ m/s, however far Dakota is from the pole. (The tip of the shadow moves at $1.4 + 0.6 = 2$ m/s.)
:::

:::example Filling a conical tank
A tank is an inverted cone with a top radius of $2$ m and a height of $6$ m. Water flows in at $2$ m³/min. How fast is the water level rising when the water is $3$ m deep?
@step Similar triangles: $\dfrac{r}{h} = \dfrac{2}{6}$, so the surface radius is $r = \dfrac{h}{3}$.
@step $V = \dfrac{1}{3}\pi r^{2}h = \dfrac{1}{3}\pi\left(\dfrac{h}{3}\right)^{2}h = \dfrac{\pi}{27}h^{3}$
@step $\dfrac{dV}{dt} = \dfrac{\pi}{9}h^{2}\dfrac{dh}{dt}$, so $2 = \dfrac{\pi}{9}(3)^{2}\dfrac{dh}{dt} = \pi\dfrac{dh}{dt}$.
@answer $\dfrac{dh}{dt} = \dfrac{2}{\pi} \approx 0.637$ m/min
:::

## Moving vehicles and changing angles

:::example Cars moving apart
Two highways cross at right angles. Car A is $6$ km north of the intersection driving north at $80$ km/h; car B is $8$ km east of it driving east at $60$ km/h. How fast is the distance between them increasing?
@step If $D$ is the distance between the cars, $D^{2} = x^{2} + y^{2}$, so $2D\dfrac{dD}{dt} = 2x\dfrac{dx}{dt} + 2y\dfrac{dy}{dt}$.
@step Now $x = 6$, $y = 8$ and $D = \sqrt{36 + 64} = 10$: $10\dfrac{dD}{dt} = 6(80) + 8(60) = 960$.
@answer The distance is increasing at $96$ km/h.
:::

If a car drives **toward** the intersection, its distance from the intersection is decreasing, so its rate is negative. The sign of $\dfrac{dD}{dt}$ then tells you whether the cars are getting closer or farther apart.

:::example A rising balloon
A hot-air balloon rises straight up at $3$ m/s from a point $60$ m from an observer. How fast is the angle of elevation $\theta$ increasing when the balloon is $80$ m up?
@step $\tan\theta = \dfrac{y}{60}$, so $\sec^{2}\theta\,\dfrac{d\theta}{dt} = \dfrac{1}{60}\cdot\dfrac{dy}{dt}$.
@step When $y = 80$ the line of sight is $100$ m long, so $\sec\theta = \dfrac{100}{60}$ and $\sec^{2}\theta = \dfrac{25}{9}$.
@step $\dfrac{d\theta}{dt} = \dfrac{9}{25}\cdot\dfrac{3}{60} = 0.018$
@answer The angle increases at $0.018$ rad/s.
:::

:::mistake Signs and "how fast is it decreasing?"
When a question asks how fast something is **decreasing**, the derivative is negative, but the answer is given as a positive rate with the word "decreasing": the ladder's top "slides down at $0.375$ m/s", not "at $-0.375$ m/s".
:::

:::summary
- Treat every changing quantity as a function of $t$ and differentiate the equation that links them with respect to $t$.
- Use geometry (Pythagoras, similar triangles, area and volume formulas, trigonometry) to find that equation.
- Substitute the values at the given instant only **after** differentiating.
- Decreasing quantities have negative rates; state the final answer in words with units.
:::
