A news report says: “$54\%$ of Riverton voters support building a new arena. The poll is accurate to within $3.5$ percentage points, 19 times out of 20.” What does the last sentence mean, and should the town start building? In this lesson you will learn to read and use poll results like this one.

## Samples and populations

Pollsters cannot ask every voter in a town, so they survey a **random sample** and use it to estimate the whole **population**. Different random samples give slightly different results, so a survey result is an estimate, not an exact value. Every good poll reports how precise its estimate is.

:::definition Margin of error and confidence interval
The **margin of error** tells how far the true population value may reasonably be from the survey result. The **confidence interval** is the range of values from the survey result minus the margin of error to the survey result plus the margin of error.
$$\text{confidence interval} = \text{survey result} \pm \text{margin of error}$$
:::

:::example The arena poll
A poll found that $54\%$ of voters support a new arena, with a margin of error of $3.5$ percentage points. Find the confidence interval.
@step Lower end: $54 - 3.5 = 50.5$
@step Upper end: $54 + 3.5 = 57.5$
@answer The confidence interval is $50.5\%$ to $57.5\%$.
:::

:::example Working backwards
A survey report says that between $38.6\%$ and $45.4\%$ of the drivers in a city use winter tires. Find the survey result and the margin of error.
@step The survey result is the midpoint: $\dfrac{38.6 + 45.4}{2} = 42$
@step The margin of error is half the width: $\dfrac{45.4 - 38.6}{2} = 3.4$
@answer The survey result was $42\%$, with a margin of error of $3.4$ percentage points.
:::

## Confidence level

:::definition Confidence level
The **confidence level** tells how often the polling method gives an interval that contains the true population value. “19 times out of 20” means $\dfrac{19}{20} = 95\%$. Polls also use $90\%$ (“9 times out of 10”) and $99\%$ (“99 times out of 100”).
:::

If the arena poll were repeated many times with new random samples, about $95\%$ of the intervals would contain the true percent of all voters. So we say: **we can be $95\%$ confident that between $50.5\%$ and $57.5\%$ of all voters support the arena.**

:::mistake What 95% does not mean
The confidence level is **not** the percent of people who agree, and it does not make the interval certain. “$95\%$ of voters support the arena” is wrong, and so is “the true percent is definitely between $50.5\%$ and $57.5\%$”. About $1$ time in $20$, the true value is outside the interval.
:::

## Sample size and confidence level

Two things change the margin of error.

- **Sample size.** A larger random sample gives a more precise estimate, so the margin of error is smaller. For a survey result near $50\%$ at a $95\%$ confidence level, the margin of error is about $4.4$ percentage points with $500$ people, $3.1$ with $1000$ people and $2.2$ with $2000$ people.
- **Confidence level.** With the same sample, being more confident needs a wider interval. For $1000$ people and a result near $50\%$, the margin of error is about $2.6$ points at $90\%$, $3.1$ at $95\%$ and $4.1$ at $99\%$.

| To make the margin of error smaller... | Trade-off |
|---|---|
| survey more people | more cost and time |
| lower the confidence level | less confidence that the interval contains the true value |

:::example A smaller margin of error
A poll of $500$ people at a $95\%$ confidence level has a margin of error of about $4.4$ percentage points. The town wants a margin of error closer to $2$ points, still at $95\%$. What should the pollster change?
@step Lowering the confidence level would shrink the margin, but the town wants to keep $95\%$.
@step A larger sample gives a smaller margin of error: about $2.2$ points with $2000$ people.
@answer Survey a larger random sample, about $2000$ people.
:::

:::tip Diminishing returns
Doubling the sample does not halve the margin of error. In the numbers above, going from $500$ to $2000$ people (four times as many) only cuts the margin of error in half.
:::

## Making decisions with confidence intervals

When you compare two results, or compare a result with a target such as $50\%$, use the whole confidence interval, not just the survey result.

:::example A majority?
Can the arena supporters be confident that a majority (more than $50\%$) of voters support the arena?
@step The confidence interval is $50.5\%$ to $57.5\%$.
@step Every value in the interval is above $50\%$.
@answer Yes. We can be $95\%$ confident that a majority of voters support the arena, though only just.
:::

:::example A close race
In a poll for mayor, Tran has $46\%$ and Bouchard has $41\%$, each accurate to within $3.5$ percentage points, 19 times out of 20. Can Tran be confident of being ahead?
@step Tran: $46 \pm 3.5$ gives $42.5\%$ to $49.5\%$.
@step Bouchard: $41 \pm 3.5$ gives $37.5\%$ to $44.5\%$.
@step The intervals overlap from $42.5\%$ to $44.5\%$, so Bouchard could actually be ahead.
@answer No. The race is too close to call.
:::

:::example Estimating a number of people
Suppose the town has $18\,000$ voters. Use the arena poll to estimate how many support the arena.
@step Least: $0.505 \times 18\,000 = 9090$
@step Greatest: $0.575 \times 18\,000 = 10\,350$
@answer Between about $9090$ and $10\,350$ voters support the arena, 19 times out of 20.
:::

:::summary
- A survey of a random sample estimates a population value. The confidence interval is the survey result $\pm$ the margin of error.
- The survey result is the midpoint of the interval, and the margin of error is half its width.
- The confidence level (such as 19 times out of 20, or $95\%$) tells how often the method captures the true value. It is not the percent of people who agree.
- A larger sample gives a smaller margin of error. A higher confidence level gives a wider interval.
- To make a decision, check whether the whole interval is above a target, or whether two intervals overlap.
:::
