A manager at a coffee shop notices that hot chocolate sales jump on every day colder than −15 °C, and starts ordering extra milk before cold snaps. Spotting a pattern and making an educated guess from it is called **inductive reasoning**. It is how most discoveries in mathematics and science begin.

## Conjectures and inductive reasoning

:::definition Conjecture and inductive reasoning
- A **conjecture** is a statement that you believe is true, based on evidence such as examples or observations, but that has not been proved.
- **Inductive reasoning** means looking at specific examples, finding a pattern, and using it to make a general conjecture.
:::

To make a conjecture, collect several examples, look for what they have in common, and state the pattern as a general rule.

:::example Sums of odd numbers
Liam adds some pairs of odd numbers: $3 + 5 = 8$, $7 + 11 = 18$, $15 + 21 = 36$ and $9 + 13 = 22$. Make a conjecture.
@step List the results: $8$, $18$, $36$ and $22$.
@step Look for what they have in common. They are all even. (They are not all multiples of 4 or of 3, so those patterns do not fit.)
@answer Conjecture: the sum of two odd numbers is even.
:::

## Choosing the conjecture that fits

Several conjectures can seem reasonable. A good conjecture must fit **every** example. One example that does not fit is enough to rule a conjecture out.

:::example Testing three conjectures
Sakura finds that $4 + 5 + 6 = 15$, $10 + 11 + 12 = 33$ and $21 + 22 + 23 = 66$. Which conjecture fits: the sum of three consecutive integers is odd, is a multiple of 6, or is three times the middle integer?
@step "Is odd" does not fit, because $66$ is even.
@step "Is a multiple of 6" does not fit, because $15$ is not a multiple of 6.
@step "Three times the middle integer": $3 \times 5 = 15$, $3 \times 11 = 33$ and $3 \times 22 = 66$. It fits every example.
@answer The sum of three consecutive integers is three times the middle integer.
:::

:::mistake Evidence is not proof
A conjecture that fits many examples can still be false. More examples make you more confident, but they never prove that a conjecture is always true. In Lesson 1.2 you will see that one counterexample shows a conjecture is false, and in Lesson 1.3 you will prove conjectures.
:::

:::

## Conjectures about number patterns

Patterns in calculations often lead to surprising conjectures. Look at how each line changes from the one before.

$$1 \times 9 + 2 = 11 \qquad 12 \times 9 + 3 = 111 \qquad 123 \times 9 + 4 = 1111$$

Each line adds the next digit to the first number and adds one more to the number being added. The answers gain one more 1 each time, so the conjecture predicts $1234 \times 9 + 5 = 11\,111$. A calculator confirms it.

:::example Predicting with a pattern
Ama notices that $37 \times 3 = 111$, $37 \times 6 = 222$ and $37 \times 9 = 333$. Predict $37 \times 24$.
@step The second number goes up by 3 each time, and the answer is a three-digit number with repeated digits.
@step Conjecture: $37 \times (3 \times k)$ is the digit $k$ written three times.
@step $24 = 3 \times 8$, so the prediction is $888$.
@answer $37 \times 24 = 888$ (a calculator agrees).
:::

:::

Figure 1 uses $4$ toothpicks, Figure 2 uses $7$ and Figure 3 uses $10$. Each new square shares a side with the one before, so it adds only $3$ toothpicks. That leads to a conjecture: Figure $n$ uses $3n + 1$ toothpicks. It fits all three figures: $3(1) + 1 = 4$, $3(2) + 1 = 7$ and $3(3) + 1 = 10$.

:::example Using a pattern rule
How many toothpicks does Figure 25 need? Which figure uses exactly $100$ toothpicks?
@step Figure 25: $3(25) + 1 = 76$ toothpicks.
@step Solve $3n + 1 = 100$: $3n = 99$, so $n = 33$.
@answer Figure 25 needs $76$ toothpicks, and Figure 33 uses exactly $100$.
:::

:::tip Check your rule on every figure
A rule such as $4n$ fits Figure 1 ($4$ toothpicks) but not Figure 2 ($8 \ne 7$). Always test a rule on every figure you can see before you use it.

:::summary
- Inductive reasoning uses specific examples to make a general conjecture.
- A conjecture must fit every example; one example that does not fit rules it out.
- Number and geometric patterns can be described by a rule, such as $3n + 1$, that predicts later cases.
- Test a rule on all the cases you have before using it.
- Supporting examples make a conjecture more believable, but they do not prove it.
:::
