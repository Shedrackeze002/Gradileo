Do students who sleep more get better marks? Has the wildfire season in our region changed over the past twenty years? A mathematics research project lets you answer a question you care about with real data. A strong project starts with a good plan: a clear question, trustworthy data and a fair way to collect it.

## Choosing a research question

:::definition Research question
A **research question** is the question your project sets out to answer. A good research question is:

- **focused**: it is about one clear relationship or change, in a specific group, place or time;
- **measurable**: it can be answered by collecting and analyzing data;
- **interesting**: the answer matters to you and to your audience;
- **neutral**: its wording does not assume the answer.
:::

| Weak question | Problem |
|---|---|
| How does sleep affect people? | too broad |
| Is sleep important? | asks for an opinion |
| How many hours of sleep do doctors recommend? | answered by a single fact |
| Why do lazy students who stay up late get bad marks? | biased: assumes the answer |

:::example Improving a question
Kiona’s first idea is “How does sleep affect people?” Improve it.
@step Narrow the group, place and time: Grade 11 students at her school, on school nights.
@step Choose things that can be measured: hours of sleep and average marks.
@step Ask about a relationship, with neutral wording.
@answer “Is there a relationship between the hours of sleep Grade 11 students at our school get on school nights and their average marks?”
:::

:::mistake A question that is too big
“What makes hockey teams good?” could fill a book, and no single data set answers it. Pick one measurable piece: “Is there a relationship between an NHL team’s average shots on goal per game and its number of wins in a season?”
:::

## Primary and secondary data

:::definition Primary and secondary data
**Primary data** is data you collect yourself, for example with a survey, an experiment or your own measurements. **Secondary data** was collected by someone else, such as the national statistics office, a government department, a sports league or another researcher.
:::

:::example Which kind of data?
Classify each source: (a) you time $30$ classmates on a reaction-time test; (b) you download census tables from the national statistics office.
@step (a) You collected the times yourself, so they are primary data.
@step (b) The national statistics office collected the census data, so it is secondary data, even though the source is trusted.
@answer (a) primary data; (b) secondary data
:::

:::tip Using both
Many strong projects use both. Secondary data can give a big picture over many years; primary data can show what is happening at your own school or in your own community.
:::

## Reliable sources and fair questions

Before you use secondary data, ask: **Who** collected it? **Why** did they collect it? **How** was it collected? **When** was it collected? Official records, government agencies and peer-reviewed research are usually reliable. Advertisements, anonymous posts and opinion pieces are not.

:::definition Bias
**Bias** is anything that pushes results away from the truth in one direction. A **biased question** leads people toward a particular answer.
:::

:::example Rewriting a biased question
A survey asks, “Should the city waste millions of dollars on a new recreation centre?” Rewrite it so that it is not biased.
@step The word “waste” tells people what to think.
@step Remove the loaded word and give both sides equally.
@answer “Do you support or oppose building a new recreation centre?”
:::

:::mistake Leading questions
Watch for loaded words (waste, lazy, beautiful, dangerous), openings like “Don’t you agree…” or “Wouldn’t you say…”, and questions that tell people what others think (“Most students hate…”). Each one pushes people toward an answer.
:::

## Fair samples

To learn about a large population, you usually survey a **sample**. In a **random sample**, every member of the population has the same chance of being chosen, so the sample is likely to represent the population fairly.

| Type of bias | What goes wrong | Example |
|---|---|---|
| sampling bias | the sample does not represent the population | surveying only the basketball team about student heights |
| response bias | people give answers that are not accurate | asking about cheating out loud in front of the class |
| non-response bias | many people chosen do not respond | only $60$ of $900$ students answer an email survey |
| measurement bias | the tool or method gives wrong values | a scale that always reads $20$ g too high |

:::example Choosing a sample
Talia wants to know how many hours of homework the students at her large city high school do each week. Which sample is fair: her own math class, an online survey on the school website, or $60$ students chosen at random from the school’s list of all students?
@step Her own class represents only one group of students.
@step An online survey includes only the students who choose to answer.
@step Choosing at random from the full list gives every student the same chance.
@answer The random sample of $60$ students from the full list is the fair choice.
:::

## From plan to presentation

A complete plan answers five questions before you collect any data.

1. **Question:** What exactly am I trying to find out?
2. **Data:** What data will answer it? Is it primary or secondary, and is the source reliable?
3. **Collection:** How will I collect the data fairly, and how much do I need?
4. **Analysis:** Which tools will I use: graphs, the mean and standard deviation, z-scores, or confidence intervals?
5. **Presentation:** How will I share my findings, their limitations and my sources?

:::example A complete plan
Plan a project on the question “How has the date of the first fall frost in our town changed over the past 50 years?”
@step Data: the first fall frost date in each year, from historical weather records published by the national weather service (secondary data).
@step Analysis: graph the date for each year, and compare the mean and standard deviation of the first and last 25 years.
@step Presentation: a short talk with a graph, a statement of the conclusion, its limits (one town only), and a list of sources.
@answer The plan matches the data to the question, uses a reliable source, and chooses analysis tools before any data is collected.
:::

:::summary
- A good research question is focused, measurable, interesting and neutral. Avoid questions that are too broad, ask for an opinion, can be answered with a single fact, or assume the answer.
- Primary data is collected by you; secondary data is collected by someone else.
- Check who collected secondary data, why, how and when. Prefer official records and peer-reviewed research.
- Biased questions and biased samples push results away from the truth. A random sample gives every member of the population the same chance.
- Plan the question, data, collection, analysis and presentation before you start.
:::
