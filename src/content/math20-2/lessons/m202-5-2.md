Measure the heights of a thousand Grade 11 students and draw a histogram. Most heights bunch up near the middle, and very short or very tall students are rare. This bell shape shows up so often (in heights, exam marks, birth masses and machine-filled bottles) that it has its own name: the **normal distribution**.

## The normal curve

The histogram shows the heights of $1000$ Grade 11 students in a large school division. It is tallest in the middle and falls away evenly on both sides.

:::definition Normal distribution
A **normal distribution** has a symmetric, bell-shaped graph called a **normal curve**.

- The mean, median and mode are equal, at the centre of the curve.
- The curve is symmetric about the mean, so half of the data is below the mean and half is above.
- The curve gets closer and closer to the horizontal axis but never touches it.
- The total area under the curve represents $100\%$ of the data.
:::

The mean, $\mu$, sets the centre of the curve, and the standard deviation, $\sigma$, sets how wide it is. The curve below models the heights, with $\mu = 168$ cm and $\sigma = 8$ cm. Its grid lines are one standard deviation apart.

## The 68–95–99.7 rule

:::definition The 68–95–99.7 rule
In a normal distribution, about

- $68\%$ of the data is within $1$ standard deviation of the mean,
- $95\%$ is within $2$ standard deviations of the mean, and
- $99.7\%$ is within $3$ standard deviations of the mean.
:::

The shaded region below is within $1$ standard deviation of the mean, from $168 - 8 = 160$ cm to $168 + 8 = 176$ cm. It holds about $68\%$ of the students.

## Percents of other regions

The curve is symmetric, so each side of the mean holds half of each percent in the rule. Split the curve into regions one standard deviation wide:

| Region (on each side of the mean) | Percent of the data |
|---|---|
| from the mean to $1\sigma$ | $34\%$ |
| from $1\sigma$ to $2\sigma$ | $13.5\%$ |
| from $2\sigma$ to $3\sigma$ | $2.35\%$ |
| beyond $3\sigma$ | $0.15\%$ |

Each side adds to $34 + 13.5 + 2.35 + 0.15 = 50\%$. To find the percent in any region, add the pieces it covers.

:::example Taller than 176 cm
What percent of the students are taller than $176$ cm?
@step $176 = 168 + 8$ is $1$ standard deviation above the mean.
@step Add the regions above it: $13.5\% + 2.35\% + 0.15\% = 16\%$
@answer About $16\%$ of the students are taller than $176$ cm. (Check: $50\% - 34\% = 16\%$.)
:::

:::example Two different distances
What percent of the students are between $160$ cm and $192$ cm tall? Of $600$ students, about how many are taller than $184$ cm?
@step $160$ is $1\sigma$ below the mean and $192 = 168 + 3(8)$ is $3\sigma$ above it.
@step $34\% + 34\% + 13.5\% + 2.35\% = 83.85\%$
@step Taller than $184$ cm (above $2\sigma$): $2.35\% + 0.15\% = 2.5\%$, and $0.025 \times 600 = 15$
@answer About $83.85\%$ are between $160$ cm and $192$ cm, and about $15$ of the $600$ students are taller than $184$ cm.
:::

:::mistake Adding the rule’s big percents
The percents $68\%$, $95\%$ and $99.7\%$ are for regions **centred on the mean**. From $152$ cm to $176$ cm is not $95\% + 68\%$. Add the small regions instead: $13.5\% + 34\% + 34\% = 81.5\%$.
:::

## Is the data normal?

The 68–95–99.7 rule only works for data that is approximately normal. Before you use it, check that:

- a histogram of the data is roughly bell-shaped and symmetric, with one peak in the middle;
- the mean and the median are close together;
- about $68\%$, $95\%$ and $99.7\%$ of the data is within $1$, $2$ and $3$ standard deviations of the mean.

Real data never matches the rule exactly, so look for percents that are **close**.

:::example Bags of flour
A mill weighed $300$ bags of flour. $67\%$ of the masses were within $1$ standard deviation of the mean, $96\%$ within $2$, and $100\%$ within $3$. Is the data approximately normal?
@step Compare with the rule: $67\%$ is close to $68\%$, $96\%$ is close to $95\%$, and $100\%$ is close to $99.7\%$.
@answer Yes. The masses are approximately normally distributed.
:::

:::example Working backwards
The masses of the bags are normally distributed, and about $95\%$ of them are between $990$ g and $1010$ g. Find the mean and the standard deviation.
@step The mean is in the middle: $\mu = \dfrac{990 + 1010}{2} = 1000$
@step $95\%$ is within $2\sigma$ on each side, so the interval is $4\sigma$ wide: $4\sigma = 1010 - 990 = 20$
@answer $\mu = 1000$ g and $\sigma = 5$ g.
:::

:::check m202-normal-percent 3
:::

:::summary
- A normal distribution is symmetric and bell-shaped, with the mean, median and mode at the centre. The area under the curve is $100\%$ of the data.
- About $68\%$, $95\%$ and $99.7\%$ of normal data is within $1$, $2$ and $3$ standard deviations of the mean.
- Each side of the mean splits into $34\%$, $13.5\%$, $2.35\%$ and $0.15\%$. Add the regions you need.
- Multiply a percent by the total to estimate a number of people or items.
- Data is approximately normal when its histogram is bell-shaped and its percents are close to the 68–95–99.7 rule.
:::
