Ice keeps its shape on a plate. Pour water and it spreads across the table. Let steam out of a kettle and it fills the whole kitchen. All three are water, so why do they behave so differently? The answer is one of the most useful ideas in science: everything is made of tiny particles.

## Solids, liquids and gases

:::definition States of matter
Matter on Earth is usually found in one of three **states**: **solid**, **liquid** or **gas**. The same substance can be in any of them: water can be ice (solid), water (liquid) or steam (gas). We name the states because each one behaves in its own way.

| | Solid | Liquid | Gas |
|---|---|---|---|
| Shape | keeps its own shape | takes the shape of its container | fills its whole container |
| Volume | fixed | fixed | not fixed |
| Does it flow? | no | yes | yes |
| Can it be squashed? | no | hardly at all | yes, easily |
:::

:::example Naming a state
A substance can be poured, and it takes the shape of the bottom of any container. Its volume does not change. What state is it in?
@step It flows, so it is not a solid.
@step It keeps a fixed volume and does not fill the whole container, so it is not a gas.
@answer It is a liquid.
:::

## The particle model

:::definition Particle model
The **particle model** says that:

- all matter is made of **particles**, far too small to see;
- the particles are **always moving**;
- the particles attract one another, more strongly the closer they are;
- the particles never change size. Only their spacing and movement change.
:::

The model explains each state:

- In a **solid**, the particles are packed tightly, usually in neat rows. They are held in place and can only **vibrate** on the spot. So a solid keeps its shape and cannot be squashed.
- In a **liquid**, the particles are still touching, but jumbled up, and they **slide past one another**. So a liquid flows, but it is hard to squash because there is almost no space between the particles.
- In a **gas**, the particles are **far apart** and move quickly in all directions. So a gas spreads out to fill its container, and it can be squashed because there is lots of empty space.

:::example Explaining with particles
Why can the air in a sealed syringe be pushed in, but water in a sealed syringe cannot?
@step Air is a gas: its particles are far apart, so pushing the plunger moves them closer together.
@step Water is a liquid: its particles are already touching, so there is no space to push them into.
@answer The gas can be squashed because its particles are far apart; the liquid cannot because its particles touch.
:::

:::mistake Particles in a solid still move
A solid stays still, but its particles do not. They vibrate all the time; they just cannot leave their places. The particles of every substance are always moving.
:::

:::mistake Particles do not shrink
When a gas is squashed, its particles do not get smaller. The spaces between them do.
:::

:::summary
- The three states are **solid**, **liquid** and **gas**.
- A solid has a fixed shape and volume; a liquid has a fixed volume and flows; a gas fills its container and can be squashed.
- The **particle model**: matter is made of tiny particles that are always moving and never change size.
- Solid particles are packed tightly and vibrate on the spot; liquid particles touch but slide past each other; gas particles are far apart and move quickly.
:::
