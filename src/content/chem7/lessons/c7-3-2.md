Stir a spoonful of sugar into water and it vanishes. It is still there, though: the water tastes sweet, and the glass weighs exactly as much as the water and sugar did before. In this lesson you will learn the words for the parts of a solution, and how to say how strong a solution is.

## Solutes, solvents and solutions

:::definition Solution, solute and solvent
A **solution** forms when one substance **dissolves** in another.

- The **solute** is the substance that dissolves (the sugar).
- The **solvent** is the substance it dissolves in (the water).
- The **solution** is the mixture that forms (the sugary water).

Water dissolves so many substances that it is called the universal solvent, but other solvents are used too: alcohol, acetone in nail varnish remover, and oils.
:::

When a solute dissolves, its particles spread out between the particles of the solvent. They become too small to see, but they are not destroyed. So **mass is conserved**:

mass of solution = mass of solute + mass of solvent

:::example The mass of a solution
25 g of salt is dissolved in 200 g of water. What is the mass of the solution?
@step Nothing is lost when the salt dissolves.
@answer $25 + 200 = 225$ g.
:::

:::mistake Dissolving is not melting
Sugar in tea does not melt: it would need a temperature of over 180 °C to do that. It **dissolves**: its particles spread out between the water particles.
:::

A substance that dissolves in a solvent is **soluble** in it; one that does not, such as sand in water, is **insoluble**.

## Concentration

A cup of tea with one spoonful of sugar is less sweet than one with three. The amount of solute in a solution is its concentration.

:::definition Concentration
The **concentration** of a solution is the mass of solute dissolved in each litre of solution, in grams per litre (g/L). We use it to say exactly how strong a solution is.

$$\text{concentration} = \frac{\text{mass of solute (g)}}{\text{volume of solution (L)}}$$

A **concentrated** solution has a lot of solute in a little solvent; a **dilute** solution has only a little solute.
:::

:::example Calculating a concentration
4 g of salt is dissolved to make 250 mL of solution. What is the concentration?
@step The volume must be in litres: $250\ \text{mL} = 0.25\ \text{L}$.
@step $\text{concentration} = \dfrac{4}{0.25} = 16$.
@answer $16\ \text{g/L}$.
:::

Turning the formula round gives the mass of solute needed: mass = concentration × volume. To make 2 L of a 15 g/L solution, you need $15 \times 2 = 30$ g of solute.

:::tip Diluting
Adding more solvent makes a solution more dilute: the same mass of solute is spread through a bigger volume, so each litre holds less.
:::

:::summary
- The **solute** dissolves in the **solvent** to make a **solution**.
- Dissolved particles spread out but are not destroyed: mass of solution = mass of solute + mass of solvent.
- **Concentration** (g/L) = mass of solute (g) ÷ volume of solution (L). Change mL to L first.
- Concentrated solutions have a lot of solute per litre; dilute solutions have a little.
:::
