We say a drink is hot or a day is cold, but what does "hot" mean to the particles of the drink? And why does a metal spoon in hot soup quickly get too hot to hold, while a wooden one does not? In this lesson you will meet temperature and heat, and the three ways heat moves.

## Temperature

:::definition Temperature
**Temperature** tells how hot or cold something is. In the particle model, it is a measure of how fast the particles are moving, on average: the faster they move, the higher the temperature. We measure temperature with a **thermometer**, in degrees Celsius (°C). On the Celsius scale, pure water freezes at 0 °C and boils at 100 °C.
:::

Heating a substance makes its particles move faster, so its temperature rises. Cooling slows them down. They never stop completely.

Temperatures below 0 °C are written with a minus sign. To find a change in temperature, count from one reading to the other; a number line helps when one of them is below zero.

:::example A temperature change
The temperature on a winter morning is −6 °C. By afternoon it is 4 °C. By how much did it rise?
@step From −6 °C up to 0 °C is 6 degrees.
@step From 0 °C up to 4 °C is 4 more degrees.
@answer It rose by 10 °C.
:::

## Heat

:::definition Heat
**Heat** is energy that moves from a hotter object to a cooler one. It always flows from hot to cold, until both are at the same temperature.
:::

Heat and temperature are not the same. A bath of warm water has a lower temperature than a cup of boiling water, but it holds far more heat energy, because it has far more particles. Temperature tells how fast the particles move; the total heat energy also depends on how many there are.

:::mistake Cold does not flow
When you hold an ice cube, your hand feels cold because heat flows out of your hand into the ice. Nothing called "cold" flows into your hand.
:::

## How heat moves

Heat moves in three ways.

:::definition Conduction, convection and radiation
- **Conduction**: heat passes from particle to particle through a material, as faster-vibrating particles knock into their neighbours. It happens best in solids, and best of all in metals.
- **Convection**: heat is carried by a liquid or gas that moves. Warm liquid or gas rises, and cooler liquid or gas sinks to take its place, making a **convection current**.
- **Radiation**: heat travels as invisible rays, which need no particles at all. This is how the Sun's heat crosses empty space to reach Earth.
:::

A material that lets heat through easily is a **conductor**, such as copper, iron and aluminium. A material that lets heat through slowly is an **insulator**, such as wood, plastic, wool and air. Woolly jumpers and double glazing keep us warm because they trap still air, a good insulator.

:::example Naming the way heat moves
A pan of water is heated from below. The water at the bottom warms, rises, and cooler water sinks to be heated in turn. How is heat moving through the water?
@step The water itself is moving and carrying the heat with it.
@answer Convection.
:::

:::summary
- **Temperature**, measured in °C with a thermometer, tells how fast the particles move on average.
- **Heat** is energy moving from a hotter object to a cooler one.
- **Conduction**: through touching particles, best in metals. **Convection**: carried by moving liquids and gases. **Radiation**: as rays, even through empty space.
- **Conductors** let heat through easily; **insulators**, such as wood, plastic, wool and air, let it through slowly.
:::
