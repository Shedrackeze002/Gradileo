Chemists measure all the time: how much of a solid to weigh out, how much water to add, how much gas a reaction gives off. A measurement is only useful if it is read correctly and has the right unit. In this lesson you will measure mass and volume, find the volume of an odd-shaped object, and change between units.

## Measuring mass and volume

:::definition Measuring mass and volume
- **Mass** is measured with a **balance**, in grams (g) or kilograms (kg).
- The volume of a **liquid** is measured with a **measuring cylinder**, in millilitres (mL) or litres (L).
- The volume of a **solid** is measured in cubic centimetres (cm³). One cubic centimetre is exactly the same volume as one millilitre: **1 cm³ = 1 mL**.
:::

Water in a narrow measuring cylinder curves up where it touches the glass. This curved surface is called the **meniscus**. To read the volume, put your eye level with the surface and read the scale at the **bottom of the meniscus**.

Before reading, work out what each small division is worth. If the scale goes up in 10 mL between labelled marks and there are 10 small divisions, each one is 1 mL.

:::example Reading a scale
On a measuring cylinder each small division is 2 mL. The bottom of the meniscus is 7 divisions above the 40 mL mark. What is the volume?
@step Seven divisions of 2 mL each add $7 \times 2 = 14$ mL.
@answer $40 + 14 = 54$ mL.
:::

:::mistake Reading from above
Looking down at the scale from above makes the reading look too high; looking up from below makes it look too low. Crouch so your eye is level with the meniscus.
:::

## Volume by displacement

A box has a volume you can calculate: length × width × height. But what about a stone, a key or a toy car? For an irregular solid that sinks, we use **displacement**.

:::definition Displacement
When a solid sinks in water, it pushes aside, or **displaces**, a volume of water equal to its own volume. So the rise in the water level in a measuring cylinder is the volume of the solid:

volume of the solid = final reading − first reading
:::

:::example A stone in a cylinder
A measuring cylinder holds 35 mL of water. A stone is lowered in and the water rises to 48 mL. What is the volume of the stone?
@step The rise is $48 - 35 = 13$ mL.
@step $1\ \text{mL} = 1\ \text{cm}^3$.
@answer The stone's volume is $13\ \text{cm}^3$.
:::

For very small objects, put several identical ones in together, then divide the total volume by how many there are. This gives a more accurate result than measuring just one.

## Changing units

Units of mass and volume are linked by 1000s.

| Mass | Volume |
|---|---|
| 1 kg = 1000 g | 1 L = 1000 mL |
| 1 g = 1000 mg | 1 mL = 1 cm³, so 1 L = 1000 cm³ |

To change to a **smaller** unit (kg to g, L to mL), **multiply** by 1000: there will be more of the smaller units. To change to a **bigger** unit, **divide** by 1000.

:::example Converting
Convert 2.5 L to millilitres, and 750 g to kilograms.
@step Litres to millilitres is to a smaller unit, so multiply: $2.5 \times 1000 = 2500$ mL.
@step Grams to kilograms is to a bigger unit, so divide: $750 \div 1000 = 0.75$ kg.
@answer 2.5 L = 2500 mL, and 750 g = 0.75 kg.
:::

:::tip Same units before you add
To add 2 kg of flour and 350 g of sugar, change both to grams first: 2000 g + 350 g = 2350 g.
:::

:::summary
- Measure mass with a **balance** and the volume of a liquid with a **measuring cylinder**, reading at the bottom of the **meniscus** with your eye level.
- **1 mL = 1 cm³.**
- The volume of an irregular solid is the rise in the water level when it sinks: **displacement**.
- 1 kg = 1000 g, 1 g = 1000 mg and 1 L = 1000 mL. Multiply by 1000 to go to the smaller unit; divide by 1000 to go to the bigger one.
:::
