You can keep stirring sugar into a cup of tea for a while, but at some point the extra sugar just sits at the bottom. There is a limit to how much of a solute any solvent can hold. That limit is called **solubility**, and it changes with temperature.

## Solubility

:::definition Solubility
The **solubility** of a substance is the greatest mass of it that can dissolve in a fixed amount of solvent, at a given temperature. It is usually given as grams per 100 g of water. We use it to compare how well substances dissolve, and to predict how much will dissolve.
:::

For most solids, solubility **rises as the temperature rises**: hot water dissolves more sugar than cold water. Solubility tables give the values at several temperatures.

| Temperature (°C) | 0 | 20 | 40 | 60 | 80 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| potassium nitrate (g per 100 g water) | 13 | 32 | 64 | 110 | 169 |
| sodium chloride (salt) | 36 | 36 | 37 | 37 | 38 |
| copper sulfate | 14 | 20 | 29 | 40 | 55 |

The table shows that temperature makes a huge difference to potassium nitrate, but hardly any difference to salt.

:::example Reading a solubility table
How much potassium nitrate dissolves in 100 g of water at 40 °C? How much in 50 g of water at 40 °C?
@step From the table, 64 g dissolves in 100 g of water at 40 °C.
@step 50 g of water is half as much, so half as much dissolves: $64 \div 2 = 32$ g.
@answer 64 g in 100 g of water, and 32 g in 50 g of water.
:::

If you add more solute than can dissolve, the extra stays as solid. And if a full, hot solution is cooled, it can hold less, so the extra comes out as **crystals**.

:::example Crystals from cooling
A solution of potassium nitrate in 100 g of water holds all it can at 60 °C. It is cooled to 20 °C. What mass of crystals forms?
@step At 60 °C it holds 110 g. At 20 °C it can hold only 32 g.
@answer $110 - 32 = 78$ g of crystals.
:::

## Saturated and unsaturated solutions

:::definition Saturated and unsaturated
A **saturated solution** holds as much solute as it can at that temperature. Any extra solute stays undissolved. An **unsaturated solution** could still dissolve more solute.
:::

To tell which you have, add a little more solute and stir. If it dissolves, the solution was unsaturated. If it stays as solid, the solution is saturated.

:::example Saturated or not?
At 20 °C, 36 g of salt dissolves in 100 g of water. 30 g of salt is stirred into 100 g of water at 20 °C. Is the solution saturated?
@step 30 g is less than the 36 g that can dissolve.
@answer No: all the salt dissolves, and the solution is unsaturated. It could take another 6 g.
:::

:::mistake Insoluble is not the same as saturated
Sand at the bottom of a glass of water is **insoluble**: none of it ever dissolves. Sugar at the bottom of a glass of sugary water is there because the solution is **saturated**: some sugar has dissolved, but no more can.
:::

:::summary
- **Solubility** is the most solute that can dissolve in a fixed amount of solvent (often 100 g of water) at a given temperature.
- For most solids, solubility rises with temperature.
- Scale a table value to other masses of water; any extra solute stays undissolved; cooling a saturated solution gives crystals.
- A **saturated** solution can dissolve no more solute; an **unsaturated** one can.
:::
