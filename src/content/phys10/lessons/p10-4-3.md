Rest a finger lightly on your throat and hum. You can feel the vibration that is making the sound. Every sound starts this way, with something vibrating, and reaches your ear as a wave travelling through the air.

## Sound waves

:::definition Sound
**Sound** is a longitudinal wave made by a vibrating object. The vibrations pass from particle to particle through a solid, a liquid or a gas as a series of compressions and rarefactions.
:::

Because sound is passed on by particles, it **cannot travel through a vacuum**. If a ringing bell is sealed in a jar and the air is pumped out, the sound fades away, although the bell can still be seen ringing.

Sound travels fastest in solids, where the particles are closest together, slower in liquids, and slowest in gases. In air its speed is about 340 m/s.

What we hear depends on two features of the wave:

| What we hear | Feature of the wave |
|---|---|
| a **higher pitch** | a higher frequency |
| a **louder** sound | a larger amplitude |

Human ears can hear frequencies from about 20 Hz to 20 000 Hz. Sound above 20 000 Hz is called **ultrasound**. It is too high for us to hear, but it is used in medical scans, and bats and dolphins use it to find their way.

:::example Pitch and loudness
A guitar string is plucked gently, then the same string is plucked hard. What changes about the sound wave?
@step The same string vibrates at the same frequency, so the pitch does not change.
@step A harder pluck makes bigger vibrations.
@answer The amplitude increases, so the note is louder. The frequency and the pitch stay the same.
:::

:::mistake Thinking loud sounds travel faster
Loudness and pitch do not change the speed of sound. A shout and a whisper cross a room in the same time. The speed depends only on what the sound is travelling through.
:::

## The speed of sound and echoes

Sound travels at a steady speed, so distance = speed × time. Light is so much faster that a flash is seen almost at the moment it happens, and the delay before the bang tells you how far away it was.

:::example How far away is the storm?
Sound travels at 340 m/s in air. Thunder is heard 4 s after the lightning flash is seen. How far away is the storm?
@step $d = vt = 340 \times 4$.
@answer The storm is 1360 m away.
:::

An **echo** is a sound reflected from a hard surface such as a wall or a cliff. The sound travels to the surface and back again, so it covers **twice** the distance.

:::example An echo from a cliff
Sound travels at 340 m/s in air. A person shouts towards a cliff and hears the echo 3 s later. How far away is the cliff?
@step Distance there and back: $340 \times 3 = 1020$ m.
@step Halve it: $1020 \div 2$.
@answer The cliff is 510 m away.
:::

:::example Measuring the depth of the sea
Sound travels at 1500 m/s in sea water. A ship sends a pulse of sound straight down, and the echo from the sea bed returns 0.4 s later. How deep is the sea?
@step Distance down and back: $1500 \times 0.4 = 600$ m.
@step Halve it: $600 \div 2$.
@answer The sea is 300 m deep.
:::

:::example Measuring the speed of sound
A student stands 170 m from a large wall and claps. The echo is heard 1 s after the clap. What speed of sound does this give?
@step The sound travels to the wall and back: $2 \times 170 = 340$ m.
@step $v = \dfrac{340}{1}$.
@answer The speed of sound is 340 m/s.
:::

:::mistake Forgetting the return journey
For an echo, the time is for the trip there **and back**. In the cliff example, $340 \times 3 = 1020$ m is the whole path of the sound. The cliff is half of that away.
:::

:::summary
- Sound is a longitudinal wave made by vibrations. It needs a material to travel through.
- Sound is fastest in solids and slowest in gases, about 340 m/s in air.
- Higher frequency means higher pitch. Larger amplitude means louder.
- Humans hear from about 20 Hz to 20 000 Hz. Above that is ultrasound.
- Distance = speed × time. For an echo, halve the distance there and back.
:::
