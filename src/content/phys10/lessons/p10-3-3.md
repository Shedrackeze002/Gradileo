Lifting a bucket of water takes work. That energy is not lost: it is stored in the raised bucket, and you get it back, as motion, if the bucket falls. Energy stored by lifting something against gravity is called gravitational potential energy.

## Gravitational potential energy

:::definition Gravitational potential energy
**Gravitational potential energy** is the energy an object has because of its height. Lifting an object of mass $m$ through a height $h$ stores

$$\text{gravitational potential energy} = \text{mass} \times g \times \text{height} \qquad E_p = mgh$$

With mass in kg, $g$ in N/kg and height in m, the energy is in joules. On Earth, $g = 9.8$ N/kg.
:::

The equation comes straight from work. To lift an object steadily you need an upward force equal to its weight, $mg$, and you move it through a distance $h$. So the work done is force × distance $= mg \times h$.

:::example Finding the energy
On Earth, $g = 9.8$ N/kg. A crate of mass 20 kg is lifted through 5 m. How much gravitational potential energy does it gain?
@step $E_p = mgh = 20 \times 9.8 \times 5$.
@answer $E_p = 980$ J.
:::

$$h = \dfrac{E_p}{mg} \qquad m = \dfrac{E_p}{gh}$$

:::example Finding the height
On Earth, $g = 9.8$ N/kg. A 5 kg bucket of water gains 490 J of gravitational potential energy. Through what height does it rise?
@step $h = \dfrac{E_p}{mg} = \dfrac{490}{5 \times 9.8} = \dfrac{490}{49}$.
@answer $h = 10$ m.
:::

:::example Finding the mass
On Earth, $g = 9.8$ N/kg. An object gains 2940 J of gravitational potential energy when it is lifted through 15 m. What is its mass?
@step $m = \dfrac{E_p}{gh} = \dfrac{2940}{9.8 \times 15} = \dfrac{2940}{147}$.
@answer $m = 20$ kg.
:::

:::mistake Leaving out g
Mass × height is not an energy. The force that has to be overcome is the weight, $mg$, so the 9.8 must be there: for the crate above, $20 \times 5 = 100$ is wrong and $20 \times 9.8 \times 5 = 980$ J is right.
:::

## Changes in height

What matters is the **change** in height, not where you measure it from. Going up, an object gains gravitational potential energy. Coming down, it loses the same amount.

$$\text{change in energy} = \text{mass} \times g \times \text{change in height}$$

:::example Moving up
On Earth, $g = 9.8$ N/kg. A 10 kg load is raised from a height of 2 m to a height of 12 m. By how much does its gravitational potential energy change?
@step Change in height: $12 - 2 = 10$ m.
@step $10 \times 9.8 \times 10$.
@answer It gains 980 J.
:::

:::example Moving down
On Earth, $g = 9.8$ N/kg. A 50 kg load is lowered from 25 m to 10 m. By how much does its gravitational potential energy change?
@step Change in height: $10 - 25 = -15$ m.
@step $50 \times 9.8 \times (-15)$.
@answer $-7350$ J. The minus sign shows that it loses 7350 J.
:::

:::example Climbing stairs
On Earth, $g = 9.8$ N/kg. A person of mass 60 kg climbs 20 stairs, each 0.25 m high. How much gravitational potential energy does the person gain?
@step Total height: $20 \times 0.25 = 5$ m.
@step $E_p = 60 \times 9.8 \times 5$.
@answer $E_p = 2940$ J.
:::

:::mistake Using the length of the path
Only the vertical height counts. A person who walks up a long, gentle ramp to a platform 3 m high gains exactly the same gravitational potential energy as one who climbs a ladder straight up to it.
:::

:::summary
- Gravitational potential energy is the energy of height: $E_p = mgh$, in joules.
- On Earth, $g = 9.8$ N/kg.
- More mass or more height means more gravitational potential energy.
- Only the change in vertical height matters: up is a gain, down is a loss.
:::
