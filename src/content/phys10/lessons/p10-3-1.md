In everyday speech, "work" means anything that tires you out. In physics it means something exact: a force doing work is how energy gets moved from one place to another. This lesson shows how to measure it.

## Work done by a force

:::definition Work
**Work** is done when a force moves an object in the direction of the force.

$$\text{work} = \text{force} \times \text{distance moved in the direction of the force} \qquad W = Fd$$

With force in newtons and distance in metres, work is in **joules (J)**. One joule is the work done when a force of 1 N moves an object 1 m.
:::

Doing work **transfers energy**. If you do 200 J of work pushing a trolley, 200 J of energy leaves you and goes to the trolley and its surroundings. This is why work and energy share the same unit.

$$F = \dfrac{W}{d} \qquad d = \dfrac{W}{F}$$

:::example Finding the work
A student pushes a crate with a steady force of 80 N for 5 m, in the direction of the force. How much work is done?
@step $W = Fd = 80 \times 5$.
@answer $W = 400$ J.
:::

:::example Finding the force
A sledge is pulled 20 m, and 1500 J of work is done. How large is the force along the motion?
@step $F = \dfrac{W}{d} = \dfrac{1500}{20}$.
@answer $F = 75$ N.
:::

:::example Finding the distance
A force of 150 N does 900 J of work on a trolley. How far does the trolley move?
@step $d = \dfrac{W}{F} = \dfrac{900}{150}$.
@answer $d = 6$ m.
:::

:::example Work in kilojoules
A tow truck pulls a car with a steady force of 1200 N for 50 m. How much work does it do, in kilojoules?
@step $W = 1200 \times 50 = 60\,000$ J.
@step 1 kJ = 1000 J, so divide by 1000.
@answer $W = 60$ kJ.
:::

## When is work done?

Work needs **both** a force and a movement along the force. Take either one away and no work is done on the object, however much effort it seems to take.

| Situation | Work done on the object? | Why |
|---|---|---|
| A crane lifts a crate | yes | the upward force moves the crate upwards |
| A person pushes a wall that does not move | no | there is a force but no distance |
| A weightlifter holds a bar still overhead | no | the bar does not move |
| A waiter carries a tray across a level room | no work by the upward force | the force is upwards but the motion is sideways |

When work is done, the energy has to go somewhere. Lifting stores it as height. Speeding an object up stores it as motion. Pushing against friction at a steady speed warms the surfaces.

:::example Where does the energy go?
A box is pushed across a rough floor at a steady speed. The push does 300 J of work. What happens to this energy?
@step The speed is steady, so the energy of motion is not growing.
@step The floor is level, so no energy is stored as height.
@answer All 300 J ends up as heat in the box and the floor, because the work is done against friction.
:::

:::mistake Thinking effort is work
Holding a heavy bag still makes your muscles tired, but the bag is not moving, so no work is done **on the bag**. Work is counted on the object, from the force and the distance it moves.
:::

:::mistake Counting the time
Work depends on the force and the distance only. Pushing a crate 5 m with 80 N takes 400 J whether it takes two seconds or two minutes. How quickly the work is done is a different quantity, called power.
:::

:::summary
- Work = force × distance moved in the direction of the force: $W = Fd$, in joules.
- 1 J is the work done when 1 N moves an object 1 m, and 1 kJ = 1000 J.
- No movement, or a force at right angles to the movement, means no work is done.
- Doing work transfers energy to the object or to its surroundings.
:::
