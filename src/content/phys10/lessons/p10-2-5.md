Friction is the reason a sliding book stops, and also the reason you can walk at all. Air resistance is the reason a skydiver does not keep speeding up all the way to the ground. Both are forces that act against motion, and both decide how real objects move.

## Friction

:::definition Friction
**Friction** is the force between two surfaces that touch, acting **against** the sliding of one over the other. It always points opposite to the direction of sliding.

Rough, dry surfaces give more friction. Smooth, wet or oiled surfaces give less. Friction turns some of the energy of motion into heat.
:::

| Friction is useful when | Friction is a nuisance when |
|---|---|
| tyres and shoes grip the ground | machine parts rub and wear out |
| brakes slow a wheel | a bicycle chain wastes energy as heat |
| a knot holds | a drawer sticks |

A **lubricant** such as oil keeps two surfaces slightly apart, so they slide past each other with less friction.

:::example The direction of friction
A crate slides east across a floor. Which way does friction act on it?
@step Friction acts against the sliding.
@answer West.
:::

:::example Stopping by friction
A 5 kg box slides across a floor at 6 m/s. The only force along the floor is friction of 10 N. Find its deceleration and the time it takes to stop.
@step Deceleration: $\dfrac{F}{m} = \dfrac{10}{5} = 2$ m/s².
@step Time: the velocity must fall by 6 m/s, at 2 m/s every second. $t = \dfrac{6}{2}$.
@answer It decelerates at 2 m/s² and stops in 3 s.
:::

:::mistake Thinking friction always slows things down
Friction acts against **sliding**. When you walk, your foot pushes backwards on the ground and friction stops it from sliding back, so friction pushes you forwards.

## Air resistance and terminal velocity

:::definition Air resistance
**Air resistance** (drag) is the force of the air on an object moving through it, acting against the motion. It grows as the object moves **faster**, and it is larger for a larger **area** facing the air.
:::

Follow a skydiver from the moment of jumping.

| Stage | Forces | Motion |
|---|---|---|
| just after jumping | weight much larger than air resistance | speeds up quickly |
| falling faster | air resistance growing, net force shrinking | still speeding up, but more gently |
| terminal velocity | air resistance equals weight | steady velocity |
| parachute opens | air resistance larger than weight | slows down to a new, lower terminal velocity |

:::definition Terminal velocity
**Terminal velocity** is the steady velocity a falling object reaches when the air resistance has grown to equal its weight. The net force is then zero, so by the first law the velocity stops changing.
:::

:::example The air resistance at terminal velocity
On Earth, $g = 9.8$ N/kg. A skydiver of mass 70 kg falls at terminal velocity. How large is the air resistance?
@step Weight: $W = mg = 70 \times 9.8 = 686$ N.
@step At terminal velocity the forces are balanced.
@answer The air resistance is 686 N, upwards.
:::

:::example Part of the way there
On Earth, $g = 9.8$ N/kg. A 60 kg skydiver is falling, and at one moment the air resistance is 288 N. Find the net force and the acceleration.
@step Weight: $60 \times 9.8 = 588$ N.
@step Net force: $588 - 288 = 300$ N downwards.
@step $a = \dfrac{300}{60}$.
@answer Net force 300 N downwards, acceleration 5 m/s².
:::

:::mistake Thinking heavy things always fall faster
With no air, as on the Moon, a feather and a hammer fall together. On Earth the feather falls slowly only because air resistance is large compared with its tiny weight.

:::summary
- Friction acts between touching surfaces, against sliding. It can be useful (grip, brakes) or a nuisance (wear, wasted energy).
- Air resistance acts against motion through the air and grows with speed and with area.
- A falling object speeds up until the air resistance equals its weight. It then falls at its terminal velocity.
- Opening a parachute makes the air resistance larger than the weight, so the skydiver slows to a lower terminal velocity.
:::
