A table of times and distances holds everything about a journey, but a graph lets you see it at a glance: where the object was fast, where it rested, where it sped up. Two graphs do most of the work in physics, and each hides a second quantity in its slope.

## Distance-time graphs

:::definition Distance-time graph
A **distance-time graph** shows the distance an object has travelled (up the side) against the time taken (along the bottom).

The **slope** of the line is the **speed**: the steeper the line, the faster the object. A flat line means the object is not moving.
:::

:::figure

:::

| Shape of the line | What the object is doing |
|---|---|
| straight and sloping | moving at a steady speed |
| steeper | moving faster |
| flat | at rest |
| curving upwards | speeding up |

:::example Reading the graph
Use the graph above. Describe the cyclist's ride and find the speed in the first 4 s.
@step From 0 s to 4 s the line is straight and sloping: a steady speed. The distance rises by 20 m in 4 s.
@step From 4 s to 8 s the line is flat: the cyclist is at rest.
@step From 8 s to 12 s the line slopes again, by 20 m in 4 s.
@answer Speed in the first 4 s $= \dfrac{20}{4} = 5$ m/s. The cyclist rides, rests for 4 s, then rides on at the same speed.
:::

:::example Average speed from the graph
What is the cyclist's average speed for the whole 12 s?
@step Total distance: the line ends at 40 m. Total time: 12 s.
@answer Average speed $= \dfrac{40}{12} \approx 3.3$ m/s. It is less than 5 m/s because of the rest.
:::

## Velocity-time graphs

:::definition Velocity-time graph
A **velocity-time graph** shows an object's velocity (up the side) against time (along the bottom).

The **slope** of the line is the **acceleration**. The **area under the line** is the **distance travelled**.
:::

:::figure
{"type":"plane","grid":{"xMin":0,"xMax":12,"yMin":0,"yMax":10,"xStep":2,"yStep":2,"xLabel":"Time (s)","yLabel":"Velocity (m/s)"},"polylines":[{"points":[[0,0],[4,8],[8,8],[12,0]]}],"caption":"Velocity-time graph of a tram"}
:::

| Shape of the line | What the object is doing |
|---|---|
| sloping up | speeding up (positive acceleration) |
| flat, above zero | moving at a steady velocity |
| sloping down | slowing down (negative acceleration) |
| flat, at zero | at rest |

:::example Acceleration from the slope
Use the graph above. Find the tram's acceleration in the first 4 s and in the last 4 s.
@step First 4 s: the velocity rises from 0 to 8 m/s. $a = \dfrac{8 - 0}{4} = 2$ m/s².
@step Last 4 s: the velocity falls from 8 m/s to 0. $a = \dfrac{0 - 8}{4} = -2$ m/s².
@answer $2$ m/s² while speeding up and $-2$ m/s² while slowing down.
:::

:::example Distance from the area
How far does the tram travel in the whole 12 s?
@step Split the area under the line into a triangle, a rectangle and a triangle.
@step First triangle: $\tfrac12 \times 4 \times 8 = 16$ m. Rectangle: $4 \times 8 = 32$ m. Last triangle: $\tfrac12 \times 4 \times 8 = 16$ m.
@answer $16 + 32 + 16 = 64$ m.
:::

:::mistake Reading a flat line as "at rest" on a velocity-time graph
On a distance-time graph a flat line means no motion. On a velocity-time graph a flat line above zero means a **steady velocity**: the tram is still moving, at 8 m/s. Always read the label on the side axis first.
:::

:::summary
- On a distance-time graph, the slope is the speed. A flat line means at rest.
- On a velocity-time graph, the slope is the acceleration and the area under the line is the distance travelled.
- A flat line above zero on a velocity-time graph means a steady velocity, not rest.
:::
