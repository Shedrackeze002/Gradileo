Push hard against a wall while standing on a skateboard and you roll backwards, although you never pushed yourself. The wall pushed you. Forces never come alone: every push is answered by a push back. That is Newton's third law, and it explains how we walk, how fish swim and how rockets fly.

## Newton's third law

:::definition Newton's third law of motion
When object A exerts a force on object B, object B exerts a force on object A that is **equal in size** and **opposite in direction**.

The two forces are called a **third-law pair** (or an action and reaction pair).
:::

Every third-law pair has four features.

| Feature | Example: you push a wall with 50 N |
|---|---|
| equal in size | the wall pushes you with 50 N |
| opposite in direction | you push away from you, the wall pushes towards you |
| on two different objects | one force is on the wall, the other is on you |
| the same kind of force | both are contact pushes |

To find the partner of a force, swap the two objects: "A pushes B" becomes "B pushes A".

:::example Finding the partner
The Earth pulls a falling apple downwards. What is the other force of the pair?
@step The force is "Earth pulls apple". Swap the objects.
@answer The apple pulls the Earth upwards, with an equal force.
:::

:::example How a rocket works
A rocket engine pushes hot gas downwards. What pushes the rocket up?
@step The force is "rocket pushes gas down". Swap the objects.
@answer The gas pushes the rocket upwards. No air is needed, so this works in space.
:::

:::mistake Thinking the pair cancels out
The two forces of a pair act on **different** objects, so they can never cancel each other. Forces balance only when they act on the **same** object. A book on a table is held by its weight and the normal force: these act on the same object, so they balance, but they are not a third-law pair.
:::

## Equal forces, different accelerations

The forces of a pair are equal, but their effects need not be. Each object accelerates by $a = \dfrac{F}{m}$, so the one with less mass accelerates more.

:::example Two skaters
Two skaters stand at rest on smooth ice. Amara (mass 40 kg) pushes Kwame (mass 80 kg) with a force of 200 N. Find each skater's acceleration while they push.
@step By the third law, each skater feels 200 N, in opposite directions.
@step Amara: $a = \dfrac{200}{40} = 5$ m/s². Kwame: $a = \dfrac{200}{80} = 2.5$ m/s².
@answer Amara 5 m/s², Kwame 2.5 m/s², in opposite directions. The lighter skater accelerates more.
:::

:::example A car and a truck
A car of mass 1000 kg collides with a truck of mass 10 000 kg. The force on the car is 30 000 N. Find the force on the truck and the truck's acceleration.
@step By the third law, the force on the truck is also 30 000 N.
@step Truck: $a = \dfrac{30\,000}{10\,000} = 3$ m/s². (The car's is $\dfrac{30\,000}{1000} = 30$ m/s².)
@answer 30 000 N and 3 m/s². The forces are equal, but the car's motion changes ten times as much.
:::

:::example A probe in deep space
A probe of mass 500 kg fires its engine, which pushes gas backwards with 1500 N. What is the probe's acceleration?
@step The gas pushes the probe forwards with 1500 N.
@answer $a = \dfrac{1500}{500} = 3$ m/s².
:::

:::mistake Thinking the bigger object pushes harder
In a collision between a truck and a car, the force on each is exactly the same size. The car is damaged more because the same force acts on a much smaller mass.
:::

:::summary
- Newton's third law: if A exerts a force on B, B exerts an equal and opposite force on A.
- The two forces act on different objects and are the same kind of force, so they never cancel.
- To find the partner of a force, swap the two objects.
- Equal forces give different accelerations when the masses differ: $a = \dfrac{F}{m}$ for each object.
:::
