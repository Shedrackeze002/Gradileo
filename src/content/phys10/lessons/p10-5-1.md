Pull off a jumper in a dark room and you may see tiny sparks and hear them crackle. Flick a switch and a lamp lights at once. Both are the work of electric charge: standing still in the first case, and flowing steadily round a circuit in the second.

## Electric charge

:::definition Electric charge
**Electric charge** is a property of matter that comes in two kinds, **positive** and **negative**. Protons carry positive charge and electrons carry negative charge. Like charges **repel** each other, and unlike charges **attract**. Charge is measured in **coulombs (C)**.
:::

Most objects have equal amounts of positive and negative charge, so they are neutral. Rubbing two materials together can move **electrons** from one to the other. The one that gains electrons becomes negatively charged, and the one that loses them is left positively charged. No charge is created or destroyed: it is only moved.

| | Conductors | Insulators |
|---|---|---|
| Charge | flows through them easily | cannot flow through them |
| Why | some electrons are free to move | all the electrons are held in their atoms |
| Examples | copper, aluminium, graphite, salt water | rubber, glass, plastic, dry wood |

:::example Charging by rubbing
A plastic rod is rubbed with a cloth and becomes negatively charged. What has happened to the cloth?
@step The rod has gained electrons, and they came from the cloth.
@step The cloth now has fewer electrons than protons.
@answer The cloth is positively charged, with a charge equal in size to the rod's.
:::

:::mistake Thinking positive charges move
In solids, the protons are fixed inside the atoms. Only electrons move. An object becomes positively charged by **losing** electrons, not by gaining protons.
:::

## Electric current

Connect a metal wire in a complete loop to a battery, and the free electrons in the wire all drift the same way. This steady flow of charge is an electric current.

:::definition Electric current
**Electric current** is the rate of flow of electric charge.

$$\text{current} = \dfrac{\text{charge}}{\text{time}} \qquad I = \dfrac{Q}{t} \qquad Q = It$$

With charge in coulombs and time in seconds, current is in **amperes (A)**. A current of 1 A carries 1 C of charge past a point each second.
:::

A current flows only in a **complete circuit**: an unbroken loop of conductor with a source of energy, such as a battery. Circuit diagrams show **conventional current** running from the positive terminal round to the negative terminal. The electrons themselves drift the other way, from negative to positive, because they are negatively charged.

:::example Finding the charge
A current of 2 A flows through a lamp for 30 s. How much charge passes through the lamp?
@step $Q = It = 2 \times 30$.
@answer $Q = 60$ C.
:::

:::example Finding the current
A charge of 120 C passes through a heater in 40 s. What is the current?
@step $I = \dfrac{Q}{t} = \dfrac{120}{40}$.
@answer $I = 3$ A.
:::

:::example Finding the time
A current of 0.5 A flows through a lamp. How long does it take for 20 C of charge to pass?
@step $t = \dfrac{Q}{I} = \dfrac{20}{0.5}$.
@answer $t = 40$ s.
:::

:::example Milliamperes and minutes
A current of 200 mA flows for 5 minutes. How much charge passes?
@step Change the units: 200 mA = 0.2 A, and 5 min = 300 s.
@step $Q = It = 0.2 \times 300$.
@answer $Q = 60$ C.
:::

:::mistake Thinking current is used up
The current is the same on both sides of a lamp. The charge is not used up as it goes round: it carries energy from the battery to the lamp, and it is the energy that is transferred.
:::

:::summary
- Charge is positive or negative. Like charges repel, and unlike charges attract.
- Rubbing moves electrons from one object to another. Charge is never created.
- Conductors have electrons that are free to move. Insulators do not.
- Current is the rate of flow of charge: $Q = It$, with current in amperes.
- Conventional current runs from positive to negative. Electrons drift the other way.
:::
