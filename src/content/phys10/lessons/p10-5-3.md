In some strings of decorative lights, one failed bulb puts the whole string out. That is the mark of a series circuit: the components are connected one after another in a single loop, so the charge has only one path to follow.

## Resistance and current in series

:::definition Series circuit
In a **series circuit** the components are connected one after another in a single loop.

- The **current** is the same at every point.
- The **total resistance** is the sum of the resistances: $R = R_1 + R_2 + \dots$
- The **voltages** across the components add up to the supply voltage.
:::

There is only one path, so every coulomb that leaves the battery passes through every component. Adding a resistor in series makes the path harder, so the total resistance goes up and the current goes down.

:::example Total resistance
Resistors of 4 Ω, 6 Ω and 10 Ω are connected in series. What is their total resistance?
@step $R = 4 + 6 + 10$.
@answer $R = 20$ Ω.
:::

:::example Finding the current
A 12 V supply is connected to resistors of 2 Ω and 4 Ω in series. What current flows in the circuit?
@step Total resistance: $2 + 4 = 6$ Ω.
@step $I = \dfrac{V}{R} = \dfrac{12}{6}$.
@answer $I = 2$ A, the same through both resistors.
:::

:::example Finding a missing resistor
A 9 V supply drives a current of 0.5 A through a 6 Ω resistor and a second resistor in series. What is the resistance of the second resistor?
@step Total resistance: $\dfrac{9}{0.5} = 18$ Ω.
@step Subtract the one that is known: $18 - 6$.
@answer The second resistor is 12 Ω.
:::

:::mistake Using one resistor to find the current
The supply pushes charge through **all** the resistors in the loop. In the second example, $12 \div 2 = 6$ A is wrong, because it ignores the 4 Ω resistor. Add the resistances first.
:::

## Sharing the voltage

Each coulomb leaves the supply with a fixed amount of energy and gives it up, part by part, as it passes through the components. So the supply voltage is **shared** between them, and the component with the larger resistance takes the larger share.

:::example Two lamps in series
Two lamps are connected in series to a 12 V battery. The voltage across the first lamp is 5 V. What is the voltage across the second?
@step The voltages add up to 12 V.
@step $12 - 5$.
@answer The voltage across the second lamp is 7 V.
:::

:::example The voltage across each resistor
A 12 V supply is connected to resistors of 2 Ω and 4 Ω in series. Find the voltage across each resistor.
@step Current: $I = \dfrac{12}{2 + 4} = 2$ A.
@step Across the 2 Ω resistor: $V = IR = 2 \times 2 = 4$ V.
@step Across the 4 Ω resistor: $V = IR = 2 \times 4 = 8$ V.
@answer 4 V and 8 V. Together they make the 12 V of the supply.
:::

Adding more lamps in series makes every lamp dimmer, because the same supply voltage is shared between more of them and the current falls. And if one lamp breaks, the loop is broken, so all of them go out.

:::mistake Giving each component the full voltage
In a series circuit, no single component has the whole supply voltage across it, unless it is the only one. The voltages across the components add up to the supply voltage.
:::

:::summary
- A series circuit is a single loop: the current is the same everywhere.
- Total resistance in series: $R = R_1 + R_2 + \dots$
- Current = supply voltage ÷ total resistance.
- The voltages across the components add up to the supply voltage.
- A break anywhere stops the current everywhere.
:::
