A battery does not make charge. The charge is already in the wires. What the battery does is push it round the circuit and give it energy to deliver. How hard it pushes is the voltage, and how much the circuit holds the charge back is the resistance.

## Voltage and resistance

:::definition Voltage
The **voltage** (or potential difference) across a component is the energy transferred by each coulomb of charge that passes through it.

$$\text{voltage} = \dfrac{\text{energy transferred}}{\text{charge}} \qquad V = \dfrac{E}{Q}$$

Voltage is measured in **volts (V)**. One volt is one joule for each coulomb.
:::

:::definition Resistance
The **resistance** of a component is a measure of how much it opposes the flow of charge. It is measured in **ohms (Ω)**.
:::

For the same battery, more resistance means less current. A long wire has more resistance than a short one of the same kind, and a thin wire has more than a thick one.

| To measure | Use | Connect it |
|---|---|---|
| the current through a component | an ammeter | in series, in line with the component |
| the voltage across a component | a voltmeter | in parallel, across the component |

:::example Energy from a battery
A battery transfers 24 J of energy to 4 C of charge. What is the voltage of the battery?
@step $V = \dfrac{E}{Q} = \dfrac{24}{4}$.
@answer $V = 6$ V.
:::

:::mistake Mixing up current and voltage
Current is the charge flowing each second, **through** a component. Voltage is the energy given up by each coulomb, **across** a component. A battery can have a voltage with no current flowing, when the circuit is not complete.
:::

## Ohm's law

For a resistor at a steady temperature, the current through it is proportional to the voltage across it. Double the voltage and the current doubles.

:::definition Ohm's law
$$\text{voltage} = \text{current} \times \text{resistance} \qquad V = IR$$

With current in amperes and resistance in ohms, the voltage is in volts.
:::

$$I = \dfrac{V}{R} \qquad R = \dfrac{V}{I}$$

:::example Finding the voltage
A current of 2 A flows through a resistor of 6 Ω. What is the voltage across it?
@step $V = IR = 2 \times 6$.
@answer $V = 12$ V.
:::

:::example Finding the current
A voltage of 9 V is connected across a resistor of 20 Ω. What current flows?
@step $I = \dfrac{V}{R} = \dfrac{9}{20}$.
@answer $I = 0.45$ A.
:::

:::example Finding the resistance
A voltage of 12 V across a lamp drives a current of 1.5 A through it. What is the resistance of the lamp?
@step $R = \dfrac{V}{I} = \dfrac{12}{1.5}$.
@answer $R = 8$ Ω.
:::

:::example Milliamperes and kilohms
A current of 5 mA flows through a resistor of 2 kΩ. What is the voltage across it?
@step Change the units: 5 mA = 0.005 A, and 2 kΩ = 2000 Ω.
@step $V = IR = 0.005 \times 2000$.
@answer $V = 10$ V.
:::

A graph of current against voltage for a resistor is a straight line through the origin. The resistance is the voltage divided by the current at any point on the line.

:::example Reading a resistance from a graph
What is the resistance of the resistor in the graph?
@step Pick a point on the line: at 12 V the current is 3 A.
@step $R = \dfrac{V}{I} = \dfrac{12}{3}$.
@answer $R = 4$ Ω. Any other point on the line gives the same answer: at 8 V the current is 2 A, and $8 \div 2 = 4$.
:::

:::mistake Dividing the wrong way round
Resistance is voltage ÷ current, not current ÷ voltage. Check with the units: volts divided by amperes gives ohms.
:::

:::summary
- Voltage is the energy transferred by each coulomb of charge: $V = \dfrac{E}{Q}$, in volts.
- Resistance opposes the flow of charge and is measured in ohms.
- An ammeter goes in series. A voltmeter goes in parallel.
- Ohm's law: $V = IR$.
- More voltage gives more current. More resistance gives less.
:::
