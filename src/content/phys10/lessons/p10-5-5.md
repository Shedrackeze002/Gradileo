Every appliance has a power rating printed on it: 9 W on a lamp, 2000 W on a kettle. The rating says how quickly the appliance transfers energy, and with it you can work out what the appliance costs to run.

## Electrical power

:::definition Electrical power
The **power** of an electrical component is the energy it transfers each second. It is the current through the component multiplied by the voltage across it.

$$\text{power} = \text{current} \times \text{voltage} \qquad P = IV$$

With current in amperes and voltage in volts, the power is in watts.
:::

The equation follows from what the two quantities mean. The voltage is the energy given up by each coulomb, and the current is the number of coulombs passing each second, so their product is the energy transferred each second.

$$I = \dfrac{P}{V} \qquad V = \dfrac{P}{I}$$

:::example Finding the power
A car headlamp carries a current of 5 A when the voltage across it is 12 V. What is its power?
@step $P = IV = 5 \times 12$.
@answer $P = 60$ W.
:::

:::example Finding the current
A kettle has a power of 2300 W when the voltage across it is 230 V. What current does it carry?
@step $I = \dfrac{P}{V} = \dfrac{2300}{230}$.
@answer $I = 10$ A.
:::

:::example Power in a resistor
A current of 3 A flows through a resistor of 10 Ω. What power does the resistor transfer as heat?
@step Voltage: $V = IR = 3 \times 10 = 30$ V.
@step Power: $P = IV = 3 \times 30$.
@answer $P = 90$ W.
:::

Putting $V = IR$ into $P = IV$ gives $P = I^2R$, which does the last example in one step: $3^2 \times 10 = 90$ W.

:::mistake Using the supply voltage for one component in series
In $P = IV$, the voltage is the voltage across **that component**. For a lamp in series with other components, that is only its share of the supply voltage.
:::

## Electrical energy and cost

The energy an appliance transfers is its power multiplied by the time it runs.

$$\text{energy} = \text{power} \times \text{time} \qquad E = Pt$$

With power in watts and time in seconds, the energy is in joules. A joule is very small for household use, so electricity is sold by the **kilowatt-hour (kWh)**: the energy transferred by a 1 kW appliance running for 1 hour.

$$\text{energy in kWh} = \text{power in kW} \times \text{time in hours}$$

:::example Energy in joules
A lamp has a power of 60 W. How much energy does it transfer in 120 s?
@step $E = Pt = 60 \times 120$.
@answer $E = 7200$ J.
:::

:::example Energy in kilowatt-hours
A 100 W lamp is on for 20 hours. How much energy does it transfer, in kilowatt-hours?
@step Change watts to kilowatts: 100 W = 0.1 kW.
@step $0.1 \times 20$.
@answer 2 kWh.
:::

:::example The cost of running a heater
Electricity costs \$0.20 for each kilowatt-hour. A heater with a power of 2 kW runs for 5 hours. What does this cost?
@step Energy: $2 \times 5 = 10$ kWh.
@step Cost: 10 × \$0.20.
@answer It costs \$2.00.
:::

The price of a kilowatt-hour differs from place to place and changes over time, so each question states the price to use.

:::mistake Mixing the two sets of units
Use watts with seconds to get joules, or kilowatts with hours to get kilowatt-hours. Multiplying watts by hours gives neither. A 100 W lamp on for 20 hours transfers $0.1 \times 20 = 2$ kWh, not 2000.
:::

:::summary
- Electrical power: $P = IV$, in watts.
- For a resistor, $P = I^2R$.
- Energy = power × time: watts × seconds gives joules.
- Kilowatts × hours gives kilowatt-hours, the unit electricity is sold in.
- Cost = energy in kWh × the price of one kWh.
:::
