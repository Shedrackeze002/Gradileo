When one lamp in a house fails, the others stay on, and each can be switched without touching the rest. That is because household circuits are wired in parallel: every lamp sits on its own branch, connected straight across the supply.

## Current and voltage in parallel

:::definition Parallel circuit
In a **parallel circuit** the components are on separate branches, each connected across the supply.

- The **voltage** across every branch is the same, equal to the supply voltage.
- The **current from the supply** is the sum of the currents in the branches.
:::

At a junction the current splits between the branches, and it joins up again on the other side. No charge is lost, so the branch currents always add up to the current from the supply. The branch with the smaller resistance carries the larger current.

:::example Adding the branch currents
Two lamps are connected in parallel to a battery. The currents in the two branches are 0.5 A and 1.5 A. What current flows from the battery?
@step $0.5 + 1.5$.
@answer 2 A flows from the battery.
:::

:::example The current in each branch
A 12 V supply is connected to resistors of 4 Ω and 6 Ω in parallel. Find the current in each resistor.
@step Each resistor has the full 12 V across it.
@step In the 4 Ω resistor: $I = \dfrac{12}{4} = 3$ A.
@step In the 6 Ω resistor: $I = \dfrac{12}{6} = 2$ A.
@answer 3 A and 2 A.
:::

:::example The current from the supply
What current flows from the supply in the circuit above?
@step Add the branch currents: $3 + 2$.
@answer 5 A flows from the supply.
:::

:::mistake Sharing the voltage between branches
The voltage is shared in a series circuit, not in a parallel one. In parallel, each branch gets the **full** supply voltage. It is the current that is shared.
:::

## Total resistance in parallel

Adding a branch gives the charge an extra path, so more current flows from the same supply. That means the total resistance has gone **down**. The total resistance of resistors in parallel is always less than the smallest of them.

:::definition Two resistors in parallel
$$\dfrac{1}{R} = \dfrac{1}{R_1} + \dfrac{1}{R_2}$$

Add the fractions, then turn the result upside down to get $R$.
:::

:::example Two equal resistors
Two 10 Ω resistors are connected in parallel. What is their total resistance?
@step $\dfrac{1}{R} = \dfrac{1}{10} + \dfrac{1}{10} = \dfrac{2}{10}$.
@step Turn it upside down: $R = \dfrac{10}{2}$.
@answer $R = 5$ Ω, half of one resistor.
:::

:::example Two different resistors
Resistors of 6 Ω and 3 Ω are connected in parallel. What is their total resistance?
@step $\dfrac{1}{R} = \dfrac{1}{6} + \dfrac{1}{3} = \dfrac{1}{6} + \dfrac{2}{6} = \dfrac{3}{6} = \dfrac{1}{2}$.
@step Turn it upside down.
@answer $R = 2$ Ω, less than either resistor.
:::

:::example Series or parallel?
Resistors of 12 Ω and 4 Ω can be connected in series or in parallel. Find the total resistance each way.
@step Series: $12 + 4 = 16$ Ω.
@step Parallel: $\dfrac{1}{R} = \dfrac{1}{12} + \dfrac{1}{4} = \dfrac{1}{12} + \dfrac{3}{12} = \dfrac{4}{12} = \dfrac{1}{3}$, so $R = 3$ Ω.
@answer 16 Ω in series and 3 Ω in parallel.
:::

You can check a parallel answer with the supply. For the 4 Ω and 6 Ω resistors above, $\dfrac{1}{R} = \dfrac{1}{4} + \dfrac{1}{6} = \dfrac{5}{12}$, so $R = 2.4$ Ω, and $12 \div 2.4 = 5$ A, the same current as adding the branches.

:::mistake Forgetting to turn the fraction over
Adding $\dfrac{1}{6} + \dfrac{1}{3}$ gives $\dfrac{1}{2}$. That is $\dfrac{1}{R}$, not $R$. The total resistance is 2 Ω, not 0.5 Ω.
:::

:::summary
- In parallel, every branch has the full supply voltage across it.
- The current from the supply is the sum of the branch currents.
- The smaller resistance carries the larger current.
- Two resistors in parallel: $\dfrac{1}{R} = \dfrac{1}{R_1} + \dfrac{1}{R_2}$.
- Adding a branch lowers the total resistance. If one branch breaks, the others still work.
:::
