Slide a book across a table and it soon stops. For two thousand years people concluded that moving things naturally come to rest unless something keeps pushing them. Galileo and Newton saw that this was backwards: the book stops because a force, friction, stops it. Take the friction away and it would never stop.

## Newton's first law

:::definition Newton's first law of motion
An object stays **at rest**, or keeps moving at a **steady velocity** (the same speed in a straight line), unless a **net force** acts on it.

So balanced forces do not mean "no motion". They mean **no change** in motion.
:::

| Forces on the object | What the object does |
|---|---|
| balanced (net force zero) | stays at rest, or keeps moving at a steady velocity |
| unbalanced (net force not zero) | speeds up, slows down or changes direction |

:::example A parked car and a cruising car
A car is parked. Later it travels along a straight road at a steady 25 m/s. What is the net force on it each time?
@step Parked: its velocity is not changing (it stays at 0 m/s), so the forces are balanced.
@step Cruising: its velocity is not changing either, so the forces are balanced again. The engine's driving force only cancels friction and air resistance.
@answer Zero both times.
:::

:::example Finding a force from a steady velocity
A crate is pushed across a level floor at a steady velocity with a push of 85 N. How large is the friction?
@step A steady velocity means the net force is zero.
@step So the friction must cancel the push exactly.
@answer 85 N.
:::

:::mistake Thinking a force is needed to keep something moving
A spacecraft far from any planet can switch its engines off and carry on for ever at the same velocity. On Earth things slow down only because friction and air resistance are hard to remove.

## Inertia

:::definition Inertia
**Inertia** is the tendency of an object to keep doing what it is doing: to stay at rest if it is at rest, and to keep its velocity if it is moving.

Inertia is not a force. It is a property of **mass**: the more mass an object has, the more inertia it has, and the harder it is to start, stop or turn.
:::

:::example Braking in a bus
A bus brakes suddenly and the standing passengers lurch forwards. Explain why.
@step Before braking, the passengers and the bus share the same forward velocity.
@step The brakes slow the bus, but no force has yet slowed the passengers.
@answer By inertia the passengers carry on at their old velocity, so they move forwards compared with the slowing bus.
:::

:::example Which is harder to stop?
A football (0.45 kg) and a bowling ball (6 kg) roll at the same speed. Which is harder to stop?
@step Inertia depends on mass.
@answer The bowling ball: it has more mass, so more inertia.
:::

This is why a seat belt matters. In a sudden stop the car slows, and the belt supplies the force that slows the person with it. Without a belt, the person would carry on at the car's old velocity.

:::mistake Treating inertia as a force
Nothing pushes the bus passengers forwards. They simply keep moving while the bus slows down beneath them. Inertia is the absence of a change, not a push.

:::summary
- Newton's first law: with no net force, an object stays at rest or keeps a steady velocity.
- A steady velocity means balanced forces, so one force can be found from the one that cancels it.
- Inertia is the tendency to resist a change in motion. More mass means more inertia.
- Inertia is a property of mass, not a force.
:::
