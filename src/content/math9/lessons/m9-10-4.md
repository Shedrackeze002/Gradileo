Weather forecasts, sports statistics, medicine labels and contest rules all use probability. Understanding where a probability comes from, and what it really means, helps you make better decisions every day.

## Three kinds of probability

:::definition Theoretical, experimental and subjective probability
- **Theoretical probability** comes from counting equally likely outcomes, without doing the experiment. Example: $P(\text{rolling a } 6) = \dfrac{1}{6}$.
- **Experimental probability** comes from data: the results of trials or records of what happened in the past.
- **Subjective probability** comes from a person’s opinion, feelings or judgement, not from counting or from data.
:::

:::example Which kind?
Classify each statement. (a) “A spinner has $8$ equal sections and $3$ are blue, so $P(\text{blue}) = \dfrac{3}{8}$.” (b) “In the past $200$ school days, the bus was late $30$ times, so the probability it is late tomorrow is about $15\%$.” (c) “A coach thinks her team has about a $70\%$ chance of winning the final.”
@step (a) It counts equal sections: **theoretical**.
@step (b) It uses records of what happened: **experimental**.
@step (c) It is the coach’s opinion: **subjective**.
@answer Theoretical, experimental, subjective
:::

## What probability statements mean

A probability describes what happens over many similar situations. It never guarantees what will happen next time.

:::example A forecast
The forecast for Brookfield says there is a $70\%$ chance of rain tomorrow. What does that mean?
@step On many days with weather like this, rain falls on about $7$ of every $10$ days.
@step Rain is likely, but it is not certain.
@answer Rain is likely but not certain. It does **not** mean it will rain for $70\%$ of the day.
:::

:::example “1 in 5”
A cereal company says “$1$ in every $5$ boxes contains a prize.” If you buy $5$ boxes, are you sure to win?
@step “$1$ in $5$” means $P(\text{prize}) = \dfrac{1}{5}$ over a very large number of boxes.
@step The prize boxes are mixed in at random, so any $5$ boxes could have no prize, or more than one.
@answer No. About $\dfrac{1}{5}$ of all boxes contain a prize, but buying $5$ does not guarantee one.
:::

:::mistake Probability is not a promise
A $90\%$ chance is not a guarantee, and a $10\%$ chance can still happen. Words like “definitely”, “exactly” and “sure to” are signs that a statement claims too much.
:::

## Predicting with probability

:::definition Expected number
$$\text{expected number} = \text{probability} \times \text{number of trials}$$
The actual number is usually close to the expected number, but not exactly equal.
:::

:::example Planting seeds
The probability that a carrot seed sprouts is $0.88$. Leah plants $250$ seeds. About how many should sprout?
@step $0.88 \times 250 = 220$
@answer About $220$ seeds should sprout.
:::

## Making decisions

People use probability to choose between options. Compare probabilities, not raw counts, and think about expected costs.

:::example Which brand?
Last year, $180$ of $200$ Brand A seeds sprouted, and $255$ of $300$ Brand B seeds sprouted. Which brand should a farmer buy?
@step Brand A: $180 \div 200 = 0.9$, or $90\%$
@step Brand B: $255 \div 300 = 0.85$, or $85\%$
@answer Brand A. Brand B had more seeds sprout, but only because more were planted. Brand A has the higher probability of sprouting.
:::

:::example Is the warranty worth it?
A one-year warranty on a phone costs \$49. A repair costs \$240, and suppose the probability of needing a repair in the first year is $15\%$.
@step Expected repair cost: $0.15 \times 240 = 36$, so \$36.00 per phone on average.
@step The warranty costs \$49, which is more than \$36.
@answer On average, paying for repairs only when needed costs less. Someone who could not afford a surprise repair bill might still choose the warranty.
:::

:::summary
- Theoretical probability: from equally likely outcomes. Experimental: from data. Subjective: from opinion.
- A probability describes what happens over many situations. It is never a promise about one event.
- Expected number $=$ probability $\times$ number of trials.
- Compare probabilities (percents), not raw counts, when making decisions.
- Expected cost $=$ probability $\times$ cost helps you judge warranties, insurance and games.
:::
