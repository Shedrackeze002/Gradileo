How much do students at your school really sleep? How do most of them get to school? A data project lets you answer a question like this with evidence. A good plan makes the difference between a useful answer and a pile of confusing numbers.

## The steps of a project plan

:::definition Project plan
A **project plan** lists the steps for answering a question with data:
1. Write a clear question to investigate.
2. Choose a data-collection method, thinking about bias, privacy and other social issues.
3. Decide whether to use a population or a sample, and choose who will take part.
4. Collect the data.
5. Organize the data and display it in a suitable graph or table.
6. Analyze the data and draw conclusions that answer the question.
:::

Each step depends on the one before. You cannot graph data you have not collected, and you cannot collect data until you know what you are asking.

## Writing a good question

:::definition Good project question
A **good project question** is clear and specific. It names the population, says exactly what will be measured or counted, uses neutral words and respects people’s privacy.
:::

:::example Choosing a question
Which question is best for a project about screen time? (A) “Do teens use screens too much?” (B) “Don’t you agree that phones are ruining teenagers’ lives?” (C) “On a typical school day, how many hours do Grade 9 students at our school spend on screens outside of class?”
@step (A) is vague: who are the teens, and what is “too much”?
@step (B) is a leading question.
@step (C) names the population (Grade 9 students at our school) and something you can measure (hours per day).
@answer Question (C)
:::

:::mistake Questions you cannot answer
“Which sport does every person in the world like best?” sounds clear, but you could never collect the data. Choose a population you can actually reach.
:::

## Collecting the data

Choose a method that fits the question:

- **Survey or questionnaire:** for people’s opinions, habits or choices.
- **Experiment:** to test whether changing one thing changes another.
- **Observation:** to watch and count something as it happens.
- **Existing data from records or a database:** for facts about the past.

Then think about the social issues from Lesson 10.1 (bias, wording, ethics, cost, timing, privacy and cultural sensitivity), and choose a population or a representative sample.

:::example Choosing a method
Which method fits each question? (a) “Does the height a ball is dropped from change how high it bounces?” (b) “How many vehicles pass the school crosswalk between 8:00 and 8:30 a.m.?” (c) “How has the population of our country changed since 1950?”
@step (a) Change the drop height and measure the bounce: an **experiment**.
@step (b) Stand at the crosswalk and count: **observation**.
@step (c) The data is about the past: **existing records**.
@answer Experiment, observation, existing records
:::

## Displaying data and drawing conclusions

Choose a display that suits the data: a bar graph to compare categories, a line graph for change over time, a circle graph for parts of a whole. Then use the data to answer your question, and be careful not to claim more than the data shows.

:::example From a sample to a conclusion
Talia surveyed a random sample of $40$ of the $600$ students at her school about how they travel to school. Predict how many students at the school take the bus, and state a fair conclusion.
@step Bus riders in the sample: $14$ of $40$, which is $\dfrac{14}{40} = 35\%$.
@step Prediction for the school: $0.35 \times 600 = 210$ students.
@step The sample represents only this school, and a sample gives an estimate, not an exact count.
@answer About $210$ students (about $35\%$) at Talia’s school probably take the bus. The true number may be a little different.
:::

:::mistake Claiming too much
Talia’s data does not tell you about all students in the country, and it does not prove that *exactly* $210$ students take the bus. A conclusion should match the population that was sampled.
:::

:::summary
- A project plan: question, method, population or sample, collect, display, conclude.
- A good question is clear, specific, measurable, neutral and respectful of privacy.
- Methods: survey, experiment, observation, or existing records.
- Choose a display that suits the data, and draw conclusions only about the population you sampled.
- A sample gives an estimate: use “about” and “probably”.
:::
