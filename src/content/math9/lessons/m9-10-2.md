To find out what all Canadian teens think, you cannot ask every one of them. Instead, you ask a smaller group and use their answers to learn about everyone. The trick is choosing that smaller group well.

## Population or sample?

:::definition Population, census and sample
The **population** is the whole group you want to learn about. A **census** collects data from every member of the population. A **sample** is a part of the population that you collect data from.
:::

Use the whole population when it is small and easy to reach, or when you need information about every single member. Use a sample when the population is too large to reach in a reasonable time and at a reasonable cost, or when testing an item destroys it.

:::example Census or sample?
Decide for each: (a) the shoe sizes of the $12$ players on the school basketball team, to order team shoes; (b) how long the light bulbs made in a factory last.
@step (a) The team is small, and every player needs the right size. Use the **population** (a census).
@step (b) Testing a bulb until it burns out destroys it. If every bulb were tested, none would be left to sell. Use a **sample**.
@answer (a) Population. (b) Sample.
:::

## Sampling methods

:::definition Six ways to choose a sample
- **Simple random sample:** every member has the same chance of being chosen, like drawing names from a hat.
- **Systematic sample:** choose from a list or a line at a regular interval, such as every $10$th person.
- **Stratified sample:** split the population into groups (such as grades) and choose at random from **each** group, in proportion to its size.
- **Cluster sample:** split the population into groups (such as homerooms), choose some whole groups at random, and survey **everyone** in them.
- **Convenience sample:** choose the people who are easiest to reach.
- **Voluntary response sample:** people choose themselves by deciding to answer, as in an online poll.
:::

:::example Naming the method
A grocery store in a small town surveys every $20$th customer who walks through the door. Which method is this?
@step Customers are chosen at a regular interval: the $20$th, $40$th, $60$th, and so on.
@answer A **systematic sample**
:::

:::tip Stratified or cluster?
Stratified: **some** people from **every** group. Cluster: **all** the people from **some** groups.
:::

## Representative samples

:::definition Representative sample
A **random sample** gives every member of the population a fair chance to be chosen. A **representative sample** has the same mix of people as the population, like a small copy of it. Random sampling is the best way to get one.
:::

:::example Choosing a plan
Liam wants to know what all the students at a large city high school think of the new cafeteria menu. Which plan is best: (A) ask $80$ students in line at the cafeteria, (B) put a poll on the school website, or (C) choose $80$ students at random from the list of all students?
@step (A) is a convenience sample. Students in line already eat in the cafeteria.
@step (B) is a voluntary response sample. Students with strong opinions are more likely to answer.
@step (C) gives every student a fair chance.
@answer Plan (C) gives a representative sample.
:::

:::mistake “More is better”
A huge sample is not automatically representative. $1000$ answers to an online poll can still be biased, because the people chose themselves. How the sample is chosen matters more than how big it is.
:::

## Stratified samples in proportion

In a stratified sample, each group gets the same fraction of its members chosen.

:::example How many from each grade?
A junior high has $240$ Grade 7, $200$ Grade 8 and $160$ Grade 9 students. The principal wants a stratified sample of $60$ students. How many should come from each grade?
@step Total: $240 + 200 + 160 = 600$ students.
@step The sample is $\dfrac{60}{600} = \dfrac{1}{10}$ of the school.
@step Grade 7: $240 \times \dfrac{1}{10} = 24$. Grade 8: $200 \times \dfrac{1}{10} = 20$. Grade 9: $160 \times \dfrac{1}{10} = 16$.
@answer $24$ Grade 7, $20$ Grade 8 and $16$ Grade 9 students (check: $24 + 20 + 16 = 60$)
:::

:::check m9-population-sample 3
:::

:::summary
- Population: the whole group. Census: data from everyone. Sample: data from part of the group.
- Use a census for a small group or when every member matters; use a sample for a huge group or when testing destroys items.
- Sampling methods: simple random, systematic, stratified, cluster, convenience and voluntary response.
- Random methods give representative samples. Convenience and voluntary response samples are usually biased.
- In a stratified sample, take the same fraction from every group.
:::
