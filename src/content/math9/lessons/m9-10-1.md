Surveys and studies shape decisions about schools, parks and products. But data is only as good as the way it was collected. A survey can give misleading results long before anyone draws a graph. In this lesson you will learn seven factors that can affect data collection.

## Bias and the use of language

:::definition Bias
**Bias** happens when the way data is collected favours some answers or some people over others. Biased data does not represent the whole group.
:::

:::example A biased sample
To find out whether people in a city want a new hockey arena, a group surveys only fans leaving a hockey game. What is the problem?
@step Hockey fans are much more likely than other people to want an arena.
@step The results will favour “yes”, so they do not show what the whole city thinks.
@answer The survey is **biased**. Surveying people chosen at random from all over the city would fix it.
:::

:::definition Leading question
A **leading question** uses its wording to push people toward an answer. Loaded words (“greasy”, “waste”), opinions (“Don’t you agree…?”) and claims about what others think (“Most students want…”) all lead people.
:::

:::example Rewriting a leading question
Rewrite this question so it is neutral: “Should the town waste money on a new skatepark?”
@step The word “waste” tells people the skatepark is a bad idea.
@step Remove it and give fair choices.
@answer “Should the town build a new skatepark? (Yes / No / Not sure)”
:::

:::tip Plain words
The **use of language** also means using words people understand. A question full of hard words gets careless answers.
:::

## Ethics, privacy and cultural sensitivity

:::definition Ethics, privacy and cultural sensitivity
- **Ethics:** collecting data honestly and fairly, without harming or pressuring anyone, and using it only in the way people were told.
- **Privacy:** protecting personal information. People should not have to share more than is needed, and their answers should be kept private.
- **Cultural sensitivity:** respecting people’s cultures, beliefs and traditions in the questions and choices.
:::

:::example Three problems
Which factor does each plan raise? (a) A survey asks students for their name, home address and family income. (b) A student changes some answers so the results support her prediction. (c) A survey asks every student what they will get for Christmas.
@step (a) Address and income are personal information: **privacy**.
@step (b) Changing data is dishonest: **ethics**.
@step (c) Not every student celebrates Christmas: **cultural sensitivity**.
@answer Privacy, ethics, cultural sensitivity
:::

:::mistake Names on a sensitive survey
A survey about cheating or health that asks for names is a privacy problem, and it also gives poor data, because people will not answer honestly. Make sensitive surveys anonymous.
:::

## Cost, time and timing

:::definition Cost, time and timing
- **Cost:** some plans cost far more money than the project can pay.
- **Time:** some plans take far too long to finish.
- **Timing:** *when* the data is collected can change the answers.
:::

:::example Bad timing
Right after a news story about a school bus accident, a group surveys parents about whether school buses are safe. What is the problem?
@step The news is fresh in parents’ minds, so many may answer differently than they would on an ordinary day.
@answer This is a **timing** problem. Surveying at a calmer time, or at several different times, would give fairer results.
:::

:::example Too costly
A class wants to know what every adult in the country thinks about daylight saving time, so it plans to mail a survey with a stamped return envelope to every home in the country.
@step Printing and postage for every home in the country would cost far more than a class can pay.
@answer This is a **cost** problem. A random sample of adults would cost much less.
:::

## Fixing a plan

To improve a data-collection plan, first name the problem, then choose a change that removes that exact problem. Adding more questions or making a fancier graph does not fix how the data was collected.

:::example Identify and fix
A gym wants to know if people in its city exercise enough, so it surveys its own members.
@step Gym members exercise more than most people, so the results are **biased**.
@step The fix must change *who* is asked.
@answer Survey residents chosen at random from all over the city.
:::

:::summary
- Seven factors affect data collection: bias, use of language, ethics, cost, time and timing, privacy, and cultural sensitivity.
- Bias: the method favours some answers or people. A leading question is one cause of bias.
- Ethics: be honest and fair, and never harm or pressure people. Privacy: protect personal information.
- Cultural sensitivity: respect everyone’s cultures, beliefs and traditions.
- Cost, time and timing: a plan must be affordable, finish in time, and happen at a fair time.
- To fix a plan, change the part that causes the problem.
:::
