In most countries, the weather forecast uses degrees Celsius. Many ovens, recipes and thermometers from the United States use degrees Fahrenheit. Cooks, servers and anyone who stores food at work need to read both.

## Two temperature scales

:::definition Celsius
The **Celsius** scale ($^\circ\text{C}$) is used in Canada. Water freezes at $0^\circ\text{C}$ and boils at $100^\circ\text{C}$.
:::

:::definition Fahrenheit
The **Fahrenheit** scale ($^\circ\text{F}$) is used in the United States. Water freezes at $32^\circ\text{F}$ and boils at $212^\circ\text{F}$.
:::

:::example Which temperature makes sense?
Which temperature makes sense for a hot summer afternoon: $32^\circ\text{C}$, $32^\circ\text{F}$ or $90^\circ\text{C}$?
@step $32^\circ\text{F}$ is the temperature where water freezes. That is far too cold.
@step $90^\circ\text{C}$ is almost hot enough to boil water. That is far too hot.
@answer $32^\circ\text{C}$ makes sense. It is hotter than a room but cooler than your body.
:::

:::mistake Mixing up the scales
$30^\circ\text{F}$ is below freezing, but $30^\circ\text{C}$ is a hot summer day. Always look at the letter after the degree sign.
:::

## Celsius to Fahrenheit

To change Celsius to Fahrenheit, use this formula:
$$F = 1.8C + 32$$
**Multiply** by $1.8$ first. **Then add** $32$.

:::example Weather for a cousin in Texas
The forecast for tomorrow is $25^\circ\text{C}$. What is this in degrees Fahrenheit?
@step Multiply: $1.8 \times 25 = 45$
@step Add 32: $45 + 32 = 77$
@answer It will be $77^\circ\text{F}$.
:::

:::example A cold day
It is $-10^\circ\text{C}$ on a winter day. What is this in degrees Fahrenheit?
@step Multiply: $1.8 \times (-10) = -18$
@step Add 32: $-18 + 32 = 14$
@answer It is $14^\circ\text{F}$.
:::

:::mistake Adding first
$(25 + 32) \times 1.8 = 102.6$ is wrong. A warm day of $25^\circ\text{C}$ is not $102.6^\circ\text{F}$. Multiply by $1.8$ first, then add $32$.
:::

## Fahrenheit to Celsius

To change Fahrenheit to Celsius, undo the steps in reverse order:
$$C = (F - 32) \div 1.8$$
**Subtract** $32$ first. **Then divide** by $1.8$.

:::example A US recipe
A US recipe says to bake at $350^\circ\text{F}$. The oven shows degrees Celsius. What temperature should you use, to the nearest degree?
@step Subtract 32: $350 - 32 = 318$
@step Divide by 1.8: $318 \div 1.8 \approx 176.67$
@answer Set the oven to about $177^\circ\text{C}$.
:::

:::example Is the food hot enough?
Hot food on a buffet must be kept at $60^\circ\text{C}$ or hotter. A US thermometer reads $135^\circ\text{F}$. Is the food hot enough?
@step Subtract 32: $135 - 32 = 103$
@step Divide by 1.8: $103 \div 1.8 \approx 57.2$, about $57^\circ\text{C}$
@answer $57^\circ\text{C}$ is colder than $60^\circ\text{C}$, so the food is **not** hot enough.
:::

:::mistake Dividing first
$350 \div 1.8 - 32 \approx 162$ is wrong. The brackets in $(F - 32)$ tell you to subtract $32$ first.
:::

:::tip A quick check
To estimate Fahrenheit, double the Celsius temperature and add $30$. For $25^\circ\text{C}$: $2 \times 25 + 30 = 80$, close to the exact $77^\circ\text{F}$.
:::

:::summary
- Water freezes at $0^\circ\text{C}$ ($32^\circ\text{F}$) and boils at $100^\circ\text{C}$ ($212^\circ\text{F}$).
- Celsius to Fahrenheit: $F = 1.8C + 32$. Multiply first, then add.
- Fahrenheit to Celsius: $C = (F - 32) \div 1.8$. Subtract first, then divide.
- Use temperatures you know to check that an answer makes sense.
:::
