A coffee shop has to guess how many large cups to order. An insurance company has to guess how many claims it will get. Both use **probability** to predict what will happen.

## Theoretical probability

:::definition Theoretical probability
**Theoretical probability** is what *should* happen, worked out by thinking about the equally likely outcomes.
$$P(\text{event}) = \dfrac{\text{favourable outcomes}}{\text{total outcomes}}$$
:::

:::figure

:::

:::example A spinner
The spinner has $4$ equal sections numbered $1$ to $4$. What is the theoretical probability of spinning a $3$ or a $4$?
@step Favourable outcomes: $3$ and $4$, so $2$.
@step Total outcomes: $4$.
@answer $P(3 \text{ or } 4) = \dfrac{2}{4} = \dfrac{1}{2}$, or $50\%$.
:::

## Experimental probability

:::definition Experimental probability
**Experimental probability** is what *did* happen in a test or in real records. Each test is called a **trial**.
$$P(\text{event}) = \dfrac{\text{number of times it happened}}{\text{number of trials}}$$
:::

:::figure
{"type":"table","headers":["Size","Count"],"rows":[["Small","46"],["Medium","84"],["Large","52"],["Extra large","18"]]}
:::

:::example Drink sizes
A coffee shop recorded the drink size for its last $200$ customers. What is the experimental probability that a customer orders a large?
@step Trials: $46 + 84 + 52 + 18 = 200$
@step $P(\text{large}) = \dfrac{52}{200} = 0.26$
@answer The experimental probability is $26\%$.
:::

:::tip More trials, better results
With only a few trials, results can be far from the theory. With many trials, the experimental probability usually gets close to the theoretical probability.
:::

## Making predictions

:::definition Prediction
A **prediction** is a careful guess about what will happen, based on probability. To predict how many times something will happen, multiply the probability by the number of trials.
:::

$$\text{expected number} = P(\text{event}) \times \text{number of trials}$$

:::example Rolling a die
A regular die is rolled $120$ times. About how many times should a $5$ come up?
@step $P(5) = \dfrac{1}{6}$
@step $\dfrac{1}{6} \times 120 = 20$
@answer Expect about $20$ fives.
:::

:::example Ordering cups
The coffee shop above expects $500$ customers on Saturday. About how many will order a large?
@step Use the experimental probability: $26\% = 0.26$
@step $0.26 \times 500 = 130$
@answer Plan for about $130$ large drinks.
:::

:::mistake A prediction is not a promise
"About $20$ fives" does not mean exactly $20$. Real results go up and down a little. That is why we say **about**.
:::

## Comparing predictions with results

:::figure

:::

:::example Real rolls
Kai rolled a die $120$ times. The table shows the results. Compare the number of 4s with the prediction.
@step Predicted: $\dfrac{1}{6} \times 120 = 20$
@step Real: $24$ fours, so $24 - 20 = 4$ more than predicted.
@step Experimental: $\dfrac{24}{120} = 20\%$. Theoretical: $\dfrac{1}{6} \approx 16.7\%$.
@answer The real result was $4$ more than predicted. That is normal for $120$ rolls.
:::

:::check m204-probability 3
:::

:::summary
- Theoretical probability: favourable outcomes over total outcomes. It is what should happen.
- Experimental probability: times it happened over number of trials. It is what did happen.
- Expected number $=$ probability $\times$ number of trials.
- Real results are usually close to the prediction, but not exactly the same. More trials get closer.
:::
