Ads, news stories and bosses often use numbers to make a point. Some numbers tell the whole story. Others leave things out. In this lesson you will compare data sets and learn to spot a graph or a claim that misleads.

## Comparing two data sets

:::definition Measures of data
The **mean** is the total divided by the number of values. The **median** is the middle value when the data is in order. The **mode** is the most common value. The **range** (highest minus lowest) shows how spread out the data is.
:::

:::figure
{"type":"table","headers":["Shift","Ava","Ben"],"rows":[["1","$45","$30"],["2","$60","$85"],["3","$52","$40"],["4","$48","$70"],["5","$55","$35"]]}
:::

:::example Two servers' tips
Ava and Ben are servers at a downtown restaurant. Compare their tips for five shifts.
@step Means: Ava $260 \div 5 = 52$. Ben $260 \div 5 = 52$.
@step Medians: Ava $45,\ 48,\ 52,\ 55,\ 60$ gives $52$. Ben $30,\ 35,\ 40,\ 70,\ 85$ gives $40$.
@step Ranges: Ava $60 - 45 = 15$. Ben $85 - 30 = 55$.
@answer They have the same mean, \$52. But Ava's tips are steady (small range), while Ben's go up and down a lot. On a typical shift, Ben earns less (median \$40).
:::

:::tip Look at more than one measure
Two data sets can have the same mean and still be very different. Check the median and the range too.
:::

## Outliers and the best measure

:::definition Outlier
An **outlier** is a value much higher or much lower than the rest of the data. One outlier can pull the mean a long way.
:::

:::example A delivery in a snowstorm
A driver's delivery times, in minutes, were $22$, $25$, $24$, $28$, $26$, $23$ and $80$. The $80$ was during a snowstorm. Find the mean with and without the outlier, and the median.
@step With: $22 + 25 + 24 + 28 + 26 + 23 + 80 = 228$, and $228 \div 7 \approx 32.6$
@step Without: $228 - 80 = 148$, and $148 \div 6 \approx 24.7$
@step Median: in order $22,\ 23,\ 24,\ 25,\ 26,\ 28,\ 80$, so the median is $25$.
@answer The outlier raises the mean by about $8$ minutes. The median, $25$ minutes, describes a typical delivery better.
:::

| Use the... | when... |
|---|---|
| mean | the values are close together, with no outliers |
| median | there is an outlier |
| mode | you want the most common value, like the boot size to order most |

## Misleading graphs

:::definition Misleading
A graph or claim is **misleading** when it gives the wrong idea, even if its numbers are true.
:::

:::figure
{"type":"plane","grid":{"xMin":2020.4,"xMax":2024.6,"yMin":90,"yMax":110,"xStep":1,"yStep":2,"xLabel":"Year","yLabel":"Cars sold"},"polylines":[{"points":[[2020.7,90],[2020.7,92],[2021.3,92],[2021.3,90]]},{"points":[[2021.7,90],[2021.7,96],[2022.3,96],[2022.3,90]]},{"points":[[2022.7,90],[2022.7,100],[2023.3,100],[2023.3,90]]},{"points":[[2023.7,90],[2023.7,106],[2024.3,106],[2024.3,90]]}],"caption":"Cars sold, with the axis starting at 90"}
:::

A car dealership put this graph in an ad. The 2024 bar looks $8$ times as tall as the 2021 bar. But look at the scale: it starts at $90$, not $0$.

:::example The real change
How much did sales really grow from 2021 to 2024?
@step 2021: $92$ cars. 2024: $106$ cars.
@step Increase: $106 - 92 = 14$ cars
@step Percent increase: $14 \div 92 \times 100 \approx 15\%$
@answer Sales grew by about $15\%$, not $8$ times.
:::

:::figure
{"type":"barChart","categories":["2021","2022","2023","2024"],"values":[92,96,100,106],"valueLabel":"Cars sold","categoryLabel":"Year","max":120,"step":20,"showValues":true}
:::

The same data with the axis starting at $0$ shows the honest picture. Other graph tricks:
- **Unequal intervals:** years like 2000, 2010, 2020, 2022 spaced the same width apart.
- **Picture sizes:** a picture twice as tall **and** twice as wide looks $4$ times as big.

## Misleading claims

Claims can mislead too. Ask: who was asked, how many, and what was left out?
- **Small sample:** "4 out of 5 people prefer our drink!" when only $5$ people were asked.
- **Biased sample:** asking only people at a hockey game if the town needs a new arena.
- **Cherry-picking:** showing only the months when sales went up.
- **Misleading average:** nine workers earn \$18 an hour and the owner earns \$220. The mean is \$38.20, but the median is \$18.

:::mistake Trusting the word "average"
"Average" usually means the mean. If there is an outlier, ask for the median too.
:::

:::summary
- Compare data sets with the mean, the median and the range, not just one number.
- An outlier pulls the mean toward it. Use the median when there is an outlier.
- Check that a graph's axis starts at $0$ and that the time gaps are equal.
- Be careful with small or biased samples, cherry-picked data and "averages" with outliers.
:::
