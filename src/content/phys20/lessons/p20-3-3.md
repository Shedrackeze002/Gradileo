Newton imagined a cannon on a very high mountain, firing a ball horizontally. A slow ball curves down and lands near the foot of the mountain. A faster ball lands farther away. Fire it fast enough and the ground curves away beneath the ball as fast as the ball falls, so it never lands at all. The ball is in orbit: it is falling all the way round the Earth.

## Orbital speed

A satellite in a circular orbit is in uniform circular motion, and the only force on it is gravity. Gravity is therefore the centripetal force. For a satellite of mass $m$ at a distance $r$ from the centre of a planet of mass $M$:

$$\dfrac{GMm}{r^2} = \dfrac{mv^2}{r}$$

The mass of the satellite cancels. Solving for the speed:

:::definition Orbital speed
The speed of a satellite in a circular orbit of radius $r$ round a planet of mass $M$ is

$$v = \sqrt{\dfrac{GM}{r}}$$

$r$ is measured from the **centre** of the planet, and $G = 6.67 \times 10^{-11}\ \text{N·m}^2/\text{kg}^2$.
:::

Two things follow at once. The speed does not depend on the mass of the satellite, so a spacecraft and a loose bolt beside it orbit together. And each orbit has exactly one speed: a larger orbit needs a **smaller** speed.

:::example The speed of a satellite
A satellite is in a circular orbit round a planet of mass $6.0 \times 10^{24}$ kg, with its centre $7.0 \times 10^{6}$ m from the centre of the planet. What is its speed?
@step $v = \sqrt{\dfrac{GM}{r}} = \sqrt{\dfrac{6.67 \times 10^{-11} \times 6.0 \times 10^{24}}{7.0 \times 10^{6}}}$.
@step Inside the root: $\dfrac{4.002 \times 10^{14}}{7.0 \times 10^{6}} = 5.717 \times 10^{7}$.
@answer $v = 7.56 \times 10^{3}$ m/s, about seven and a half kilometres every second.
:::

:::example An orbit at a height above the surface
A planet has a mass of $6.4 \times 10^{23}$ kg and a radius of $3.4 \times 10^{6}$ m. A satellite is in a circular orbit at a height of $6.0 \times 10^{5}$ m above the surface. What is its speed?
@step Radius of the orbit: $r = 3.4 \times 10^{6} + 0.6 \times 10^{6} = 4.0 \times 10^{6}$ m.
@step $v = \sqrt{\dfrac{6.67 \times 10^{-11} \times 6.4 \times 10^{23}}{4.0 \times 10^{6}}} = \sqrt{1.0672 \times 10^{7}}$.
@answer $v = 3.27 \times 10^{3}$ m/s.
:::

Astronauts in an orbiting spacecraft float about inside it. This is not because gravity has gone: at the height of a low orbit it is still almost as strong as on the ground. They float because they and the spacecraft are falling round the Earth together, so nothing presses them against the floor.

:::mistake Using the height as the radius of the orbit
The $r$ in $v = \sqrt{\dfrac{GM}{r}}$ is the distance from the centre of the planet. For a satellite at a height above the surface, add the radius of the planet to the height first.
:::

## Orbital period

The period of a satellite is the time for one orbit. As for any circular motion, it is the circumference divided by the speed:

$$T = \dfrac{2\pi r}{v}$$

:::example The period of a satellite
The satellite of the first example orbits at $7.56 \times 10^{3}$ m/s in an orbit of radius $7.0 \times 10^{6}$ m. What is its period?
@step $T = \dfrac{2\pi r}{v} = \dfrac{2\pi \times 7.0 \times 10^{6}}{7.56 \times 10^{3}} = \dfrac{4.398 \times 10^{7}}{7.56 \times 10^{3}}$.
@answer $T = 5.82 \times 10^{3}$ s, which is about 97 minutes.
:::

When only the mass of the planet and the radius of the orbit are given, find the speed first and then the period.

:::example The period of a moon
A moon is in a circular orbit round a planet of mass $6.0 \times 10^{24}$ kg, with its centre $4.0 \times 10^{7}$ m from the centre of the planet. What is the period of the moon?
@step Speed: $v = \sqrt{\dfrac{GM}{r}} = \sqrt{\dfrac{6.67 \times 10^{-11} \times 6.0 \times 10^{24}}{4.0 \times 10^{7}}} = 3.163 \times 10^{3}$ m/s.
@step Period: $T = \dfrac{2\pi r}{v} = \dfrac{2\pi \times 4.0 \times 10^{7}}{3.163 \times 10^{3}}$.
@answer $T = 7.95 \times 10^{4}$ s, which is about 22 hours.
:::

A satellite far from its planet has farther to go and moves more slowly, so its period is much longer. Putting $v = \sqrt{\dfrac{GM}{r}}$ into $T = \dfrac{2\pi r}{v}$ and squaring gives the exact rule:

$$T^2 = \dfrac{4\pi^2}{GM}\,r^3$$

:::definition Kepler's third law
For all the satellites of the same planet (or all the planets of the same star), the square of the period is proportional to the cube of the radius of the orbit:

$$T^2 \propto r^3$$

Kepler found this rule from measurements of the planets, about seventy years before Newton explained it with gravity.
:::

The law lets you compare two orbits without any constants. If the radius is multiplied by a factor $k$, then $T^2$ is multiplied by $k^3$, so $T$ is multiplied by $\sqrt{k^3}$.

:::example Comparing two moons
Two moons are in circular orbits round the same planet. The inner moon has a period of 6 days. The radius of the outer moon's orbit is 4 times the radius of the inner moon's orbit. What is the period of the outer moon?
@step $T^2 \propto r^3$, so multiplying $r$ by 4 multiplies $T^2$ by $4^3 = 64$.
@step $T$ is multiplied by $\sqrt{64} = 8$.
@answer $6 \times 8 = 48$ days.
:::

:::example A factor that is not a perfect square
Another moon of the same planet has an orbit of twice the radius of the inner moon's orbit. What is its period?
@step Multiplying $r$ by 2 multiplies $T^2$ by $2^3 = 8$, so $T$ is multiplied by $\sqrt{8} = 2.828$.
@answer $6 \times 2.828 = 17.0$ days.
:::

:::mistake Doubling the period when the radius doubles
The period is not proportional to the radius. Twice the radius gives $\sqrt{2^3} = 2.83$ times the period, and four times the radius gives 8 times the period. Cube the factor, then take the square root.
:::

:::summary
- In a circular orbit, gravity is the centripetal force: $\dfrac{GMm}{r^2} = \dfrac{mv^2}{r}$.
- Orbital speed: $v = \sqrt{\dfrac{GM}{r}}$, with $r$ measured from the centre of the planet. It does not depend on the mass of the satellite.
- A larger orbit has a smaller speed and a longer period.
- Period: $T = \dfrac{2\pi r}{v}$.
- Kepler's third law: for orbits round the same planet, $T^2 \propto r^3$.
:::
