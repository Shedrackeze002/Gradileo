How long must a runway be for a plane to reach take-off speed? How far does a car travel after the driver presses the brake? Questions like these can be answered without drawing a graph, with four equations that link the five quantities of uniformly accelerated motion.

## The five quantities

Every problem about motion with a constant acceleration uses the same five quantities.

| Symbol | Quantity | Unit |
|---|---|---|
| $d$ | displacement | m |
| $u$ | initial velocity | m/s |
| $v$ | final velocity | m/s |
| $a$ | acceleration | m/s² |
| $t$ | time | s |

:::definition The equations of motion
For motion in a straight line with a **constant acceleration**:

$$v = u + at$$

$$d = \tfrac12(u + v)t$$

$$d = ut + \tfrac12at^2$$

$$v^2 = u^2 + 2ad$$

Each equation links four of the five quantities and leaves one out. If you know three quantities, you can find a fourth.
:::

The equations come from the velocity-time graph. The first is the definition of acceleration, rearranged. The second says that the displacement is the average velocity times the time, which is the area under the graph. The other two come from putting the first two together.

To choose an equation, list the three quantities you know and the one you want. Then pick the equation that leaves out the fifth.

| Equation | Leaves out |
|---|---|
| $v = u + at$ | $d$ |
| $d = \tfrac12(u + v)t$ | $a$ |
| $d = ut + \tfrac12at^2$ | $v$ |
| $v^2 = u^2 + 2ad$ | $t$ |

## Equations that contain the time

:::example Displacement from two velocities and a time
A train speeds up steadily from 12 m/s to 26 m/s in 4 s. How far does it travel in that time?
@step Known: $u = 12$ m/s, $v = 26$ m/s, $t = 4$ s. Wanted: $d$. The acceleration is not involved, so use $d = \tfrac12(u + v)t$.
@step $d = \tfrac12(12 + 26) \times 4 = \tfrac12 \times 38 \times 4$.
@answer $d = 76$ m.
:::

:::example Displacement while slowing down
A car is moving at 8 m/s when it starts to slow down with a steady acceleration of $-1\ \text{m/s}^2$. How far does it travel in the next 4 s?
@step Known: $u = 8$ m/s, $a = -1\ \text{m/s}^2$, $t = 4$ s. Wanted: $d$. The final velocity is not involved, so use $d = ut + \tfrac12at^2$.
@step $d = 8 \times 4 + \tfrac12 \times (-1) \times 4^2 = 32 - 8$.
@answer $d = 24$ m.
:::

An object that starts **from rest** has $u = 0$, so the third equation becomes $d = \tfrac12at^2$.

:::example Finding a time
A sprinter starts from rest and accelerates steadily at $4\ \text{m/s}^2$. How long does the sprinter take to cover 32 m?
@step From rest, $d = \tfrac12at^2$. Rearrange for $t^2$: $t^2 = \dfrac{2d}{a}$.
@step $t^2 = \dfrac{2 \times 32}{4} = 16$.
@step Take the square root: $t = \sqrt{16}$.
@answer $t = 4$ s.
:::

:::mistake Squaring the whole of ½at²
In $d = ut + \tfrac12at^2$ only the time is squared. With $a = 4\ \text{m/s}^2$ and $t = 3$ s, the last term is $\tfrac12 \times 4 \times 3^2 = 18$ m, not $(\tfrac12 \times 4 \times 3)^2 = 36$ m.
:::

## When the time is not known

Many problems give a distance where you expected a time. The fourth equation, $v^2 = u^2 + 2ad$, is the one without $t$.

:::example A final velocity
A motorbike moving at 10 m/s accelerates steadily at $1\ \text{m/s}^2$ over a distance of 112 m. What is its final velocity?
@step Known: $u = 10$ m/s, $a = 1\ \text{m/s}^2$, $d = 112$ m. Wanted: $v$. The time is not involved.
@step $v^2 = u^2 + 2ad = 10^2 + 2 \times 1 \times 112 = 100 + 224 = 324$.
@step Take the square root: $v = \sqrt{324}$.
@answer $v = 18$ m/s.
:::

:::example A stopping distance
A car travelling at 12 m/s brakes with a steady acceleration of $-4\ \text{m/s}^2$. How far does it travel before it stops?
@step The car stops, so $v = 0$. Known: $u = 12$ m/s, $a = -4\ \text{m/s}^2$.
@step $0 = 12^2 + 2 \times (-4) \times d$, so $8d = 144$.
@answer $d = 18$ m.
:::

Try the same car at twice the speed, 24 m/s. Now $8d = 576$, so $d = 72$ m. Doubling the speed makes the stopping distance four times as long, because the speed is squared. This is why small increases in speed matter so much on the road.

:::example Two steps
A train's velocity changes steadily from 15 m/s to 7 m/s while it travels 44 m in the positive direction. Find its acceleration and the time this takes.
@step For the acceleration, the time is not known: $a = \dfrac{v^2 - u^2}{2d} = \dfrac{7^2 - 15^2}{2 \times 44} = \dfrac{49 - 225}{88} = -2\ \text{m/s}^2$.
@step Now use $v = u + at$ for the time: $t = \dfrac{v - u}{a} = \dfrac{7 - 15}{-2} = \dfrac{-8}{-2}$.
@answer $a = -2\ \text{m/s}^2$ and $t = 4$ s.
:::

:::mistake Forgetting the square root
$v^2 = u^2 + 2ad$ gives the **square** of the final velocity. In the motorbike example, 324 is $v^2$. The velocity is $\sqrt{324} = 18$ m/s.
:::

:::mistake Using the equations when the acceleration changes
The four equations hold only while the acceleration is constant. If a car speeds up and then brakes, treat the two parts separately: the final velocity of the first part is the initial velocity of the second.
:::

:::summary
- Uniformly accelerated motion has five quantities: $d$, $u$, $v$, $a$ and $t$.
- $v = u + at$, $\;d = \tfrac12(u + v)t$, $\;d = ut + \tfrac12at^2$, $\;v^2 = u^2 + 2ad$.
- List what you know and what you want, then choose the equation that leaves out the fifth quantity.
- From rest, $u = 0$. When an object stops, $v = 0$.
- Slowing down in the positive direction is a negative acceleration.
- The equations hold only for a constant acceleration.
:::
