Kinematics describes how things move. Dynamics asks why. The answer is always the same: forces. Before any calculation, a physicist draws the object alone, strips away everything around it, and marks each force that acts on it. Get that drawing right and the rest of the problem follows from it.

## The forces you will meet

A **force** is a push or a pull. It is a vector, measured in newtons (N). Four forces appear again and again in mechanics.

| Force | Symbol | Acts when | Direction |
|---|---|---|---|
| Weight (force of gravity) | $F_g$ | always, near a planet | straight down |
| Normal force | $F_N$ | the object touches a surface | perpendicular to the surface, away from it |
| Tension | $F_T$ | a rope, string or cable pulls | along the rope, away from the object |
| Friction | $F_f$ | surfaces rub or try to slide | along the surface, against the sliding |

:::definition Weight
The **weight** of an object is the force of gravity on it. It acts straight down, and its size is

$$F_g = mg$$

where $m$ is the mass in kilograms and $g = 9.81\ \text{m/s}^2$ near the Earth's surface. Mass is measured in kilograms and is the same everywhere. Weight is a force, measured in newtons.
:::

:::example The weight of a box
A box has a mass of 12 kg. Use $g = 9.81\ \text{m/s}^2$. What is its weight?
@step $F_g = mg = 12 \times 9.81$.
@answer $F_g = 117.7$ N, straight down.
:::

## Free-body diagrams

:::definition Free-body diagram
A **free-body diagram** shows one object on its own, with an arrow for every force that acts **on** that object. Each arrow starts on the object, points in the direction of the force and is labelled.

Forces that the object exerts on other things do not belong on its diagram.
:::

To draw one, ask two questions. Is the object near a planet? Then it has a weight. What is touching the object? Each thing that touches it, a surface, a rope or a hand, can exert a force on it. Nothing else can.

When an object is not accelerating in some direction, the forces along that direction **balance**. A box at rest on a floor does not move up or down, so the forces up equal the forces down. That is how we find the normal force.

:::example A push from above
A crate of mass 20 kg rests on a level floor. A worker pushes straight down on it with a force of 50 N. Use $g = 9.81\ \text{m/s}^2$. What is the normal force of the floor on the crate?
@step Weight: $F_g = mg = 20 \times 9.81 = 196.2$ N, downwards.
@step Forces down: the weight and the push. Force up: the normal force. The crate does not accelerate, so they balance.
@step $F_N = F_g + 50 = 196.2 + 50$.
@answer $F_N = 246.2$ N. The floor must hold up the crate and resist the push as well.
:::

:::example A pull from above
The same 20 kg crate is pulled straight up by a rope with a force of 80 N, which is not enough to lift it. What is the normal force now?
@step Forces up: the normal force and the pull. Force down: the weight, 196.2 N.
@step $F_N + 80 = 196.2$, so $F_N = 196.2 - 80$.
@answer $F_N = 116.2$ N. The rope carries part of the weight, so the floor has less to hold up.
:::

:::example Two boxes in a stack
A 4 kg box rests on top of a 10 kg box, which rests on a level floor. Find the normal force of the lower box on the upper box, and the normal force of the floor on the lower box.
@step Upper box alone: only its weight pulls down, so $F_N = 4 \times 9.81 = 39.2$ N.
@step The floor supports both boxes: $F_N = (4 + 10) \times 9.81 = 137.3$ N.
@answer 39.2 N on the upper box, and 137.3 N from the floor.
:::

:::mistake Assuming the normal force always equals the weight
The normal force equals $mg$ only when an object rests on a level surface with no other vertical forces. Push down on the object, pull up on it or tilt the surface, and the normal force changes. Always find it from the balance of forces.
:::

:::mistake Adding a force "of motion"
A ball that has left your hand has no force of the throw on it. Once nothing is touching it, the only force is its weight. An object does not need a force to keep moving, only to change its motion.
:::

## Net force along a line

:::definition Net force
The **net force**, $F_{\text{net}}$, is the vector sum of all the forces on an object. It is the single force that would have the same effect as all of them together.

For forces along one line, choose a positive direction, give each force a sign, and add.
:::

:::example Two forces in opposite directions
Two forces act on a cart along one line: 25 N [E] and 35 N [W]. What is the net force?
@step Take east as positive: $F_{\text{net}} = 25 - 35 = -10$ N.
@step The answer is negative, so it points west.
@answer 10 N [W].
:::

:::example Three forces
A sled is pulled east with a force of 120 N and pushed east from behind with a force of 45 N. A friction force of 60 N acts west. What is the net force on the sled?
@step Take east as positive: $F_{\text{net}} = 120 + 45 - 60$.
@answer 105 N [E].
:::

If an object is at rest or moves at a **steady velocity**, it is not accelerating, so the net force on it is zero. This lets you find a force you were not given.

:::example A steady velocity
A box is pushed north across a level floor with a horizontal force of 45 N. It moves at a steady velocity. What is the friction force on the box?
@step The velocity is steady, so the net force is zero.
@step The friction must balance the push: equal in size and opposite in direction.
@answer 45 N [S].
:::

:::example A vertical net force
A crane cable pulls upwards on a 250 kg load with a force of 2800 N. Use $g = 9.81\ \text{m/s}^2$. Find the net force on the load.
@step Weight: $F_g = 250 \times 9.81 = 2452.5$ N, downwards.
@step Take up as positive: $F_{\text{net}} = 2800 - 2452.5 = 347.5$ N.
@answer 347.5 N upwards. The load speeds up as it rises.
:::

:::mistake Thinking that a moving object must have a net force forwards
The box in the third example is moving, yet the net force on it is zero. A net force is needed to change a velocity, not to keep one. This is the idea behind Newton's first law, in the next lesson.
:::

:::summary
- The common forces are weight ($F_g = mg$, straight down), the normal force (perpendicular to the surface), tension (along the rope) and friction (against the sliding).
- A free-body diagram shows one object, with an arrow for every force that acts on it.
- When an object does not accelerate along a direction, the forces along that direction balance. Use this to find the normal force.
- The net force is the vector sum of the forces. Along a line, give each force a sign and add.
- At rest or at a steady velocity, the net force is zero.
:::
