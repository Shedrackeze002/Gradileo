For two thousand years people believed that a moving object needs a force to keep it moving, because everything they pushed soon stopped. Isaac Newton saw that the stopping was itself the work of a force, friction, and wrote three laws that have been used ever since to predict the motion of everything from tennis balls to spacecraft.

## The three laws

:::definition Newton's first law
An object stays at rest, or keeps moving at a constant velocity, unless a net force acts on it.

The tendency of an object to keep its velocity is called **inertia**. The more mass an object has, the more inertia it has.
:::

:::definition Newton's second law
The acceleration of an object is proportional to the net force on it and inversely proportional to its mass. It points in the direction of the net force.

$$F_{\text{net}} = ma$$

with the net force in newtons, the mass in kilograms and the acceleration in m/s². One newton is the net force that gives 1 kg an acceleration of $1\ \text{m/s}^2$.
:::

:::definition Newton's third law
When object A exerts a force on object B, object B exerts a force on object A that is equal in size and opposite in direction.

The two forces are called an **interaction pair**. They always act on **different** objects, so they never cancel each other.
:::

When a truck hits a small car, the force of the truck on the car is exactly as large as the force of the car on the truck. The car comes off worse because the same force acts on a much smaller mass, and so gives it a much larger acceleration.

## Using the second law

The second law uses the **net** force. When several forces act, find the net force first.

:::example An acceleration with friction
A 40 kg parcel is pushed along a level floor with a horizontal force of 60 N. A friction force of 40 N acts on it. What is its acceleration?
@step Net force: $F_{\text{net}} = 60 - 40 = 20$ N, in the direction of the push.
@step $a = \dfrac{F_{\text{net}}}{m} = \dfrac{20}{40}$.
@answer $a = 0.5\ \text{m/s}^2$.
:::

:::example The force needed
A friction force of 50 N acts on a 40 kg crate as it slides along a level floor. What horizontal push gives the crate an acceleration of $2\ \text{m/s}^2$?
@step Net force needed: $F_{\text{net}} = ma = 40 \times 2 = 80$ N.
@step The push must supply this net force and also cancel the friction: push $= 80 + 50$.
@answer 130 N.
:::

The acceleration links dynamics to kinematics. If you know how the motion changes, the equations of motion give the acceleration, and the second law then gives the force.

:::example A braking force
A car of mass 1200 kg travelling at 15 m/s brakes steadily and stops in 50 m. Find the size of its acceleration and of the net braking force.
@step The time is not known, so use $v^2 = u^2 + 2ad$ with $v = 0$: $0 = 15^2 + 2a \times 50$.
@step $a = \dfrac{-225}{100} = -2.25\ \text{m/s}^2$. Its size is $2.25\ \text{m/s}^2$.
@step $F_{\text{net}} = ma = 1200 \times 2.25$.
@answer The acceleration has a size of $2.25\ \text{m/s}^2$, and the braking force is 2700 N.
:::

:::mistake Using one force in place of the net force
In the first example the acceleration is not $\dfrac{60}{40} = 1.5\ \text{m/s}^2$. The friction takes away 40 N of the push, and only the 20 N that is left accelerates the parcel.
:::

:::mistake Cancelling the two forces of an interaction pair
The Earth pulls a book down, and the book pulls the Earth up. These forces are equal and opposite, but they act on different objects, so they cannot cancel. The force that balances the weight of a book on a table is the normal force of the table, a different force that acts on the book.
:::

## Forces in vertical motion

A load on a cable, a lift, a person on a scale: each has two vertical forces, an upward force and the weight. Take **up as positive**. Then the second law reads

$$F_{\text{up}} - mg = ma$$

where $F_{\text{up}}$ is the tension or the normal force, and $a$ is positive for an upward acceleration and negative for a downward one.

:::example A lift that accelerates
A lift and its passengers have a total mass of 650 kg. Use $g = 9.81\ \text{m/s}^2$. Find the tension in its cable when the lift accelerates upwards at $0.5\ \text{m/s}^2$, when it moves at a steady velocity, and when it accelerates downwards at $0.5\ \text{m/s}^2$.
@step Rearrange: $F_T = mg + ma = m(g + a)$.
@step Upwards, $a = +0.5$: $F_T = 650 \times (9.81 + 0.5) = 6701.5$ N.
@step Steady velocity, $a = 0$: $F_T = 650 \times 9.81 = 6376.5$ N. The tension equals the weight, whether the lift is going up or down.
@step Downwards, $a = -0.5$: $F_T = 650 \times (9.81 - 0.5) = 6051.5$ N.
@answer 6701.5 N, 6376.5 N and 6051.5 N.
:::

A bathroom scale does not measure your weight directly. It reads the normal force that it exerts on you, which is called your **apparent weight**.

:::example Standing on a scale in a lift
A 60 kg person stands on a scale in a lift that accelerates upwards at $2\ \text{m/s}^2$. What force does the scale read?
@step Take up as positive: $F_N - mg = ma$, so $F_N = m(g + a)$.
@step $F_N = 60 \times (9.81 + 2) = 60 \times 11.81$.
@answer The scale reads 708.6 N. The person's true weight is $60 \times 9.81 = 588.6$ N, so the person feels heavier.
:::

:::example An acceleration from a tension
A cable pulls upwards with a force of 1300 N on a 120 kg load. Taking up as positive, what is the acceleration of the load?
@step Weight: $mg = 120 \times 9.81 = 1177.2$ N.
@step Net force: $F_{\text{net}} = 1300 - 1177.2 = 122.8$ N.
@step $a = \dfrac{122.8}{120}$.
@answer $a = 1.02\ \text{m/s}^2$, upwards.
:::

:::mistake Deciding the tension from the direction of travel
The tension depends on the **acceleration**, not on which way the lift is moving. A lift that travels upwards while slowing down has a downward acceleration, so the tension in its cable is less than the weight.
:::

:::summary
- First law: with no net force, an object keeps its velocity. Mass measures inertia.
- Second law: $F_{\text{net}} = ma$. Find the net force first, then the acceleration, or the other way round.
- Third law: forces come in pairs that are equal in size, opposite in direction and act on different objects.
- The equations of motion and the second law share the acceleration.
- For vertical motion, with up as positive: $F_{\text{up}} - mg = ma$. The scale reading or the tension is $m(g + a)$.
:::
