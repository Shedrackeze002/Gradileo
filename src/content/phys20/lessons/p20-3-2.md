Newton's second law says that an acceleration needs a net force. An object that moves round a circle accelerates towards the centre, so something must be pushing or pulling it towards the centre. This lesson is about finding that something.

## Centripetal force

:::definition Centripetal force
The **centripetal force**, $F_c$, is the net force towards the centre that keeps an object moving in a circle. From $F_{\text{net}} = ma$ with $a_c = \dfrac{v^2}{r}$:

$$F_c = \dfrac{mv^2}{r}$$
:::

Centripetal force is not a new kind of force. It is a **job** that an ordinary force does. For every object that moves in a circle, ask which real force points to the centre.

| Motion | Force that provides the centripetal force |
|---|---|
| a ball whirled on a string | tension in the string |
| a car on a level bend | friction between the tyres and the road |
| the Moon round the Earth | gravity |
| clothes in a spinning drum | the normal force of the drum |

:::example A ball on a string
A 0.5 kg ball on a string moves in a horizontal circle of radius 0.8 m on a frictionless table, at a steady 4 m/s. What centripetal force acts on it?
@step $F_c = \dfrac{mv^2}{r} = \dfrac{0.5 \times 4^2}{0.8} = \dfrac{8}{0.8}$.
@answer $F_c = 10$ N, towards the centre. The tension in the string provides it.
:::

:::example From the period
A 0.2 kg puck on a frictionless table is tied to a string and moves in a horizontal circle of radius 0.5 m with a period of 0.8 s. What is the tension in the string?
@step Speed: $v = \dfrac{2\pi r}{T} = \dfrac{2\pi \times 0.5}{0.8} = 3.927$ m/s.
@step The tension is the only horizontal force, so it is the centripetal force: $F_T = \dfrac{mv^2}{r} = \dfrac{0.2 \times 3.927^2}{0.5}$.
@answer $F_T = 6.2$ N.
:::

:::example The speed at which a string breaks
A string breaks if its tension goes above 40 N. It whirls a 0.5 kg puck in a horizontal circle of radius 0.8 m on a frictionless table. What is the greatest speed the puck can have?
@step At the greatest speed the tension is 40 N. Rearrange $F_c = \dfrac{mv^2}{r}$: $v = \sqrt{\dfrac{F_c r}{m}}$.
@step $v = \sqrt{\dfrac{40 \times 0.8}{0.5}} = \sqrt{64}$.
@answer $v = 8$ m/s.
:::

What happens when the string does break? With no force towards the centre, there is nothing to turn the puck. By Newton's first law it carries on with the velocity it had at that instant, in a straight line along the **tangent** to the circle. It does not fly straight outwards.

:::mistake Adding an outward force
A passenger in a car that turns left feels thrown to the right, and it is tempting to draw a force that pushes outwards. No such force acts. The passenger's body tries to keep going in a straight line, and the car door pushes the passenger **inwards**, round the bend. On a free-body diagram, draw only real forces: tension, friction, gravity and the normal force.
:::

## Curves and vertical circles

On a level road only friction can push a car sideways. It is static friction, because the tyres roll without sliding.

:::example A car on a level bend
A car of mass 1200 kg takes a level bend of radius 50 m at a steady 15 m/s. What friction force must the road provide?
@step $F_f = \dfrac{mv^2}{r} = \dfrac{1200 \times 15^2}{50} = \dfrac{270\,000}{50}$.
@answer $F_f = 5400$ N, towards the centre of the bend.
:::

Static friction has a greatest value, $\mu_s F_N$, and on a level road $F_N = mg$. If the bend needs more than that, the car skids.

:::example The greatest speed without skidding
The coefficient of static friction between a car's tyres and a level road is 0.6. Use $g = 9.81\ \text{m/s}^2$. What is the greatest speed at which the car can take a bend of radius 50 m?
@step At the greatest speed, the greatest friction is the centripetal force: $\mu_s mg = \dfrac{mv^2}{r}$.
@step The mass cancels: $v = \sqrt{\mu_s g r} = \sqrt{0.6 \times 9.81 \times 50} = \sqrt{294.3}$.
@answer $v = 17.2$ m/s, whatever the mass of the car.
:::

On a wet road the coefficient is smaller, so the greatest safe speed is lower. This is why drivers must slow down for a bend in the rain.

In a **vertical circle** the weight of the object matters. At each point, add up the forces **towards the centre** and set the total equal to $\dfrac{mv^2}{r}$.

- At the **lowest** point the tension points up, towards the centre, and the weight points down, away from it: $F_T - mg = \dfrac{mv^2}{r}$.
- At the **highest** point both the tension and the weight point down, towards the centre: $F_T + mg = \dfrac{mv^2}{r}$.

:::example A ball in a vertical circle
A 0.5 kg ball on a string is whirled in a vertical circle of radius 1 m. It passes the highest point at 5 m/s and the lowest point at 8 m/s. Use $g = 9.81\ \text{m/s}^2$. Find the tension in the string at each point.
@step Weight: $mg = 0.5 \times 9.81 = 4.905$ N.
@step Highest point: $F_T = \dfrac{mv^2}{r} - mg = \dfrac{0.5 \times 5^2}{1} - 4.905 = 12.5 - 4.905$.
@step Lowest point: $F_T = \dfrac{mv^2}{r} + mg = \dfrac{0.5 \times 8^2}{1} + 4.905 = 32 + 4.905$.
@answer 7.6 N at the highest point and 36.9 N at the lowest point.
:::

The string is under the most strain at the bottom, where it must hold the ball up and turn it as well. The ball is also faster there, because it has fallen through the height of the circle.

At the top, gravity helps to turn the ball. If the ball goes slowly enough, gravity alone is all the centripetal force that is needed, and the string goes slack.

:::example The smallest speed at the top of a loop
A roller coaster car goes round a vertical loop of radius 10 m. Use $g = 9.81\ \text{m/s}^2$. What is the smallest speed at the top for which the car stays on the track without being held?
@step At the smallest speed the track exerts no force at the top, so gravity alone is the centripetal force: $mg = \dfrac{mv^2}{r}$.
@step The mass cancels: $v = \sqrt{gr} = \sqrt{9.81 \times 10} = \sqrt{98.1}$.
@answer $v = 9.90$ m/s.
:::

:::mistake Using the same equation at the top and the bottom
The weight always points down, but "towards the centre" is down at the top of the circle and up at the bottom. At the top the weight **helps** ($F_T + mg$), and at the bottom it **opposes** ($F_T - mg$). Draw the free-body diagram at each point before you write an equation.
:::

:::summary
- Centripetal force is the net force towards the centre: $F_c = \dfrac{mv^2}{r}$.
- It is provided by a real force: tension, friction, gravity or a normal force.
- If the force stops, the object moves off along the tangent.
- On a level bend, friction is the centripetal force, and the greatest speed is $v = \sqrt{\mu_s g r}$.
- In a vertical circle: $F_T - mg = \dfrac{mv^2}{r}$ at the bottom and $F_T + mg = \dfrac{mv^2}{r}$ at the top. The smallest speed at the top is $\sqrt{gr}$.
:::
