A child on a swing, a guitar string, a mass bobbing on a spring and the pendulum of a clock all do the same thing: they move back and forth through a middle position, over and over. Motion like this is called an **oscillation**, and the simplest kind has a name of its own.

## Describing an oscillation

:::definition The words for an oscillation
- The **equilibrium position** is where the object rests when it is not oscillating. The net force on it there is zero.
- The **displacement**, $x$, is how far the object is from the equilibrium position at some instant.
- The **amplitude**, $A$, is the greatest displacement: the distance from the equilibrium position to one extreme.
- The **period**, $T$, is the time for one complete oscillation, in seconds.
- The **frequency**, $f$, is the number of complete oscillations each second, in hertz (Hz).

$$f = \dfrac{1}{T} \qquad T = \dfrac{1}{f}$$
:::

One complete oscillation brings the object back to where it started, moving the same way. For a mass on a spring that starts at the top, that is down to the bottom **and back up to the top**.

:::example Period and frequency
A mass on a spring makes 20 complete oscillations in 8 s. Find its period and its frequency.
@step Period: $T = \dfrac{8}{20} = 0.4$ s for each oscillation.
@step Frequency: $f = \dfrac{20}{8} = 2.5$ oscillations each second.
@answer $T = 0.4$ s and $f = 2.5$ Hz.
:::

:::example Reading a displacement-time graph
The graph shows the displacement of an oscillating mass. Read its amplitude and its period.
@step The graph reaches 4 cm above zero and 4 cm below, so the amplitude is 4 cm.
@step The first peak is at 0.5 s and the next at 2.5 s. One complete cycle takes $2.5 - 0.5 = 2$ s.
@answer The amplitude is 4 cm and the period is 2 s.
:::

In one complete oscillation the object goes from the middle out to one extreme, back through the middle to the other extreme, and back to the middle. That is **four amplitudes** of distance.

:::example Distance travelled
A mass on a spring oscillates with an amplitude of 5 cm and a frequency of 2 Hz. How far does it travel in 6 s?
@step Number of oscillations: $2 \times 6 = 12$.
@step Each oscillation covers $4 \times 5 = 20$ cm.
@answer $12 \times 20 = 240$ cm.
:::

:::mistake Measuring the amplitude from one extreme to the other
The amplitude is measured from the **equilibrium position** to an extreme. A mass that moves between 4 cm above and 4 cm below its rest position has an amplitude of 4 cm, not 8 cm.
:::

## The restoring force

Why does an oscillating object keep coming back? Because whenever it is away from equilibrium, a force pushes or pulls it back. This is the **restoring force**.

For a mass on a spring, Hooke's law gives the size of the force: $kx$. Its direction is always opposite to the displacement. Stretch the spring to the right and it pulls to the left. Compress it to the left and it pushes to the right. A minus sign records this:

:::definition Simple harmonic motion
An object is in **simple harmonic motion** when the restoring force on it is proportional to its displacement and points towards the equilibrium position:

$$F = -kx$$

By Newton's second law the acceleration is $a = -\dfrac{kx}{m}$.
:::

This one rule explains the whole motion.

| Position | Displacement | Restoring force and acceleration | Speed |
|---|---|---|---|
| at an extreme | greatest ($x = A$) | greatest, towards equilibrium | zero for an instant |
| at equilibrium | zero | zero | greatest |

At equilibrium there is no force, but the object is moving at its fastest, so it overshoots. The force then builds up against the motion, stops the object at the other extreme and sends it back.

:::example The size of the restoring force
A mass on a spring with a spring constant of 50 N/m is 8 cm from its equilibrium position. What is the size of the restoring force on it?
@step Change the units: $x = 8\ \text{cm} = 0.08$ m.
@step $F = kx = 50 \times 0.08$.
@answer $F = 4$ N, towards the equilibrium position.
:::

:::example The greatest acceleration
A 0.5 kg mass on that spring oscillates on a frictionless surface with an amplitude of 8 cm. What is the size of its greatest acceleration?
@step The acceleration is greatest at an extreme, where $x = 0.08$ m and the force is 4 N.
@step $a = \dfrac{F}{m} = \dfrac{4}{0.5}$.
@answer $a = 8\ \text{m/s}^2$.
:::

:::example From an acceleration to a position
A 0.5 kg mass on a horizontal spring with a spring constant of 100 N/m oscillates on a frictionless surface. At one instant its acceleration is $5\ \text{m/s}^2$ to the left. Where is the mass?
@step Restoring force: $F = ma = 0.5 \times 5 = 2.5$ N.
@step Displacement: $x = \dfrac{F}{k} = \dfrac{2.5}{100} = 0.025$ m.
@step The acceleration points to the left, towards equilibrium, so the mass must be on the right.
@answer 2.5 cm to the right of the equilibrium position.
:::

:::mistake Thinking the force is greatest where the speed is greatest
It is the other way round. At the equilibrium position the object is fastest, but the restoring force and the acceleration are **zero**. At the extremes the object stops for an instant, and the force and the acceleration are greatest.
:::

:::summary
- An oscillation is a motion back and forth through an equilibrium position.
- The amplitude is the greatest displacement from equilibrium. One oscillation covers four amplitudes.
- Period and frequency: $f = \dfrac{1}{T}$.
- Simple harmonic motion: the restoring force is proportional to the displacement and points towards equilibrium, $F = -kx$.
- At the extremes the force and acceleration are greatest and the speed is zero. At equilibrium the force is zero and the speed is greatest.
:::
