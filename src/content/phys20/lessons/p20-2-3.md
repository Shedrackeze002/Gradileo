Lean on a heavy wardrobe and at first nothing happens. Push harder, and harder still, and suddenly it lurches forward, and then it is oddly easier to keep it sliding than it was to get it started. Those two stages are two different kinds of friction.

## Static friction

:::definition Static friction
**Static friction** acts between two surfaces that are **not sliding** over each other. It takes whatever size is needed to prevent sliding, up to a greatest value:

$$F_{f,\,\text{max}} = \mu_s F_N$$

where $\mu_s$ is the **coefficient of static friction** and $F_N$ is the normal force. A coefficient of friction has no unit. It depends on the two surfaces: about 0.1 for a ski on snow, and close to 1 for a rubber tyre on dry road.
:::

Static friction is a force that adjusts itself. Push a resting box with 10 N and static friction pushes back with 10 N. Push with 30 N and it pushes back with 30 N. Only when your push passes the greatest value, $\mu_s F_N$, does the box break free.

On a level surface with no other vertical forces, the normal force equals the weight, so $F_N = mg$.

:::example Will it move?
A 16 kg suitcase rests on a level floor. The coefficient of static friction between them is 0.5. Use $g = 9.81\ \text{m/s}^2$. Does the suitcase move when it is pushed horizontally with 60 N? With 90 N?
@step Normal force: $F_N = mg = 16 \times 9.81 = 156.96$ N.
@step Greatest static friction: $\mu_s F_N = 0.5 \times 156.96 = 78.5$ N.
@step A push of 60 N is less than 78.5 N, so the suitcase stays at rest. The static friction on it is 60 N, just enough to balance the push.
@step A push of 90 N is greater than 78.5 N, so static friction cannot hold the suitcase.
@answer It stays at rest with 60 N and moves with 90 N.
:::

:::example Finding the coefficient
A horizontal force on a 13 kg box at rest on a level floor is slowly increased. The box just starts to move when the force reaches 45 N. What is the coefficient of static friction?
@step At that moment the force equals the greatest static friction: $45 = \mu_s F_N$.
@step $F_N = mg = 13 \times 9.81 = 127.53$ N.
@step $\mu_s = \dfrac{45}{127.53}$.
@answer $\mu_s = 0.35$.
:::

:::mistake Giving static friction its greatest value every time
$\mu_s F_N$ is the **most** that static friction can be. If a box with a greatest static friction of 78.5 N is pushed with 60 N, the friction on it is 60 N, not 78.5 N. If it were 78.5 N, the box would accelerate backwards towards you.
:::

## Kinetic friction

:::definition Kinetic friction
**Kinetic friction** acts between two surfaces that **are sliding** over each other. It opposes the sliding, and its size is

$$F_f = \mu_k F_N$$

where $\mu_k$ is the **coefficient of kinetic friction**. For the same pair of surfaces, $\mu_k$ is usually smaller than $\mu_s$, which is why an object is easier to keep moving than to start.
:::

In this model, kinetic friction does not depend on the speed of the object or on the area in contact. It depends only on the surfaces and on how hard they are pressed together.

:::example A friction force and an acceleration
A 12 kg box is pushed across a level floor with a horizontal force of 50 N. The coefficient of kinetic friction is 0.2. Use $g = 9.81\ \text{m/s}^2$. Find the friction force and the acceleration of the box.
@step Normal force: $F_N = mg = 12 \times 9.81 = 117.72$ N.
@step Friction: $F_f = \mu_k F_N = 0.2 \times 117.72 = 23.5$ N, against the motion.
@step Net force: $F_{\text{net}} = 50 - 23.54 = 26.46$ N.
@step $a = \dfrac{F_{\text{net}}}{m} = \dfrac{26.46}{12}$.
@answer The friction force is 23.5 N, and $a = 2.20\ \text{m/s}^2$.
:::

When friction is the only horizontal force, something useful happens. The net force is $\mu_k mg$, so $ma = \mu_k mg$, and the mass cancels:

$$a = \mu_k g$$

A heavy sled and a light sled on the same snow slow down at the same rate.

:::example Sliding to a stop
A hockey puck slides across level ice with an initial speed of 6 m/s. The coefficient of kinetic friction is 0.2, and friction is the only horizontal force. Find the size of its acceleration and how far it slides before it stops.
@step The size of the acceleration is $\mu_k g = 0.2 \times 9.81 = 1.96\ \text{m/s}^2$. It points against the motion.
@step Use $v^2 = u^2 + 2ad$ with $v = 0$ and $a = -1.962\ \text{m/s}^2$: $0 = 6^2 - 2 \times 1.962 \times d$.
@step $d = \dfrac{36}{3.924}$.
@answer The acceleration has a size of $1.96\ \text{m/s}^2$, and the puck slides 9.2 m.
:::

:::mistake Using the weight when the normal force is different
Friction depends on the **normal force**, which equals $mg$ only on a level surface with no other vertical forces. If someone presses down on the box, pulls up on it at an angle, or the surface is tilted, find $F_N$ first from the balance of forces.
:::

:::summary
- Static friction acts when surfaces do not slide. It matches the applied force, up to a greatest value of $\mu_s F_N$.
- An object starts to move only when the applied force is greater than $\mu_s F_N$.
- Kinetic friction acts when surfaces slide: $F_f = \mu_k F_N$, against the sliding.
- Coefficients of friction have no unit, and $\mu_k$ is usually smaller than $\mu_s$.
- When friction is the only horizontal force, $a = \mu_k g$, whatever the mass.
:::
