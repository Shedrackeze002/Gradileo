Pluck a guitar string and it does not look like a wave running back and forth. It looks like a blur with a fixed shape, wide in the middle and still at the ends. That shape is a standing wave, and it is the reason a string, a flute and a bridge each have their own favourite frequencies.

## Standing waves on a string

A wave sent along a string reflects from the fixed end and travels back through the waves that are still arriving. Two waves of the same frequency and amplitude, travelling in opposite directions, add up to a pattern that stays in one place.

:::definition Standing wave
A **standing wave** is the pattern made by two identical waves travelling in opposite directions.

- A **node** is a point that never moves. The two waves always cancel there.
- An **antinode** is a point of greatest movement, halfway between two nodes.

Neighbouring nodes are **half a wavelength** apart, so each loop of the pattern is $\dfrac{\lambda}{2}$ long.
:::

A string fixed at both ends must have a node at each end. Only patterns with a whole number of loops fit. With $n$ loops on a string of length $L$:

$$L = n \times \dfrac{\lambda}{2} \qquad\text{so}\qquad \lambda = \dfrac{2L}{n}$$

and from $v = f\lambda$ the frequencies that fit are

$$f = \dfrac{nv}{2L}$$

The pattern with one loop has the lowest frequency, called the **fundamental**. The patterns with two, three and more loops are the higher **harmonics**, at twice, three times and more times the fundamental frequency.

:::example The spacing of the nodes
A standing wave is set up on a string by waves of wavelength 1.2 m. How far apart are neighbouring nodes?
@step Neighbouring nodes are half a wavelength apart: $\dfrac{1.2}{2}$.
@answer 0.6 m.
:::

:::example The fundamental
A guitar string 0.65 m long is fixed at both ends and vibrates with a single loop. What is the wavelength of the standing wave?
@step One loop is half a wavelength, and it fills the string: $L = \dfrac{\lambda}{2}$.
@answer $\lambda = 2 \times 0.65 = 1.3$ m.
:::

:::example The frequency of a harmonic
A string 0.8 m long is fixed at both ends, and waves travel along it at 240 m/s. At what frequency does it vibrate with 3 loops?
@step Three loops fill the string: $\lambda = \dfrac{2L}{n} = \dfrac{2 \times 0.8}{3} = 0.5333$ m.
@step $f = \dfrac{v}{\lambda} = \dfrac{240}{0.5333}$.
@answer $f = 450$ Hz. The fundamental of this string is 150 Hz, and this is three times that.
:::

:::example The speed of the waves
A vibrator shakes a string at 60 Hz. The string is 1.5 m long and fixed at both ends, and a standing wave with 4 loops appears. How fast do waves travel on the string?
@step Each loop is $\dfrac{1.5}{4} = 0.375$ m long, which is half a wavelength: $\lambda = 0.75$ m.
@step $v = f\lambda = 60 \times 0.75$.
@answer $v = 45$ m/s.
:::

:::mistake Taking one loop for a whole wavelength
A loop of a standing wave, from one node to the next, is only **half** a wavelength. A whole wavelength is two loops. A string with 4 loops holds 2 wavelengths, not 4.
:::

## Resonance

Every object that can vibrate has one or more **natural frequencies**, the frequencies at which it vibrates when it is disturbed and left alone. For a string they are the fundamental and its harmonics.

:::definition Resonance
**Resonance** is the large vibration that builds up when an object is driven at one of its natural frequencies. Each push arrives in step with the vibration, so the energy adds up.
:::

Pushing a child on a swing is resonance: small pushes, timed to the swing's own period, build a large amplitude. Pushes at any other rate work against the swing half the time. A singer can shatter a wine glass only with a note at the natural frequency of the glass, and engineers must make sure that wind and footsteps do not drive a bridge at one of its natural frequencies.

The air in a tube has natural frequencies too. Hold a vibrating tuning fork over a tube that is closed at the bottom, and for certain lengths of air column the sound becomes much louder: the air is resonating. The standing wave in the air must have a **node at the closed end**, where the air cannot move, and an **antinode at the open end**, where it is free. The shortest column that fits is a quarter of a wavelength:

$$L_1 = \dfrac{\lambda}{4} \qquad L_2 = \dfrac{3\lambda}{4} \qquad L_3 = \dfrac{5\lambda}{4} \qquad \dots$$

The resonant lengths are **half a wavelength apart**.

:::example The shortest resonant column
The speed of sound in the air is 340 m/s. A tuning fork of frequency 512 Hz is held over a tube that is closed at the bottom by water. What is the shortest length of air column that resonates?
@step Wavelength: $\lambda = \dfrac{v}{f} = \dfrac{340}{512} = 0.6641$ m.
@step $L = \dfrac{\lambda}{4} = \dfrac{0.6641}{4} = 0.1660$ m.
@answer 16.6 cm.
:::

This gives a neat way to measure the speed of sound. The real antinode sits a little above the open end of the tube, so a single length gives a slightly wrong wavelength. The **difference** between two resonant lengths does not have that problem.

:::example Measuring the speed of sound
A tuning fork of frequency 440 Hz is held over a tube whose air column can be changed. The sound is loudest when the column is 18.9 cm long, and loudest again at 58.3 cm. Find the speed of sound in the air.
@step The two lengths differ by half a wavelength: $\dfrac{\lambda}{2} = 58.3 - 18.9 = 39.4$ cm.
@step Wavelength: $\lambda = 78.8\ \text{cm} = 0.788$ m.
@step $v = f\lambda = 440 \times 0.788$.
@answer $v = 347$ m/s.
:::

:::mistake Using a quarter wavelength for the gap between resonances
The first resonant length is a quarter of a wavelength, but the **gap** between one resonant length and the next is half a wavelength. Double the gap to find the wavelength.
:::

:::summary
- A standing wave is made by two identical waves travelling in opposite directions. Nodes do not move, and antinodes move the most.
- Each loop is half a wavelength long.
- A string fixed at both ends with $n$ loops: $\lambda = \dfrac{2L}{n}$ and $f = \dfrac{nv}{2L}$.
- Resonance: a large vibration builds up when an object is driven at a natural frequency.
- An air column closed at one end resonates at $\dfrac{\lambda}{4}$, $\dfrac{3\lambda}{4}$, $\dfrac{5\lambda}{4}$, half a wavelength apart.
:::
