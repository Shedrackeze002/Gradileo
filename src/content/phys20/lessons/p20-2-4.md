A ball released on a gentle slope rolls away slowly, and on a steep one it races off. Gravity is the same in both cases. What changes is how much of the weight points along the slope. Working that out is one of the most useful skills in mechanics, and it uses the components you met in kinematics.

## Splitting the weight

On a ramp, an object can only move along the surface. So we turn the axes: one axis **along the ramp** and one **perpendicular to it**. The normal force and friction already lie along these axes. Only the weight, which points straight down, has to be resolved.

:::definition Components of the weight on a ramp
For an object of mass $m$ on a ramp inclined at an angle $\theta$ to the horizontal:

$$\text{along the ramp, pointing down the slope:}\quad mg\sin\theta$$

$$\text{perpendicular to the ramp, pointing into it:}\quad mg\cos\theta$$

The object does not move into or out of the ramp, so the normal force balances the perpendicular component: $F_N = mg\cos\theta$.
:::

Check the two extreme cases. On a level surface, $\theta = 0°$: $\sin 0° = 0$, so nothing pulls the object along, and $\cos 0° = 1$, so the normal force is the whole weight. On a vertical wall, $\theta = 90°$: the whole weight points along the surface and the normal force is zero.

:::example Along the ramp and into it
An 8 kg block rests on a ramp inclined at 25° to the horizontal. Use $g = 9.81\ \text{m/s}^2$. Find the component of its weight along the ramp and the normal force of the ramp on the block.
@step Weight: $mg = 8 \times 9.81 = 78.48$ N.
@step Along the ramp: $mg\sin\theta = 78.48 \times \sin 25° = 33.2$ N.
@step Normal force: $F_N = mg\cos\theta = 78.48 \times \cos 25° = 71.1$ N.
@answer 33.2 N along the ramp, and a normal force of 71.1 N.
:::

If the block stays at rest, something must balance the 33.2 N that pulls it down the slope. That something is static friction, pointing up the ramp.

:::example Held by friction
The 8 kg block stays at rest on the 25° ramp. Find the friction force on it, and the smallest coefficient of static friction that would hold it there.
@step The block does not accelerate along the ramp, so the friction equals the component of the weight along the ramp: 33.2 N, up the ramp.
@step At the limit, the greatest static friction just balances it: $\mu_s mg\cos\theta = mg\sin\theta$.
@step Divide both sides by $mg\cos\theta$: $\mu_s = \dfrac{\sin\theta}{\cos\theta} = \tan\theta = \tan 25°$.
@answer The friction force is 33.2 N, and the smallest coefficient is 0.47.
:::

The result $\mu_s = \tan\theta$ does not contain the mass. A heavy block and a light block made of the same material start to slip at the same angle.

:::mistake Swapping sine and cosine
On a ramp, the sine goes with the component **along** the slope and the cosine with the component **into** it. This is the other way round from a vector measured from the horizontal, because the angle $\theta$ of the ramp appears between the weight and the perpendicular to the ramp. Test with a nearly level ramp: the pull along it must be small, and $\sin\theta$ is small for a small angle.
:::

## Motion on a ramp

Add the forces along the ramp, then use $F_{\text{net}} = ma$.

**Without friction**, the only force along the ramp is $mg\sin\theta$:

$$ma = mg\sin\theta \quad\text{so}\quad a = g\sin\theta$$

**With kinetic friction**, an object sliding down has friction $\mu_k F_N = \mu_k mg\cos\theta$ pointing up the ramp:

$$ma = mg\sin\theta - \mu_k mg\cos\theta \quad\text{so}\quad a = g(\sin\theta - \mu_k\cos\theta)$$

In both cases the mass cancels.

:::example Sliding with and without friction
A block slides down a ramp inclined at 30° to the horizontal. Use $g = 9.81\ \text{m/s}^2$. Find its acceleration if the ramp is frictionless, and if the coefficient of kinetic friction is 0.2.
@step Frictionless: $a = g\sin\theta = 9.81 \times \sin 30° = 4.91\ \text{m/s}^2$.
@step With friction: $a = g(\sin\theta - \mu_k\cos\theta) = 9.81 \times (0.5 - 0.2 \times 0.8660)$.
@step $a = 9.81 \times 0.3268$.
@answer $4.91\ \text{m/s}^2$ without friction and $3.21\ \text{m/s}^2$ with it.
:::

Once you have the acceleration, the equations of motion give speeds and times.

:::example Speed at the bottom
A cart starts from rest and rolls 12 m down a frictionless ramp inclined at 15° to the horizontal. Find its speed at the bottom and the time it takes.
@step $a = g\sin\theta = 9.81 \times \sin 15° = 2.539\ \text{m/s}^2$.
@step Speed: $v^2 = u^2 + 2ad = 0 + 2 \times 2.539 \times 12 = 60.94$, so $v = 7.8$ m/s.
@step Time: $d = \tfrac12at^2$ gives $t = \sqrt{\dfrac{2 \times 12}{2.539}} = 3.07$ s.
@answer 7.8 m/s, after 3.07 s.
:::

:::example Pushing a crate up a ramp
A 20 kg crate is pushed up a ramp inclined at 20° to the horizontal, at a steady velocity, by a force parallel to the ramp. The coefficient of kinetic friction is 0.25. What is the size of the push?
@step The velocity is steady, so the forces along the ramp balance. The crate moves up, so friction points down the ramp, the same way as the component of the weight.
@step Weight along the ramp: $mg\sin\theta = 20 \times 9.81 \times \sin 20° = 67.10$ N.
@step Friction: $\mu_k mg\cos\theta = 0.25 \times 20 \times 9.81 \times \cos 20° = 46.09$ N.
@step Push $= 67.10 + 46.09$.
@answer 113.2 N.
:::

:::mistake Using mg for the normal force on a ramp
On a ramp the normal force is $mg\cos\theta$, which is less than the weight. Friction on a ramp is therefore $\mu mg\cos\theta$, not $\mu mg$.
:::

:::summary
- On a ramp, use one axis along the surface and one perpendicular to it, and resolve the weight.
- Along the ramp: $mg\sin\theta$, down the slope. Into the ramp: $mg\cos\theta$, so $F_N = mg\cos\theta$.
- An object at rest on a ramp is held by static friction equal to $mg\sin\theta$. It starts to slip when $\tan\theta$ is greater than $\mu_s$.
- Frictionless ramp: $a = g\sin\theta$. Sliding down with friction: $a = g(\sin\theta - \mu_k\cos\theta)$.
- Friction always opposes the sliding, so it points down the ramp when the object moves up.
:::
