A delivery rider tells you she has ridden 12 km this morning. That says how hard she has worked, but not where she is now. To say where something is, and how its position has changed, a size is not enough: you need a direction as well. This lesson sets up the language for the whole of kinematics, the part of physics that describes motion.

## Scalars and vectors

:::definition Scalar and vector
A **scalar** is a quantity with a size only. Distance, speed, time and mass are scalars.

A **vector** is a quantity with a size and a direction. Displacement, velocity, acceleration and force are vectors.

We need vectors because two movements of the same size can have very different results: 5 km east and 5 km west leave you 10 km apart.
:::

A vector is written with its direction in square brackets: $12\ \text{km}$ [E] is 12 km to the east, and $3\ \text{m/s}$ [S] is 3 m/s to the south. On a diagram, a vector is an arrow. The length of the arrow shows the size, and the arrowhead shows the direction.

## Position and displacement

To describe motion along a straight line, choose a point to be the **origin**, and choose one direction to be **positive**. The opposite direction is then negative.

:::definition Position and displacement
The **position** of an object, $d$, is where it is, measured from the origin. A position of $-20\ \text{m}$ is 20 m from the origin on the negative side.

The **displacement**, $\Delta d$, is the change in position:

$$\Delta d = d_2 - d_1$$

where $d_1$ is the initial position and $d_2$ is the final position. The symbol $\Delta$ (delta) means "the change in". Displacement is a vector, and on a line its sign gives its direction.
:::

:::example A displacement from two positions
On a straight track, right is the positive direction. A skater moves from the position 60 m to the position 25 m. What is the displacement?
@step Subtract the initial position from the final position: $\Delta d = d_2 - d_1 = 25 - 60$.
@step $\Delta d = -35$ m. The minus sign means the displacement points in the negative direction, which is left.
@answer $\Delta d = -35$ m, or 35 m [left].
:::

:::example Finding the final position
North is the positive direction. A hiker starts at the position $-20$ m and has a displacement of 50 m [N]. What is the final position?
@step Rearrange $\Delta d = d_2 - d_1$ to get $d_2 = d_1 + \Delta d$.
@step $d_2 = -20 + 50 = 30$ m.
@answer The hiker ends at the position 30 m, which is 30 m north of the origin.
:::

**Distance** is different. It adds up all the ground covered, whatever the direction, so it is never negative and it never gets smaller.

:::example Turning round
East is the positive direction. A dog runs from the position 10 m to 90 m, then turns round and runs to 40 m. Find the distance travelled and the displacement for the whole trip.
@step Distance: the first part is $90 - 10 = 80$ m and the second part is $90 - 40 = 50$ m, so the distance is $80 + 50 = 130$ m.
@step Displacement: only the first and the last positions matter. $\Delta d = 40 - 10 = 30$ m.
@answer The dog travels 130 m, and its displacement is 30 m [E].
:::

:::mistake Subtracting the wrong way round
Displacement is always **final minus initial**. Moving from 60 m to 25 m gives $25 - 60 = -35$ m, not $+35$ m. If the object moved in the negative direction, your answer must be negative.
:::

## Adding vectors along a line

Two or more vectors can be added to give a single vector that has the same effect. That single vector is called the **resultant**.

:::definition Resultant
The **resultant** is the sum of two or more vectors. For displacements, it is the one displacement that takes you from the starting point straight to the finishing point.

For vectors along one line, choose a positive direction, write each vector with its sign, and add.
:::

:::example Two displacements in opposite directions
A robot drives 12 m [E] and then 5 m [W]. What is the resultant displacement?
@step Take east as positive. The displacements are $+12$ m and $-5$ m.
@step Add them: $12 + (-5) = 7$ m.
@step The answer is positive, so it points east.
@answer 7 m [E].
:::

:::example Three displacements
A boat sails 7 km [N], then 5 km [S], and then 11 km [S]. What is the resultant displacement?
@step Take north as positive: $+7$, $-5$ and $-11$ km.
@step Add them: $7 - 5 - 11 = -9$ km.
@step The answer is negative, so it points south.
@answer 9 km [S].
:::

:::example Finding a missing displacement
A boat sails 5 km [E] and then makes a second move along the same line. Its resultant displacement is 7 km [W]. What was the second displacement?
@step Take east as positive. The first displacement is $+5$ km and the resultant is $-7$ km.
@step resultant = first + second, so second = resultant − first $= -7 - 5 = -12$ km.
@answer 12 km [W]. This makes sense: the boat had to sail back past its starting point.
:::

:::mistake Adding the sizes without their signs
12 m [E] followed by 5 m [W] is not a resultant of 17 m. That is the distance travelled. Vectors in opposite directions partly cancel, so give one direction a minus sign before you add.
:::

:::summary
- A scalar has a size only. A vector has a size and a direction, written like 12 km [E].
- Position, $d$, is measured from an origin, with one direction chosen as positive.
- Displacement is the change in position: $\Delta d = d_2 - d_1$, final minus initial. Its sign gives its direction.
- Distance adds up all the ground covered. Displacement compares only the start and the finish.
- The resultant of vectors along a line is their sum, with their signs.
:::
