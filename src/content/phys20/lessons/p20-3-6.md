A roller coaster car is hauled to the top of the first hill, and after that nothing drives it. It trades height for speed on the way down and speed for height on the way up, again and again. This lesson follows the energy through that trade, first when nothing is lost and then when friction takes a share.

## Conservation of mechanical energy

:::definition Mechanical energy
The **mechanical energy** of an object is the sum of its kinetic energy and its potential energies:

$$E_m = E_k + E_g + E_e$$
:::

:::definition Conservation of mechanical energy
When only gravity and spring forces do work on an object, its mechanical energy stays the same:

$$E_k + E_g + E_e \text{ at the start} = E_k + E_g + E_e \text{ at the end}$$

Energy changes from one form to another, but the total does not change.
:::

The great strength of this law is that the path does not matter. A straight drop, a curved slide and a looping track all give the same speed at the bottom, as long as friction can be ignored and the change in height is the same.

:::example A fall from rest
A diver steps off a platform, from rest, 10 m above the water. Use $g = 9.81\ \text{m/s}^2$ and ignore air resistance. With what speed does the diver reach the water?
@step All the gravitational potential energy at the top becomes kinetic energy at the bottom: $mgh = \dfrac{1}{2}mv^2$.
@step The mass cancels: $v = \sqrt{2gh} = \sqrt{2 \times 9.81 \times 10} = \sqrt{196.2}$.
@answer $v = 14.0$ m/s.
:::

The mass cancelled, so a heavy diver and a light one arrive at the same speed.

:::example A roller coaster
A roller coaster car passes the top of a hill 30 m high at 4 m/s. Ignore friction. How fast is it moving at a point 10 m high?
@step Total mechanical energy is the same at both points: $\dfrac{1}{2}mu^2 + mgh_1 = \dfrac{1}{2}mv^2 + mgh_2$.
@step The mass cancels: $v^2 = u^2 + 2g(h_1 - h_2) = 4^2 + 2 \times 9.81 \times (30 - 10) = 16 + 392.4$.
@step $v = \sqrt{408.4}$.
@answer $v = 20.2$ m/s.
:::

:::example Coasting uphill
A cyclist reaches the bottom of a hill at 12 m/s and coasts up it without pedalling. Ignore friction. How fast is the cyclist moving at a height of 4 m above the bottom?
@step Kinetic energy at the bottom becomes kinetic and gravitational potential energy higher up: $\dfrac{1}{2}mu^2 = \dfrac{1}{2}mv^2 + mgh$.
@step $v^2 = u^2 - 2gh = 12^2 - 2 \times 9.81 \times 4 = 144 - 78.48$.
@answer $v = \sqrt{65.52} = 8.1$ m/s.
:::

:::example A spring launcher
A spring with a spring constant of 500 N/m is compressed by 10 cm and launches a 0.25 kg cart along a level, frictionless track. The cart then rolls up a frictionless hill. Find the speed of the cart as it leaves the spring and the greatest height it reaches.
@step Energy stored in the spring: $E_e = \dfrac{1}{2}kx^2 = \dfrac{1}{2} \times 500 \times 0.1^2 = 2.5$ J.
@step This becomes kinetic energy: $\dfrac{1}{2}mv^2 = 2.5$, so $v = \sqrt{\dfrac{2 \times 2.5}{0.25}} = \sqrt{20}$.
@step At the greatest height it is all gravitational potential energy: $mgh = 2.5$, so $h = \dfrac{2.5}{0.25 \times 9.81}$.
@answer $v = 4.47$ m/s and $h = 1.02$ m.
:::

:::mistake Expecting a heavier object to arrive faster
In $mgh = \dfrac{1}{2}mv^2$ the mass appears on both sides and cancels. Without friction or air resistance, the speed at the bottom depends only on the change in height (and on any speed at the start), not on the mass.
:::

## When friction takes a share

Real slides, slopes and tracks have friction. Friction does negative work on the moving object and changes some of its mechanical energy into **thermal energy**: the surfaces get warmer. The mechanical energy is then no longer conserved, but the books still balance:

$$E_m \text{ at the start} = E_m \text{ at the end} + E_{\text{lost}}$$

The energy "lost" has not been destroyed. It has spread out as thermal energy, where it can no longer be used to move the object. Total energy is always conserved.

:::example Energy lost on a slide
A 30 kg child starts from rest at the top of a slide, 3 m above the bottom, and reaches the bottom at 5 m/s. Use $g = 9.81\ \text{m/s}^2$. How much mechanical energy is changed into thermal energy by friction?
@step At the top: $E_g = mgh = 30 \times 9.81 \times 3 = 882.9$ J.
@step At the bottom: $E_k = \dfrac{1}{2}mv^2 = \dfrac{1}{2} \times 30 \times 5^2 = 375$ J.
@step Energy lost: $882.9 - 375$.
@answer 508 J, to the nearest joule.
:::

:::example The speed at the bottom
A 60 kg skier starts from rest and descends through a height of 40 m. On the way, friction and air resistance change 6000 J of mechanical energy into thermal energy. What is the speed of the skier at the bottom?
@step At the top: $E_g = mgh = 60 \times 9.81 \times 40 = 23\,544$ J.
@step Kinetic energy at the bottom: $23\,544 - 6000 = 17\,544$ J.
@step $v = \sqrt{\dfrac{2E_k}{m}} = \sqrt{\dfrac{2 \times 17\,544}{60}} = \sqrt{584.8}$.
@answer $v = 24.2$ m/s.
:::

The energy lost is the size of the work done by friction, and work is force × distance. So the energy lost, divided by the distance slid, gives the average force of friction.

:::example The force of friction on a ramp
A 20 kg crate starts from rest and slides 10 m down a ramp, dropping through a height of 4 m. It reaches the bottom at 6 m/s. Find the mechanical energy lost to friction and the average force of friction.
@step At the top: $E_g = 20 \times 9.81 \times 4 = 784.8$ J. At the bottom: $E_k = \dfrac{1}{2} \times 20 \times 6^2 = 360$ J.
@step Energy lost: $784.8 - 360 = 424.8$ J.
@step Friction acts along the 10 m of the ramp: $F_f = \dfrac{424.8}{10}$.
@answer 425 J is lost, and the average force of friction is 42.5 N.
:::

Notice which distance goes where. The **height**, 4 m, goes into $mgh$. The **distance along the ramp**, 10 m, goes with the friction force, because that is the distance over which friction acts.

:::mistake Saying that energy has been destroyed
When a sliding object slows down, its mechanical energy has not disappeared. Friction has changed it into thermal energy in the object and the surface. Mechanical energy can decrease, but total energy cannot.
:::

:::summary
- Mechanical energy is the sum of kinetic, gravitational potential and elastic potential energy.
- With no friction, mechanical energy is conserved: the total at the start equals the total at the end.
- From rest through a height $h$: $v = \sqrt{2gh}$, whatever the mass and whatever the path.
- With friction: mechanical energy at the start = mechanical energy at the end + the energy lost.
- The energy lost is the work done by friction: friction force × the distance slid.
:::
