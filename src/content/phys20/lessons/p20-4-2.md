Hang a mass on a spring, pull it down and let go. It bobs up and down at a steady rate, and that rate is the same whether you pulled it down a little or a lot. This lesson finds what sets the rate, and follows the energy as it swaps between the spring and the mass.

## The period of a mass on a spring

Two things decide how quickly a mass on a spring oscillates. A **stiffer spring** pulls back harder, so the oscillation is faster. A **larger mass** is harder to speed up and slow down, so the oscillation is slower.

:::definition Period of a mass on a spring
A mass $m$ on a spring of spring constant $k$ oscillates with a period of

$$T = 2\pi\sqrt{\dfrac{m}{k}}$$

with the mass in kilograms, the spring constant in newtons per metre and the period in seconds.
:::

The amplitude is not in the formula. A larger amplitude means farther to go, but the restoring force is also larger, and the two effects cancel exactly. This is a special property of simple harmonic motion.

:::example The period
A 0.5 kg mass oscillates on a spring with a spring constant of 50 N/m. What is the period?
@step $T = 2\pi\sqrt{\dfrac{m}{k}} = 2\pi\sqrt{\dfrac{0.5}{50}} = 2\pi\sqrt{0.01} = 2\pi \times 0.1$.
@answer $T = 0.63$ s.
:::

:::example Period and frequency, with a mass in grams
A 250 g mass oscillates on a spring with a spring constant of 40 N/m. Find the period and the frequency.
@step Change the units: $m = 250\ \text{g} = 0.25$ kg.
@step $T = 2\pi\sqrt{\dfrac{0.25}{40}} = 2\pi \times 0.07906 = 0.497$ s.
@step $f = \dfrac{1}{T} = \dfrac{1}{0.497}$.
@answer $T = 0.497$ s and $f = 2.01$ Hz.
:::

Timing an oscillation is an accurate way to measure a spring constant or a mass. Square both sides of the formula first:

$$T^2 = \dfrac{4\pi^2 m}{k} \qquad\text{so}\qquad k = \dfrac{4\pi^2 m}{T^2} \qquad\text{and}\qquad m = \dfrac{kT^2}{4\pi^2}$$

:::example The spring constant from a timing
A 0.4 kg mass on a spring makes 20 complete oscillations in 16 s. Find the spring constant.
@step Period: $T = \dfrac{16}{20} = 0.8$ s.
@step $k = \dfrac{4\pi^2 m}{T^2} = \dfrac{4\pi^2 \times 0.4}{0.8^2} = \dfrac{15.79}{0.64}$.
@answer $k = 24.7$ N/m.
:::

:::example The mass for a chosen period
A spring has a spring constant of 100 N/m. What mass must be hung on it for the period to be 1 s?
@step $m = \dfrac{kT^2}{4\pi^2} = \dfrac{100 \times 1^2}{4\pi^2} = \dfrac{100}{39.48}$.
@answer $m = 2.53$ kg.
:::

:::mistake Leaving the mass in grams
The formula needs the mass in kilograms. A 250 g mass is 0.25 kg. Using 250 makes the period about 32 times too long, because $\sqrt{1000} \approx 32$.
:::

## Energy in simple harmonic motion

On a frictionless surface, only the spring does work on the mass, so mechanical energy is conserved. It swaps between two forms.

- At an **extreme** the mass is at rest for an instant. All the energy is elastic potential energy, $\dfrac{1}{2}kA^2$.
- At the **equilibrium position** the spring is neither stretched nor compressed. All the energy is kinetic, and the speed is greatest.
- **Anywhere between**, the energy is shared.

:::definition Energy of a mass on a spring
The total mechanical energy of a mass that oscillates on a spring with amplitude $A$ is

$$E = \dfrac{1}{2}kA^2$$

and at any displacement $x$, where the speed is $v$:

$$\dfrac{1}{2}mv^2 + \dfrac{1}{2}kx^2 = \dfrac{1}{2}kA^2$$
:::

:::example The total energy
A mass oscillates on a spring with a spring constant of 200 N/m, with an amplitude of 10 cm, on a frictionless surface. What is the total mechanical energy?
@step Change the units: $A = 10\ \text{cm} = 0.1$ m.
@step $E = \dfrac{1}{2}kA^2 = \dfrac{1}{2} \times 200 \times 0.1^2$.
@answer $E = 1.00$ J.
:::

:::example The greatest speed
The mass in that oscillation is 0.5 kg. What is its greatest speed?
@step At the equilibrium position all of the 1.00 J is kinetic: $\dfrac{1}{2}mv^2 = 1.00$.
@step $v = \sqrt{\dfrac{2E}{m}} = \sqrt{\dfrac{2 \times 1.00}{0.5}} = \sqrt{4}$.
@answer $v = 2.00$ m/s.
:::

:::example The speed part of the way out
How fast is the same mass moving when it is 6 cm from the equilibrium position?
@step Elastic potential energy at 6 cm: $\dfrac{1}{2}kx^2 = \dfrac{1}{2} \times 200 \times 0.06^2 = 0.36$ J.
@step Kinetic energy: $1.00 - 0.36 = 0.64$ J.
@step $v = \sqrt{\dfrac{2 \times 0.64}{0.5}} = \sqrt{2.56}$.
@answer $v = 1.60$ m/s.
:::

The amplitude is squared in $\dfrac{1}{2}kA^2$. An oscillation with twice the amplitude has four times the energy, and its greatest speed is twice as large.

:::mistake Sharing the energy in proportion to the distance
At 6 cm out of 10 cm, the spring does not hold 60% of the energy. Elastic energy depends on $x^2$, so it holds $\left(\dfrac{6}{10}\right)^2 = 36\%$, and the other 64% is kinetic. Work out $\dfrac{1}{2}kx^2$ and subtract.
:::

:::summary
- Period of a mass on a spring: $T = 2\pi\sqrt{\dfrac{m}{k}}$. It does not depend on the amplitude.
- A larger mass gives a longer period, and a stiffer spring gives a shorter one.
- Rearranged: $k = \dfrac{4\pi^2 m}{T^2}$ and $m = \dfrac{kT^2}{4\pi^2}$.
- Total energy: $E = \dfrac{1}{2}kA^2$, all elastic at the extremes and all kinetic at equilibrium.
- At any point: $\dfrac{1}{2}mv^2 + \dfrac{1}{2}kx^2 = \dfrac{1}{2}kA^2$.
:::
