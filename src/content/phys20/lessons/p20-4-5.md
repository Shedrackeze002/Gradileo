Two balls that meet head-on bounce apart. Two waves that meet head-on do something no solid object can do: they pass straight through each other and carry on as if nothing had happened. This lesson looks at what a wave does when it meets a barrier, and what two waves do while they overlap.

## Reflection and echoes

When a wave reaches the end of its medium, it turns back. This is **reflection**. How it comes back depends on the end.

:::definition Reflection from fixed and free ends
- At a **fixed end**, a pulse is reflected **inverted**: a crest comes back as a trough.
- At a **free end**, a pulse is reflected **the same way up**: a crest comes back as a crest.

In both cases the reflected pulse has the same speed as before, because it is still in the same medium.
:::

Newton's third law explains the fixed end. As the crest arrives, the rope pulls up on the wall, so the wall pulls down on the rope, and that downward pull sends back a trough. A free end is flicked upwards by the crest and sends back a crest.

At a join between a light rope and a heavy rope, both things happen at once. Part of the pulse is transmitted into the heavy rope, and part is reflected, inverted, because the heavy rope acts partly like a fixed end.

An **echo** is a reflected sound. The sound travels to the reflecting surface and back, so in a time $t$ it covers **twice** the distance to the surface:

$$d = \dfrac{vt}{2}$$

:::example The distance to a cliff
The speed of sound in the air is 340 m/s. A hiker shouts towards a cliff and hears the echo 1.5 s later. How far away is the cliff?
@step The sound travels $vt = 340 \times 1.5 = 510$ m, there and back.
@step Distance to the cliff: $\dfrac{510}{2}$.
@answer 255 m.
:::

:::example The time for an echo
The speed of sound in the air is 340 m/s. A student claps in front of a large wall 85 m away. How long after the clap does the student hear the echo?
@step Distance there and back: $2 \times 85 = 170$ m.
@step $t = \dfrac{170}{340}$.
@answer 0.50 s.
:::

Ships use the same idea to measure the depth of the sea, with pulses of sound sent straight down. Bats and dolphins find their prey with it.

:::example Echo sounding
A ship sends a pulse of sound straight down, and the echo from the sea bed returns 80 ms later. The speed of sound in the sea water is 1500 m/s. How deep is the sea there?
@step Change the units: $t = 80\ \text{ms} = 0.080$ s.
@step The pulse travels $1500 \times 0.080 = 120$ m, down and back.
@answer The depth is $\dfrac{120}{2} = 60$ m.
:::

:::mistake Forgetting that an echo travels there and back
Multiplying the speed of sound by the echo time gives the distance for the **round trip**. Halve it to find the distance to the reflecting surface. Going the other way, double the distance before you divide by the speed.
:::

## Superposition and interference

:::definition Principle of superposition
Where two waves overlap, the displacement of the medium is the **sum** of the displacements that each wave would give on its own. After they have passed through each other, each wave carries on unchanged.
:::

Count displacements above the rest position as positive and those below as negative, and add.

:::definition Interference
- **Constructive interference**: the waves displace the medium in the same direction (crest on crest, or trough on trough), and the result is larger than either wave.
- **Destructive interference**: the waves displace the medium in opposite directions (crest on trough), and the result is smaller. Two equal waves cancel completely.
:::

:::example Two crests
Two pulses travel towards each other along a rope. One is a crest 5 cm high and the other a crest 3 cm high. What is the displacement of the rope when they overlap exactly?
@step Both displacements are upwards: $5 + 3$.
@answer 8 cm above the rest position. The interference is constructive.
:::

:::example A crest and a trough
The 5 cm crest now meets a trough 3 cm deep. What is the displacement of the rope when they overlap exactly?
@step The trough is a negative displacement: $5 + (-3)$.
@answer 2 cm above the rest position. The interference is destructive.
:::

Interference explains a strange effect with two loudspeakers that play the same note in step. Walk across the room and the sound is loud in some places and quiet in others. What matters at each place is the **path difference**: how much farther the sound has travelled from one speaker than from the other.

:::definition Path difference
For two sources that vibrate in step:

- a path difference of a **whole number** of wavelengths ($0, \lambda, 2\lambda, \dots$) brings crest to crest: **constructive** interference.
- a path difference of an **odd number of half wavelengths** ($\dfrac{1}{2}\lambda, \dfrac{3}{2}\lambda, \dfrac{5}{2}\lambda, \dots$) brings crest to trough: **destructive** interference.
:::

:::example Loud or quiet?
Two speakers vibrate in step and give out sound of wavelength 0.5 m. A listener is 3 m from one speaker and 4.25 m from the other. Is the sound there loud or quiet?
@step Path difference: $4.25 - 3 = 1.25$ m.
@step In wavelengths: $\dfrac{1.25}{0.5} = 2.5$.
@step Two and a half wavelengths is an odd number of half wavelengths, so crest meets trough.
@answer The interference is destructive, and the sound is quiet.
:::

If the listener moves until the farther speaker is 4.5 m away, the path difference is 1.5 m, exactly 3 wavelengths, and the sound is loud.

:::mistake Thinking that waves which cancel are destroyed
Destructive interference lasts only while the waves overlap. Two pulses that cancel for an instant both reappear on the far side and carry on unchanged. The energy has not vanished: at that instant it is in the motion of the rope.
:::

:::summary
- A fixed end reflects a pulse inverted. A free end reflects it the same way up.
- An echo travels there and back: the distance to the surface is $\dfrac{vt}{2}$.
- Principle of superposition: where waves overlap, add their displacements.
- Crest on crest is constructive interference, and crest on trough is destructive.
- For sources in step, a path difference of whole wavelengths is constructive, and an odd number of half wavelengths is destructive.
:::
