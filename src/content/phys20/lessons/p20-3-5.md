Work done on an object does not vanish. Push a trolley and it ends up moving. Lift a box and it ends up higher. Squeeze a spring and it ends up ready to spring back. In each case the work has become **energy**, the ability to do work in turn. This lesson gives a formula to each of the three kinds.

## Kinetic and gravitational potential energy

:::definition Kinetic energy
The **kinetic energy**, $E_k$, of an object is the energy it has because it is moving:

$$E_k = \dfrac{1}{2}mv^2$$

with the mass in kilograms, the speed in metres per second and the energy in joules.
:::

:::definition Gravitational potential energy
The **gravitational potential energy**, $E_g$, of an object is the energy it has because of its height:

$$E_g = mgh$$

The height $h$ is measured from a reference level that you choose, usually the ground or the lowest point of the motion.
:::

:::example Kinetic energy
A 1200 kg car moves at 20 m/s. What is its kinetic energy?
@step $E_k = \dfrac{1}{2}mv^2 = \dfrac{1}{2} \times 1200 \times 20^2 = 600 \times 400$.
@answer $E_k = 240\,000$ J, or 240 kJ.
:::

:::example Gravitational potential energy
A 4 kg flowerpot is on a balcony 6 m above the ground. Use $g = 9.81\ \text{m/s}^2$. What is its gravitational potential energy relative to the ground?
@step $E_g = mgh = 4 \times 9.81 \times 6$.
@answer $E_g = 235.4$ J.
:::

The link between work and kinetic energy is exact.

:::definition Work-energy theorem
The net work done on an object equals the change in its kinetic energy:

$$W_{\text{net}} = \Delta E_k = \dfrac{1}{2}mv^2 - \dfrac{1}{2}mu^2$$

Positive net work speeds the object up, and negative net work slows it down.
:::

:::example Speeding up
A net force of 30 N acts on a 5 kg cart in the direction of its motion, through 4 m. The cart is moving at 2 m/s at the start. What is its speed at the end?
@step Net work: $W = Fd = 30 \times 4 = 120$ J.
@step Kinetic energy at the start: $\dfrac{1}{2} \times 5 \times 2^2 = 10$ J. At the end: $10 + 120 = 130$ J.
@step $v = \sqrt{\dfrac{2E_k}{m}} = \sqrt{\dfrac{2 \times 130}{5}} = \sqrt{52}$.
@answer $v = 7.21$ m/s.
:::

:::example Braking
A 1500 kg car travelling at 20 m/s brakes and comes to rest in 40 m. Find the change in its kinetic energy and the size of the average net force on it.
@step At the start: $E_k = \dfrac{1}{2} \times 1500 \times 20^2 = 300\,000$ J. At the end it is zero.
@step Change: $\Delta E_k = 0 - 300\,000 = -300\,000$ J, or $-300$ kJ.
@step The size of the net work is 300 000 J, and $W = Fd$, so $F = \dfrac{300\,000}{40}$.
@answer $\Delta E_k = -300$ kJ, and the average net force is 7500 N, opposite to the motion.
:::

The speed is squared in $E_k = \dfrac{1}{2}mv^2$. A car at twice the speed has four times the kinetic energy, and with the same braking force it needs four times the distance to stop.

:::mistake Doubling the kinetic energy when the speed doubles
Kinetic energy is proportional to the **square** of the speed. At twice the speed it is four times as large, and at three times the speed it is nine times as large. Square the speed before you multiply by the mass.
:::

## Springs and elastic energy

Pull on a spring and it pulls back. The more you stretch it, the harder it pulls.

:::definition Hooke's law
The force needed to stretch or compress a spring is proportional to the change in its length:

$$F = kx$$

$x$ is the **extension** or compression, measured from the natural length of the spring, in metres. $k$ is the **spring constant**, in newtons per metre (N/m). A stiff spring has a large $k$.
:::

:::example Hooke's law
A spring has a spring constant of 250 N/m. What force is needed to stretch it by 0.1 m?
@step $F = kx = 250 \times 0.1$.
@answer $F = 25$ N.
:::

The force is not steady: it rises from zero to $kx$ as the spring stretches. Its force-displacement graph is a straight line from the origin, and the work done is the area of the triangle beneath it, $\dfrac{1}{2} \times x \times kx$. That work is stored in the spring.

:::definition Elastic potential energy
The **elastic potential energy**, $E_e$, stored in a spring that is stretched or compressed by $x$ is

$$E_e = \dfrac{1}{2}kx^2$$
:::

:::example Energy stored in a spring
A spring with a spring constant of 400 N/m is compressed by 5 cm. How much elastic potential energy does it store?
@step Change the units: $x = 5\ \text{cm} = 0.05$ m.
@step $E_e = \dfrac{1}{2}kx^2 = \dfrac{1}{2} \times 400 \times 0.05^2 = 200 \times 0.0025$.
@answer $E_e = 0.5$ J.
:::

A mass hung from a spring gives a simple way to measure the spring constant. When the mass hangs at rest, the upward force of the spring balances the weight: $kx = mg$.

:::example A mass on a spring
When a 1.5 kg mass is hung from a spring and comes to rest, the spring is 12.5 cm longer than before. Use $g = 9.81\ \text{m/s}^2$. Find the spring constant and the elastic potential energy stored in the spring.
@step The spring force equals the weight: $F = mg = 1.5 \times 9.81 = 14.715$ N, and $x = 0.125$ m.
@step $k = \dfrac{F}{x} = \dfrac{14.715}{0.125} = 117.72$ N/m.
@step $E_e = \dfrac{1}{2}kx^2 = \dfrac{1}{2} \times 117.72 \times 0.125^2$.
@answer $k = 117.7$ N/m and $E_e = 0.920$ J.
:::

:::mistake Leaving the extension in centimetres
The spring constant is in newtons per **metre**, so $x$ must be in metres. A compression of 5 cm is 0.05 m. Using 5 in $\dfrac{1}{2}kx^2$ gives an answer ten thousand times too large, because the extension is squared.
:::

:::summary
- Kinetic energy: $E_k = \dfrac{1}{2}mv^2$. Twice the speed gives four times the kinetic energy.
- Gravitational potential energy: $E_g = mgh$, with $h$ measured from a chosen reference level.
- Work-energy theorem: $W_{\text{net}} = \Delta E_k$.
- Hooke's law: $F = kx$, with $x$ in metres and $k$ in N/m.
- Elastic potential energy: $E_e = \dfrac{1}{2}kx^2$.
:::
