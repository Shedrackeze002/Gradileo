Sit on a moving train and the person beside you is not moving at all, as far as you can tell. To someone standing on the platform, both of you are rushing past at 100 km/h. Neither view is wrong. A velocity is always measured relative to something, and this lesson shows how to change from one point of view to another.

## Velocities along one line

:::definition Relative velocity and frame of reference
A **frame of reference** is the thing a motion is measured against: the ground, a train, the water in a river.

A **relative velocity** is a velocity measured in a stated frame of reference. To change frames, add the velocities as vectors:

$$\vec{v}_{\text{object, ground}} = \vec{v}_{\text{object, carrier}} + \vec{v}_{\text{carrier, ground}}$$

The "carrier" is whatever the object is moving on or in, such as a train, a moving walkway, the water or the air.
:::

When everything moves along one line, choose a positive direction and add the velocities with their signs.

:::example Walking along a moving train
A train travels east at 27 m/s. A passenger walks towards the front of the train at 1.5 m/s relative to the train. What is the passenger's velocity relative to the ground?
@step Take east as positive. The passenger moves at $+1.5$ m/s relative to the train, and the train moves at $+27$ m/s relative to the ground.
@step Add: $1.5 + 27 = 28.5$ m/s.
@answer 28.5 m/s [E]. Walking towards the back of the train instead gives $-1.5 + 27 = 25.5$ m/s [E].
:::

A boat in a river works the same way. The boat's engine gives it a velocity relative to the **water**, and the water carries the boat along with it.

:::example Downstream and upstream
A boat moves at 9 m/s relative to the water in a river that flows at 5 m/s. How long does the boat take to travel 840 m, measured along the bank, when it heads downstream? And when it heads upstream?
@step Downstream, the current adds: $9 + 5 = 14$ m/s relative to the bank. Time: $\dfrac{840}{14} = 60$ s.
@step Upstream, the current takes away: $9 - 5 = 4$ m/s relative to the bank. Time: $\dfrac{840}{4} = 210$ s.
@answer 60 s downstream and 210 s upstream.
:::

The velocity of one moving object as seen from another is the **difference** between their velocities.

:::example Catching up
A car travels east at 28 m/s and is 75 m behind a truck that travels east at 23 m/s. Find the car's velocity relative to the truck, and the time the car takes to draw level.
@step Relative velocity: $28 - 23 = 5$ m/s east. Seen from the truck, the car approaches at only 5 m/s.
@step The gap of 75 m closes at 5 m/s: $t = \dfrac{75}{5}$.
@answer The car moves at 5 m/s [E] relative to the truck and draws level after 15 s.
:::

:::mistake Using the speed through the water as the speed along the bank
A boat's speedometer reads its speed relative to the water. A distance marked along the bank needs the velocity relative to the bank, which includes the current.
:::

## Crossing a current

Now the boat points straight across the river while the current flows along it. The two velocities are at right angles, so they add like the two shorter sides of a right triangle.

:::example Speed relative to the bank
A boat points straight across a river and moves at 8 m/s relative to the water. The river flows at 6 m/s. What is the boat's speed relative to the bank, and in what direction does it travel?
@step The velocities are at right angles: $v = \sqrt{8^2 + 6^2} = \sqrt{100} = 10$ m/s.
@step Direction: $\tan\theta = \dfrac{6}{8}$, so $\theta = 36.9°$ downstream from straight across.
@answer 10 m/s, at 36.9° downstream from straight across.
:::

The two motions are **independent**. The current moves the boat along the river but does nothing to carry it across. So the crossing time depends only on the width of the river and the boat's speed across it.

:::example Crossing time and drift
A river is 100 m wide and flows at 1.5 m/s. A boat points straight across and moves at 5 m/s relative to the water. Find the time it takes to cross, and how far downstream it lands.
@step Across the river, only the boat's own speed counts: $t = \dfrac{100}{5} = 20$ s.
@step During those 20 s the current carries the boat downstream: $1.5 \times 20 = 30$ m.
@answer The boat crosses in 20 s and lands 30 m downstream.
:::

To land directly opposite the starting point, the boat must point partly upstream, so that part of its velocity cancels the current. Its **heading** is the direction in which it points.

:::example The heading that goes straight across
A river flows at 3 m/s. A boat that moves at 5 m/s relative to the water must travel straight across. At what angle upstream must it point, and what is its speed relative to the bank?
@step The boat's velocity relative to the water is now the hypotenuse of the triangle. Its upstream component must equal the current: $5\sin\theta = 3$.
@step $\sin\theta = \dfrac{3}{5}$, so $\theta = 36.9°$ upstream from straight across.
@step The component left over carries the boat across: $v = \sqrt{5^2 - 3^2} = \sqrt{16} = 4$ m/s.
@answer The boat points 36.9° upstream and crosses at 4 m/s relative to the bank.
:::

Going straight across is slower. This boat crosses a 100 m river in $\dfrac{100}{4} = 25$ s, against 20 s when it points straight across and accepts the drift.

:::mistake Using the resultant speed for the crossing time
In the first example the boat moves at 10 m/s relative to the bank, but it travels along a slanted path that is longer than the river is wide. For the crossing time, divide the width of the river by the component of the velocity that points across.
:::

:::summary
- Every velocity is measured relative to a frame of reference.
- Velocity relative to the ground = velocity relative to the carrier + velocity of the carrier, added as vectors.
- Along one line: downstream the current adds, and upstream it takes away. Seen from another moving object, use the difference of the velocities.
- A boat that points straight across a current: the speed relative to the bank is $\sqrt{v_{\text{boat}}^2 + v_{\text{current}}^2}$, the crossing time is the width ÷ the boat's speed, and the drift is the current × the time.
- To travel straight across, point upstream with $\sin\theta = \dfrac{v_{\text{current}}}{v_{\text{boat}}}$.
:::
