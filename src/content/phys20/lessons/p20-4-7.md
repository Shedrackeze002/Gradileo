Stand by a road as an ambulance goes past with its siren on. The note is high as the ambulance comes towards you and drops suddenly as it passes. The siren has not changed. What has changed is how often its waves reach your ear.

## The Doppler effect

:::definition Doppler effect
The **Doppler effect** is the change in the frequency that an observer receives when a source of waves and the observer move towards or away from each other.

- Moving **closer together**: the frequency heard is **higher** than the true frequency.
- Moving **apart**: the frequency heard is **lower** than the true frequency.
:::

Picture a source that sends out one wave crest every period. If the source stands still, the crests spread out as evenly spaced circles. If the source moves, it has moved forward a little before it sends the next crest. In front of the source the crests are **squeezed together**, so the wavelength is shorter. Behind it they are **stretched apart**, so the wavelength is longer.

The speed of the waves does not change. It is set by the air, not by the motion of the source. So from $f = \dfrac{v}{\lambda}$, a shorter wavelength in front means a higher frequency for an observer there, and a longer wavelength behind means a lower one.

Three points are worth keeping straight.

- The source gives out the same frequency all the time. Only the frequency **received** changes.
- What matters is whether the distance between source and observer is shrinking or growing. A driver and a passenger in the same car hear the true note of their own horn.
- An observer who moves towards a source at rest also hears a higher frequency, because the observer meets the crests more often.

The effect is true of every kind of wave, which makes it useful.

| Use | What is measured |
|---|---|
| radar speed check | the frequency shift of waves reflected from a moving car gives its speed |
| ultrasound scan | the shift of waves reflected from blood cells gives the speed of blood flow |
| astronomy | light from a star or galaxy that moves away is shifted to lower frequencies (a **red shift**) |

When a source moves as fast as its own waves, the crests in front can no longer get away, and they pile up into a single strong pulse called a shock wave. For an aircraft that flies faster than sound, this reaches the ground as a **sonic boom**.

:::mistake Saying that the pitch rises steadily as the source comes nearer
A source that approaches at a steady speed is heard at one steady, raised pitch. The sound gets **louder** as it comes nearer, but the pitch does not keep climbing. The pitch changes at the moment the source passes, from the raised note to the lowered one.
:::

## Doppler calculations

For a source that moves at a speed $v_s$ straight towards or away from an observer who is at rest, with sound travelling at a speed $v$:

:::definition Doppler formula for a moving source
$$f_{\text{heard}} = f \times \dfrac{v}{v - v_s} \quad \text{(source approaching)}$$

$$f_{\text{heard}} = f \times \dfrac{v}{v + v_s} \quad \text{(source moving away)}$$

$f$ is the true frequency of the source.
:::

There is an easy way to remember which sign to use. An approaching source must give a **higher** frequency, so the fraction must be larger than 1, and that needs the smaller number, $v - v_s$, underneath.

:::example An approaching siren
The speed of sound in the air is 340 m/s. An ambulance sounds a siren of frequency 500 Hz and moves at 25 m/s directly towards a person standing by the road. What frequency does the person hear?
@step The source is approaching: $f_{\text{heard}} = f \times \dfrac{v}{v - v_s} = 500 \times \dfrac{340}{340 - 25}$.
@step $500 \times \dfrac{340}{315} = 500 \times 1.0794$.
@answer 539.7 Hz, higher than the true 500 Hz.
:::

:::example The siren moving away
The ambulance passes the person and moves directly away at the same speed. What frequency does the person hear now?
@step The source is moving away: $f_{\text{heard}} = f \times \dfrac{v}{v + v_s} = 500 \times \dfrac{340}{340 + 25}$.
@step $500 \times \dfrac{340}{365} = 500 \times 0.9315$.
@answer 465.8 Hz. As the ambulance passes, the note drops by about 74 Hz.
:::

Run backwards, the formula gives the speed of the source from the frequency heard. This is how a speed check works.

:::example The speed of a source
The speed of sound in the air is 340 m/s. A car sounds a horn of frequency 600 Hz as it drives directly towards a person, who hears 650 Hz. How fast is the car moving?
@step From $f_{\text{heard}} = f \times \dfrac{v}{v - v_s}$: $v - v_s = \dfrac{fv}{f_{\text{heard}}} = \dfrac{600 \times 340}{650} = 313.85$ m/s.
@step $v_s = 340 - 313.85$.
@answer $v_s = 26.2$ m/s.
:::

:::mistake Using the wrong sign
Before you calculate, decide whether the answer should be higher or lower than the true frequency. Approaching means higher, so subtract the speed of the source underneath. Moving away means lower, so add it. If your answer goes the wrong way, you have the wrong sign.
:::

:::summary
- The Doppler effect is the change in the frequency received when a source and an observer move towards or away from each other.
- Moving closer raises the frequency heard, and moving apart lowers it.
- In front of a moving source the wavelength is shorter. The speed of the waves does not change.
- Source approaching: $f_{\text{heard}} = f \times \dfrac{v}{v - v_s}$. Source moving away: $f_{\text{heard}} = f \times \dfrac{v}{v + v_s}$.
- Radar speed checks, ultrasound scans of blood flow and the red shift of galaxies all use the effect.
:::
