Hold one coin at the edge of a table and flick a second coin off the edge sideways at the same moment that you drop the first. Listen. They hit the floor together, even though one fell straight down and the other flew across the room. That single click is the key to the motion of every thrown, kicked or launched object.

## Two motions at once

:::definition Projectile
A **projectile** is an object that moves through the air with gravity as the only force acting on it. Its curved path is called its **trajectory**.

The horizontal and vertical parts of the motion are **independent**:

- **Horizontally** there is no force, so the horizontal velocity stays constant: $d_x = v_x t$.
- **Vertically** the projectile is in free fall, with an acceleration of $g = 9.81\ \text{m/s}^2$ downwards.

The one quantity the two motions share is the **time**.
:::

We ignore air resistance throughout this lesson. The plan is always the same: split the problem into a horizontal part and a vertical part, use the vertical part to find the time, and use that time in the horizontal part.

## Launched horizontally

An object launched horizontally starts with no vertical velocity. Vertically it behaves exactly like an object dropped from rest, which is why the two coins land together.

:::example Time in the air and range
A package slides off a flat roof at 14 m/s, 20 m above the ground. Use $g = 9.81\ \text{m/s}^2$. Find the time it is in the air and how far from the foot of the building it lands.
@step Vertical: the package falls 20 m from rest. $d = \tfrac12gt^2$ gives $t = \sqrt{\dfrac{2 \times 20}{9.81}} = 2.02$ s.
@step Horizontal: the velocity stays at 14 m/s for the whole of that time. $d_x = v_x t = 14 \times 2.02 = 28.3$ m.
@answer The package is in the air for 2.02 s and lands 28.3 m from the foot of the building.
:::

The horizontal distance a projectile covers is called its **range**, and the time it spends in the air is its **time of flight**. Notice that the time of flight depends only on the height. A faster launch from the same roof lands further away, but at the same moment.

Just before landing, the projectile has two velocity components: the unchanged horizontal one, and the vertical one that gravity has built up. The landing velocity is their resultant.

:::example The landing velocity
A stone is thrown horizontally from a cliff at 6 m/s, 30 m above the sea. Use $g = 9.81\ \text{m/s}^2$. Find its speed just before it lands, and the angle its velocity makes below the horizontal.
@step Horizontal component: still 6 m/s.
@step Vertical component: $v_y^2 = 2gd = 2 \times 9.81 \times 30 = 588.6$, so $v_y = 24.26$ m/s.
@step Speed: $v = \sqrt{6^2 + 24.26^2} = 25.0$ m/s.
@step Angle: $\theta = \tan^{-1}\left(\dfrac{24.26}{6}\right) = 76.1°$ below the horizontal.
@answer 25.0 m/s at 76.1° below the horizontal.
:::

:::mistake Putting the launch speed into the vertical motion
The 14 m/s of the package is a horizontal velocity. Vertically the package starts from rest, so $u = 0$ in the vertical equations. Keep a separate list of horizontal and vertical quantities, and never mix them in one equation.
:::

## Launched at an angle

A ball kicked upwards at an angle has a velocity with both a horizontal and a vertical part from the start. The first step is always to resolve the launch velocity into components:

$$v_x = v\cos\theta \qquad v_y = v\sin\theta$$

After that, the horizontal part is uniform motion at $v_x$, and the vertical part is the motion of a ball thrown straight up at $v_y$.

:::

:::example Components of the launch velocity
A ball is kicked from level ground at 20 m/s, at 35° above the horizontal. Find the horizontal and vertical components of its initial velocity.
@step Horizontal: $v_x = 20\cos 35° = 16.38$ m/s.
@step Vertical: $v_y = 20\sin 35° = 11.47$ m/s.
@answer 16.4 m/s horizontally and 11.5 m/s vertically.
:::

:::example Time of flight and greatest height
For the same ball, use $g = 9.81\ \text{m/s}^2$ to find the time it is in the air and the greatest height it reaches.
@step At the top, the vertical velocity is zero. Time to the top: $t = \dfrac{v_y}{g} = \dfrac{11.47}{9.81} = 1.169$ s.
@step On level ground the trip down takes as long as the trip up, so the time of flight is $2 \times 1.169 = 2.34$ s.
@step Greatest height: $0 = v_y^2 - 2gd$, so $d = \dfrac{11.47^2}{2 \times 9.81} = 6.7$ m.
@answer The ball is in the air for 2.34 s and rises to 6.7 m.
:::

:::example The range
How far away does the same ball land?
@step The horizontal velocity is constant at 16.38 m/s for the whole time of flight, 2.339 s.
@step Range $= v_x t = 16.38 \times 2.339 = 38.3$ m.
@answer The ball lands 38.3 m away.
:::

At the highest point the ball is not at rest. Its vertical velocity is zero, but it is still moving horizontally at 16.38 m/s, and its acceleration is still $9.81\ \text{m/s}^2$ downwards.

:::mistake Using the whole launch speed in one direction
For a ball kicked at 20 m/s and 35°, neither motion uses 20 m/s. The horizontal motion uses $20\cos 35°$ and the vertical motion uses $20\sin 35°$.
:::

:::mistake Rounding too early
Keep at least four significant digits in $v_x$, $v_y$ and the time while you work, and round only the final answer. Rounding each step can change the last digit of the range.
:::

:::summary
- A projectile moves with gravity as the only force. Its horizontal and vertical motions are independent and share only the time.
- Horizontally the velocity is constant: $d_x = v_x t$. Vertically the motion is free fall.
- Launched horizontally: vertically it starts from rest, so the time of flight comes from $d = \tfrac12gt^2$, and the range is $v_x t$.
- Launched at an angle: $v_x = v\cos\theta$ and $v_y = v\sin\theta$. On level ground, the time of flight is $\dfrac{2v_y}{g}$ and the greatest height is $\dfrac{v_y^2}{2g}$.
- The landing velocity is the resultant of the horizontal and vertical components.
:::
