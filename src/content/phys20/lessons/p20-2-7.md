The force that pulls an apple to the ground and the force that holds the Moon in its orbit look like very different things. Newton's boldest idea was that they are the same force, obeying one rule, and that this rule holds between any two objects anywhere in the universe.

## Newton's law of universal gravitation

:::definition Universal gravitation
Every object attracts every other object with a gravitational force. For two objects of masses $m_1$ and $m_2$ whose centres are a distance $r$ apart:

$$F_g = \dfrac{Gm_1m_2}{r^2}$$

$G$ is the **universal gravitational constant**: $G = 6.67 \times 10^{-11}\ \text{N·m}^2/\text{kg}^2$. The force is in newtons when the masses are in kilograms and the distance is in metres.
:::

Three things are worth noticing. The force acts on **both** objects, equal in size and opposite in direction, as the third law requires. The distance $r$ is measured between the **centres** of the objects, not between their surfaces. And $G$ is a very small number, so gravity is noticeable only when at least one of the masses is enormous.

:::example Two spheres in a laboratory
Two spheres of mass 50 kg and 150 kg have their centres 0.5 m apart. Use $G = 6.67 \times 10^{-11}\ \text{N·m}^2/\text{kg}^2$. What is the gravitational force between them?
@step $F_g = \dfrac{Gm_1m_2}{r^2} = \dfrac{6.67 \times 10^{-11} \times 50 \times 150}{0.5^2}$.
@step Top: $6.67 \times 10^{-11} \times 7500 = 5.00 \times 10^{-7}$. Bottom: $0.5^2 = 0.25$.
@answer $F_g = 2.00 \times 10^{-6}$ N, about the weight of a grain of sand. This is why you never feel the pull of the objects around you.
:::

:::example A planet and a satellite
A planet has a mass of $6.0 \times 10^{24}$ kg. A satellite of mass 800 kg orbits with its centre $7.0 \times 10^{6}$ m from the centre of the planet. What is the gravitational force between them?
@step $F_g = \dfrac{6.67 \times 10^{-11} \times 6.0 \times 10^{24} \times 800}{(7.0 \times 10^{6})^2}$.
@step Top: $6.67 \times 6.0 \times 800 = 32\,016$, and $10^{-11} \times 10^{24} = 10^{13}$, giving $3.2016 \times 10^{17}$.
@step Bottom: $(7.0 \times 10^{6})^2 = 4.9 \times 10^{13}$.
@answer $F_g = 6.53 \times 10^{3}$ N.
:::

On a calculator, enter each number in scientific notation with the exponent key, and put the whole of the bottom line in brackets or divide by $r$ twice.

A height above the ground is not the distance $r$. Add the radius of the planet first.

:::example A satellite at a height above the surface
A planet has a mass of $6.0 \times 10^{24}$ kg and a radius of $6.4 \times 10^{6}$ m. An 800 kg satellite orbits at a height of $6.0 \times 10^{5}$ m above the surface. What is the gravitational force on the satellite?
@step Distance from the centre: $r = 6.4 \times 10^{6} + 6.0 \times 10^{5} = 6.4 \times 10^{6} + 0.6 \times 10^{6} = 7.0 \times 10^{6}$ m.
@step This is the same $r$ as in the last example, so $F_g = \dfrac{6.67 \times 10^{-11} \times 6.0 \times 10^{24} \times 800}{(7.0 \times 10^{6})^2}$.
@answer $F_g = 6.53 \times 10^{3}$ N.
:::

:::mistake Forgetting to square the distance
The bottom line is $r^2$. In the planet example, dividing by $7.0 \times 10^{6}$ in place of $4.9 \times 10^{13}$ gives a force millions of times too large.
:::

:::mistake Measuring from the surface
A satellite 600 km above a planet is not 600 km from the planet as far as gravity is concerned. Gravity acts as if all the mass of a planet were at its centre, so $r$ is the radius plus the height.
:::

## The inverse square law

Often you do not need the constants at all. The formula tells you how the force **changes**.

:::definition Inverse square law
The gravitational force is proportional to each mass and inversely proportional to the square of the distance between the centres:

$$F_g \propto m_1 \qquad F_g \propto m_2 \qquad F_g \propto \dfrac{1}{r^2}$$

A quantity that falls with the square of the distance is said to follow an **inverse square law**.
:::

| Change | Effect on the force |
|---|---|
| one mass doubled | force doubled |
| both masses doubled | force four times as large |
| distance doubled | force one quarter as large |
| distance tripled | force one ninth as large |
| distance halved | force four times as large |

:::example Changing the distance
The gravitational force between two objects is 144 N. The distance between their centres is made three times as large, and the masses stay the same. What is the force now?
@step The force is proportional to $\dfrac{1}{r^2}$, so multiplying $r$ by 3 divides the force by $3^2 = 9$.
@step $F = \dfrac{144}{9}$.
@answer 16 N.
:::

:::example Changing a mass and the distance
The gravitational force between two objects is 144 N. The mass of one object is made three times as large, and the distance between their centres is doubled. What is the force now?
@step The larger mass multiplies the force by 3.
@step Doubling the distance divides the force by $2^2 = 4$.
@step $F = 144 \times \dfrac{3}{4}$.
@answer 108 N.
:::

:::mistake Halving the force when the distance doubles
Doubling the distance does not halve the force. The distance is squared, so the force falls to one **quarter**. Square the factor by which the distance changes before you divide.
:::

:::summary
- Every two masses attract each other: $F_g = \dfrac{Gm_1m_2}{r^2}$, with $G = 6.67 \times 10^{-11}\ \text{N·m}^2/\text{kg}^2$.
- The two objects pull on each other with forces of equal size.
- $r$ is measured between the centres. For a satellite, add its height to the radius of the planet.
- The force is proportional to each mass and to $\dfrac{1}{r^2}$: doubling the distance gives one quarter of the force.
- To find how a force changes, multiply by the mass factors and divide by the square of the distance factor.
:::
