In everyday speech, "work" is anything that tires you out. In physics the word has a narrower meaning. A force does work only when the object it acts on moves, and only the part of the force along the motion counts.

## Work

:::definition Work
The **work** done by a constant force $F$ on an object that moves through a displacement $d$ is

$$W = Fd\cos\theta$$

where $\theta$ is the angle between the force and the displacement. Work is measured in **joules** (J): 1 J = 1 N·m. Work is a scalar. It has no direction, but it can be positive, negative or zero.
:::

When the force points along the motion, $\theta = 0°$ and $\cos\theta = 1$, so the formula becomes $W = Fd$.

:::example A force along the motion
A worker pushes a trolley with a steady force of 150 N in the direction of motion, through 12 m. How much work does the worker do?
@step $W = Fd = 150 \times 12$.
@answer $W = 1800$ J.
:::

:::example A force at an angle
A rope pulls a sled along level ground with a force of 80 N at 30° above the horizontal. How much work does the rope do as the sled moves 10 m?
@step Only the component along the ground, $F\cos\theta$, does work.
@step $W = Fd\cos\theta = 80 \times 10 \times \cos 30° = 800 \times 0.8660$.
@answer $W = 693$ J.
:::

The sign of the work tells you which way the energy goes.

| Angle between force and displacement | Work | Example |
|---|---|---|
| less than 90° | positive | a push that speeds a trolley up |
| exactly 90° | zero | the normal force on a sliding box |
| more than 90° | negative | friction on a sliding box |

A force does **no work** when nothing moves, or when the force is at right angles to the motion. You do no work on a heavy bag while you hold it still, however tired your arm becomes. Gravity does no work on a satellite in a circular orbit, because the pull is always at right angles to the motion.

Friction points opposite to the displacement, so $\theta = 180°$ and $\cos\theta = -1$. Its work is negative: it takes energy away from the object. When several forces act, the **net work** is the sum of the work done by each force.

:::example Work done by friction, and net work
A horizontal push of 140 N moves a 25 kg crate 4 m across a level floor. The coefficient of kinetic friction is 0.4. Use $g = 9.81\ \text{m/s}^2$. Find the work done by the push, the work done by friction and the net work.
@step Push: $W = Fd = 140 \times 4 = 560$ J.
@step Friction force: $F_f = \mu_k mg = 0.4 \times 25 \times 9.81 = 98.1$ N.
@step Work done by friction: $W = -F_f d = -98.1 \times 4 = -392.4$ J.
@step Net work: $560 - 392.4$.
@answer 560 J by the push, $-392.4$ J by friction, and a net work of 167.6 J.
:::

:::mistake Counting the whole of a force that is at an angle
For a force at an angle, do not multiply the whole force by the displacement. The rope in the example pulls with 80 N, but only $80\cos 30° = 69.3$ N of that is along the ground. The upward part does no work, because the sled does not move upwards.
:::

## Force-displacement graphs

$W = Fd$ works for a steady force. Many forces change as the object moves: the pull of a spring grows as it stretches, and the push of an engine falls as a car speeds up. For these, draw a graph of force against displacement.

For a steady force the graph is a horizontal line, and $F \times d$ is the area of the rectangle beneath it. The same idea holds for any shape of graph.

:::definition Work from a force-displacement graph
The work done by a force is the **area under its force-displacement graph**. A force in newtons multiplied by a displacement in metres gives an area in joules.
:::

:::example A rectangle and a triangle
The graph shows the force on a cart, in the direction of its motion. The force is steady for the first 4 m and then falls to zero. How much work does it do over the whole 8 m?
@step Rectangle, from 0 m to 4 m: $40 \times 4 = 160$ J.
@step Triangle, from 4 m to 8 m: $\dfrac{1}{2} \times 4 \times 40 = 80$ J.
@answer $W = 160 + 80 = 240$ J.
:::

:::example Three parts, and the average force
The second graph shows another force on the cart. Find the work it does over the whole 10 m, and the average force over that displacement.
@step Trapezoid, from 0 m to 2 m: $\dfrac{1}{2} \times (20 + 60) \times 2 = 80$ J.
@step Rectangle, from 2 m to 6 m: $60 \times 4 = 240$ J.
@step Triangle, from 6 m to 10 m: $\dfrac{1}{2} \times 4 \times 60 = 120$ J.
@step Work: $80 + 240 + 120 = 440$ J.
@step The average force is the steady force that would do the same work over the same displacement: $\dfrac{440}{10}$.
@answer $W = 440$ J, and the average force is 44 N.
:::

A force that rises steadily from zero gives a triangle, with an area of half the final force multiplied by the displacement. This is the case of a spring, and it is where the formula for the energy stored in a spring comes from in the next lesson.

:::mistake Multiplying the largest force by the whole displacement
In the second graph, $60 \times 10 = 600$ J is the area of a rectangle that the graph does not fill. The force is 60 N for only part of the way. Split the area into simple shapes and add them.
:::

:::summary
- Work: $W = Fd\cos\theta$, in joules, where $\theta$ is the angle between the force and the displacement.
- A force along the motion does positive work, a force opposite to it does negative work, and a force at right angles does none.
- Friction does negative work: $W = -F_f d$.
- Net work is the sum of the work done by all the forces.
- For a force that changes, the work is the area under the force-displacement graph.
:::
