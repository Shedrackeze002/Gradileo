For nearly three hundred years the most accurate clocks in the world were regulated by a weight swinging on a rod. A pendulum keeps time so well because its period depends on almost nothing: not on the mass of the bob, and not on how wide it swings, as long as the swing is small.

## The period of a pendulum

A **simple pendulum** is a small, heavy bob on a light string. Pull the bob to one side and let go. Its weight has a component along the path of the swing that pulls it back towards the lowest point. This component is the restoring force. For small angles it is proportional to the displacement, so the motion is simple harmonic.

:::definition Period of a simple pendulum
A simple pendulum of length $L$ swings with a period of

$$T = 2\pi\sqrt{\dfrac{L}{g}}$$

for small swings, where $g$ is the acceleration due to gravity. $L$ is measured from the pivot to the centre of the bob, in metres.
:::

Look at what the formula does **not** contain. There is no mass: a heavier bob has more weight to pull it back, but it also has more inertia, and the two cancel. There is no amplitude either, provided the angle of swing stays small (less than about 15°).

:::example The period
A simple pendulum is 1 m long. Use $g = 9.81\ \text{m/s}^2$. What is its period?
@step $T = 2\pi\sqrt{\dfrac{L}{g}} = 2\pi\sqrt{\dfrac{1}{9.81}} = 2\pi \times 0.3193$.
@answer $T = 2.01$ s.
:::

:::example The length for a chosen period
How long must a simple pendulum be for its period to be 1.5 s? Use $g = 9.81\ \text{m/s}^2$.
@step Square the formula: $T^2 = \dfrac{4\pi^2 L}{g}$, so $L = \dfrac{gT^2}{4\pi^2}$.
@step $L = \dfrac{9.81 \times 1.5^2}{4\pi^2} = \dfrac{22.07}{39.48}$.
@answer $L = 0.56$ m.
:::

The period is proportional to the **square root** of the length. To double the period you need four times the length.

:::example On the Moon
The 1 m pendulum is taken to the Moon, where $g = 1.62\ \text{m/s}^2$. What is its period there?
@step $T = 2\pi\sqrt{\dfrac{1}{1.62}} = 2\pi \times 0.7857$.
@answer $T = 4.94$ s, compared with 2.01 s on Earth.
:::

Weaker gravity pulls the bob back more gently, so each swing takes longer. A pendulum clock would run slow on the Moon.

:::mistake Expecting a heavier bob to swing faster
The period of a pendulum does not depend on the mass of the bob. A steel bob and a wooden bob on strings of the same length swing in step. Only the length and the value of $g$ matter.
:::

## Measuring g with a pendulum

Because the period depends only on $L$ and $g$, a pendulum is a simple instrument for measuring the acceleration due to gravity. Rearranging the formula:

$$g = \dfrac{4\pi^2 L}{T^2}$$

:::example Gravity on another world
On the surface of a planet, a simple pendulum 0.8 m long has a period of 2.94 s. What is the acceleration due to gravity there?
@step $g = \dfrac{4\pi^2 L}{T^2} = \dfrac{4\pi^2 \times 0.8}{2.94^2} = \dfrac{31.58}{8.644}$.
@answer $g = 3.65\ \text{m/s}^2$.
:::

In an experiment, do not time a single swing. A swing lasts a second or two, and your reaction time with a stopwatch is a few tenths of a second. Time twenty swings and divide by twenty, and the same reaction error is spread twenty times more thinly.

:::example An experiment
A student times a simple pendulum 90 cm long. It makes 20 complete swings in 38.1 s. What value of $g$ does the experiment give?
@step Period: $T = \dfrac{38.1}{20} = 1.905$ s. Length: $L = 0.90$ m.
@step $g = \dfrac{4\pi^2 L}{T^2} = \dfrac{4\pi^2 \times 0.90}{1.905^2} = \dfrac{35.53}{3.629}$.
@answer $g = 9.79\ \text{m/s}^2$.
:::

A **complete swing** is there and back: from one side, across to the other, and back to the start. Counting each pass through the middle as a swing gives a period that is half the true one, and a value of $g$ four times too large.

:::mistake Forgetting to square the period
In $g = \dfrac{4\pi^2 L}{T^2}$ the period is squared. A quick check: for a pendulum about 1 m long on Earth the period is about 2 s, and $\dfrac{4\pi^2 \times 1}{2^2} \approx 9.9$. If your answer for an experiment on Earth is far from 10, look for a missing square or a length left in centimetres.
:::

:::summary
- Period of a simple pendulum, for small swings: $T = 2\pi\sqrt{\dfrac{L}{g}}$.
- The period does not depend on the mass of the bob or on the amplitude.
- Four times the length gives twice the period. Weaker gravity gives a longer period.
- Rearranged: $L = \dfrac{gT^2}{4\pi^2}$ and $g = \dfrac{4\pi^2 L}{T^2}$.
- In an experiment, time many complete swings and divide.
:::
