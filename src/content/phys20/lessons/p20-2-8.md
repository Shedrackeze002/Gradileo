An astronaut on the Moon can leap in a heavy suit that could hardly be lifted on Earth. The astronaut's mass has not changed, and neither has the suit's. What has changed is the pull of the world underfoot. This lesson gives that pull a number, and shows where the familiar 9.81 comes from.

## Gravitational field strength

A mass changes the space around it: any other mass placed there feels a force. We say that the first mass is surrounded by a **gravitational field**.

:::definition Gravitational field strength
The **gravitational field strength**, $g$, at a point is the gravitational force on each kilogram of mass placed there:

$$g = \dfrac{F_g}{m}$$

It is a vector that points towards the mass that causes the field, and it is measured in newtons per kilogram, N/kg.
:::

Rearranged, this is the rule you already know: $F_g = mg$. And 1 N/kg is the same as $1\ \text{m/s}^2$, so the field strength at a place is also the acceleration of an object in free fall there. At the Earth's surface, $g = 9.81\ \text{N/kg} = 9.81\ \text{m/s}^2$.

:::example A field strength from a weight
On the surface of a planet, a 10 kg instrument has a weight of 37.1 N. What is the gravitational field strength there?
@step $g = \dfrac{F_g}{m} = \dfrac{37.1}{10}$.
@answer $g = 3.71$ N/kg.
:::

:::example The same object on another world
On Earth, where $g = 9.81$ N/kg, a rover has a weight of 490.5 N. What is its weight on a planet where $g = 3.71$ N/kg?
@step The mass is the same everywhere. Find it from the weight on Earth: $m = \dfrac{F_g}{g} = \dfrac{490.5}{9.81} = 50$ kg.
@step On the planet: $F_g = mg = 50 \times 3.71$.
@answer 185.5 N.
:::

Where does the value of $g$ come from? Put the law of universal gravitation into the definition. For an object of mass $m$ at a distance $r$ from the centre of a planet of mass $M$:

$$g = \dfrac{F_g}{m} = \dfrac{GMm}{r^2} \div m = \dfrac{GM}{r^2}$$

The mass of the object cancels. The field strength depends only on the planet and on how far you are from its centre.

:::example The field strength at the surface of a planet
A planet has a mass of $6.4 \times 10^{23}$ kg and a radius of $3.4 \times 10^{6}$ m. Use $G = 6.67 \times 10^{-11}\ \text{N·m}^2/\text{kg}^2$. What is the gravitational field strength at its surface?
@step At the surface, $r$ is the radius of the planet.
@step $g = \dfrac{GM}{r^2} = \dfrac{6.67 \times 10^{-11} \times 6.4 \times 10^{23}}{(3.4 \times 10^{6})^2} = \dfrac{4.269 \times 10^{13}}{1.156 \times 10^{13}}$.
@answer $g = 3.69$ N/kg.
:::

:::mistake Saying that mass changes from planet to planet
Mass is the amount of matter in an object, and it is the same on Earth, on the Moon and in deep space. **Weight** is the force of gravity on the object, $mg$, and it changes whenever $g$ changes.
:::

## Field strength and distance

Because $g = \dfrac{GM}{r^2}$, the field strength follows the inverse square law. It is strongest at the surface and falls away as you move out, with $r$ always measured from the **centre** of the planet.

:::example Above the Earth
Take the mass of the Earth as $5.97 \times 10^{24}$ kg and its radius as $6.37 \times 10^{6}$ m. Use $G = 6.67 \times 10^{-11}\ \text{N·m}^2/\text{kg}^2$. What is the gravitational field strength at a height of $4.0 \times 10^{5}$ m above the Earth's surface?
@step Distance from the centre: $r = 6.37 \times 10^{6} + 0.40 \times 10^{6} = 6.77 \times 10^{6}$ m.
@step $g = \dfrac{GM}{r^2} = \dfrac{6.67 \times 10^{-11} \times 5.97 \times 10^{24}}{(6.77 \times 10^{6})^2} = \dfrac{3.982 \times 10^{14}}{4.583 \times 10^{13}}$.
@answer $g = 8.69$ N/kg.
:::

At that height, which is where many crewed spacecraft orbit, gravity is still almost 90% as strong as on the ground. Astronauts in orbit float because they and their spacecraft are falling together around the Earth, not because gravity has vanished.

When a distance is given in **planet radii**, no constants are needed. Compare with the surface.

:::example A height in Earth radii
At the Earth's surface, $g = 9.81$ N/kg. A 500 kg satellite is at a height of one Earth radius above the surface. Find the gravitational field strength there and the weight of the satellite.
@step The surface is already one Earth radius from the centre, so the satellite is $1 + 1 = 2$ Earth radii from the centre.
@step The distance from the centre has doubled, so $g$ is divided by $2^2 = 4$: $g = \dfrac{9.81}{4} = 2.45$ N/kg.
@step Weight: $F_g = mg = 500 \times 2.4525$.
@answer $g = 2.45$ N/kg, and the satellite weighs 1226.3 N.
:::

:::mistake Counting radii from the surface
"One Earth radius above the surface" is two Earth radii from the centre, and "two Earth radii above the surface" is three. Add 1 before you square.
:::

:::summary
- Gravitational field strength is the force on each kilogram: $g = \dfrac{F_g}{m}$, in N/kg, which is the same as m/s².
- Weight is $F_g = mg$. Mass stays the same everywhere, and weight changes with $g$.
- Around a planet of mass $M$: $g = \dfrac{GM}{r^2}$, with $r$ measured from the centre.
- At the surface, $r$ is the radius of the planet. At a height, add the height to the radius.
- $g$ follows the inverse square law: at twice the distance from the centre it is one quarter as large.
:::
