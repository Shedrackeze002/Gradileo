Flick one end of a long rope and a hump runs along it to the other end. The rope itself goes nowhere: each piece only moves up and down as the hump passes. What travels is the disturbance, and the energy that goes with it. That is a wave.

## Wave properties

:::definition Wave
A **wave** is a disturbance that carries energy from one place to another without carrying matter. A **mechanical wave** needs a **medium** to travel through, such as a rope, water or air. The particles of the medium only vibrate about their own positions.
:::

Waves are sorted by the direction in which the medium vibrates.

| Kind of wave | The medium vibrates | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **transverse** | at right angles to the direction of travel | a wave on a rope or a string, ripples on water |
| **longitudinal** | back and forth along the direction of travel | sound, a pulse pushed along a coiled spring |

A transverse wave has **crests** and **troughs**. A longitudinal wave has **compressions**, where the medium is squeezed together, and **rarefactions**, where it is spread out.

A source that vibrates steadily sends out a steady train of waves, described by four quantities.

:::definition The measurements of a wave
- **Amplitude**, $A$: the greatest displacement of the medium from its rest position (the height of a crest).
- **Wavelength**, $\lambda$: the length of one complete wave, for example from one crest to the next, in metres.
- **Period**, $T$: the time for one complete wave to pass a point, in seconds.
- **Frequency**, $f$: the number of complete waves that pass a point each second, in hertz. $f = \dfrac{1}{T}$.
:::

10 complete waves pass the end of a pier in 25 s. Find the frequency and the period of the waves.
@step Frequency: $f = \dfrac{10}{25} = 0.4$ waves each second.

A wave can be drawn in two ways, and the two graphs look alike. A **snapshot** has position along the bottom and shows the whole wave at one instant: the length of one cycle is the **wavelength**. A graph with **time** along the bottom shows how one point of the medium moves: the length of one cycle is the **period**.

:::mistake Reading a wavelength from a graph against time
Check the label on the horizontal axis before you read a graph of a wave. If it is a distance, one cycle is a wavelength. If it is a time, one cycle is a period, and the graph tells you nothing about the wavelength.

## The universal wave equation

In one period, $T$, a wave moves forward by exactly one wavelength, $\lambda$. Its speed is therefore $\dfrac{\lambda}{T}$, and because $\dfrac{1}{T} = f$:

:::definition Universal wave equation
For every wave, the speed is the frequency multiplied by the wavelength:

$$v = f\lambda$$
:::

:::example The speed of a wave
A wave has a frequency of 4 Hz and a wavelength of 1.5 m. What is its speed?
@step $v = f\lambda = 4 \times 1.5$.
@answer $v = 6$ m/s.
:::

:::example The wavelength of a sound
The speed of sound in the air is 340 m/s. What is the wavelength of a sound of frequency 440 Hz?
@step Rearrange: $\lambda = \dfrac{v}{f} = \dfrac{340}{440}$.
@answer $\lambda = 0.773$ m.
:::

:::example Waves at a pier
An angler notices that 10 complete waves pass the end of a pier in 20 s, and that the crests are 3 m apart. How fast are the waves travelling?
@step Frequency: $f = \dfrac{10}{20} = 0.5$ Hz.
@step The distance between crests is the wavelength: $\lambda = 3$ m.
@step $v = f\lambda = 0.5 \times 3$.
@answer $v = 1.5$ m/s.
:::

Who decides what? The **source** sets the frequency: the medium must vibrate as often as the source shakes it. The **medium** sets the speed: waves travel faster on a tight, light rope than on a slack, heavy one, whatever the frequency. The wavelength then follows from $\lambda = \dfrac{v}{f}$.

So when a wave passes from one medium into another, its frequency stays the same, its speed changes, and its wavelength changes with the speed.

:::example Into a heavier rope
A wave of frequency 5 Hz travels along a rope at 10 m/s. It passes into a heavier rope, where its speed is 4 m/s. Find its wavelength in each rope.
@step First rope: $\lambda = \dfrac{v}{f} = \dfrac{10}{5} = 2$ m.
@step The frequency is still 5 Hz in the second rope: $\lambda = \dfrac{4}{5} = 0.8$ m.
@answer 2 m in the first rope and 0.8 m in the second.
:::

:::mistake Changing the frequency when the medium changes
Each wave that arrives at the join sends exactly one wave on, so the same number of waves pass each second on both sides. The frequency cannot change. A slower medium gives a **shorter wavelength**, not a lower frequency.
:::

:::summary
- A wave carries energy through a medium whose particles only vibrate.
- Transverse waves vibrate across the direction of travel, and longitudinal waves vibrate along it.
- Wavelength is the length of one wave, period is the time for one wave to pass, and $f = \dfrac{1}{T}$.
- Universal wave equation: $v = f\lambda$.
- The source sets the frequency and the medium sets the speed. In a new medium the frequency stays the same.
:::
