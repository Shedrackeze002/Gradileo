A locomotive does not pull each wagon separately. It pulls the first, the first pulls the second, and so on down the train, through the couplings between them. To find how fast the whole train speeds up and how hard each coupling is stretched, physics uses one trick twice: first look at everything together, then look at one piece alone.

## Tension and systems

:::definition Tension
**Tension**, $F_T$, is the pulling force that a rope, string or cable exerts on whatever is attached to its ends. It acts along the rope and away from the object.

In these problems the string is **light** (its mass can be ignored) and does not stretch. Then the tension is the same all along it, and the objects it joins have accelerations of the same size.
:::

:::definition System
A **system** is a group of objects that you choose to treat as one. Forces between the objects of the system are **internal** and cancel in pairs, by Newton's third law. Only the **external** forces accelerate the system:

$$a = \dfrac{F_{\text{net, external}}}{m_{\text{total}}}$$
:::

The method has two steps.

1. Treat all the connected objects as one system to find the **acceleration**. The tension is internal, so it does not appear.
2. Draw a free-body diagram of **one** object alone to find the **tension**. Now the tension is an external force on that object.

## Objects on a level surface

:::example Two blocks pulled along
Two blocks on a level, frictionless surface are joined by a light string. A horizontal force of 24 N pulls the front block, of mass 3 kg, and the rear block, of mass 5 kg, follows. Find the acceleration of the blocks and the tension in the string between them.
@step System of both blocks: the only external horizontal force is the 24 N pull. $a = \dfrac{24}{3 + 5} = 3\ \text{m/s}^2$.
@step Rear block alone: the string is the only horizontal force on it. $F_T = ma = 5 \times 3 = 15$ N.
@step Check with the front block: $24 - 15 = 9$ N, and $3 \times 3 = 9$ N. It agrees.
@answer $a = 3\ \text{m/s}^2$ and $F_T = 15$ N.
:::

The tension is less than the pull. The pull has to accelerate both blocks, but the string has to accelerate only the block behind it.

:::example Two blocks with friction
The same blocks, 3 kg in front and 5 kg behind, are pulled with 30 N across a floor where the coefficient of kinetic friction is 0.2 for both. Use $g = 9.81\ \text{m/s}^2$. Find the acceleration and the tension.
@step Friction on the system: $\mu_k m_{\text{total}}\,g = 0.2 \times 8 \times 9.81 = 15.70$ N.
@step System: $a = \dfrac{30 - 15.70}{8} = 1.79\ \text{m/s}^2$.
@step Rear block alone: the string pulls it forward and friction, $0.2 \times 5 \times 9.81 = 9.81$ N, pulls it back. $F_T - 9.81 = 5 \times 1.788$.
@step $F_T = 8.94 + 9.81$.
@answer $a = 1.79\ \text{m/s}^2$ and $F_T = 18.8$ N.
:::

:::mistake Using the pull as the tension
The string between the blocks does not carry the full pull. In the first example the pull is 24 N but the tension is 15 N. Always find the tension from a free-body diagram of one block.
:::

## Objects joined over a pulley

A light, frictionless pulley changes the **direction** of a string without changing its tension. The two objects still move together, one along the table or upwards and the other downwards. Treat the direction of motion, round the pulley, as positive.

:::example A hanging mass that pulls a block
A 6 kg block on a level, frictionless table is joined by a light string over a frictionless pulley to a 2 kg mass that hangs over the edge. The masses are released. Use $g = 9.81\ \text{m/s}^2$. Find the acceleration of the masses and the tension in the string.
@step System: the only external force along the direction of motion is the weight of the hanging mass, $2 \times 9.81 = 19.62$ N. It has to accelerate both masses.
@step $a = \dfrac{19.62}{2 + 6} = 2.45\ \text{m/s}^2$.
@step Block on the table alone: the string is the only horizontal force on it. $F_T = 6 \times 2.4525 = 14.7$ N.
@step Check with the hanging mass: $19.62 - 14.72 = 4.90$ N, and $2 \times 2.4525 = 4.90$ N.
@answer $a = 2.45\ \text{m/s}^2$ and $F_T = 14.7$ N.
:::

The hanging mass does not fall at $9.81\ \text{m/s}^2$, because it has to drag the block with it. And the tension is less than the weight of the hanging mass. If the two were equal, the hanging mass would not accelerate at all.

Two masses hanging on either side of a pulley make an **Atwood machine**. The heavier one falls and the lighter one rises.

:::example Two hanging masses
Masses of 5 kg and 3 kg hang from the two ends of a light string that passes over a frictionless pulley. The masses are released. Find the size of their acceleration and the tension in the string.
@step System: the two weights pull in opposite senses round the pulley, so the net driving force is their difference. $(5 - 3) \times 9.81 = 19.62$ N.
@step $a = \dfrac{19.62}{5 + 3} = 2.45\ \text{m/s}^2$.
@step Lighter mass alone, moving up: $F_T - mg = ma$, so $F_T = 3 \times (9.81 + 2.4525)$.
@answer $a = 2.45\ \text{m/s}^2$ and $F_T = 36.8$ N.
:::

Notice that the tension, 36.8 N, lies between the two weights, 29.4 N and 49.1 N. It is more than the weight of the mass that accelerates upwards and less than the weight of the mass that accelerates downwards.

:::mistake Dividing by only one of the masses
The driving force accelerates **every** mass in the system. In the table example the acceleration is $\dfrac{19.62}{8}$, not $\dfrac{19.62}{2}$ and not $\dfrac{19.62}{6}$.
:::

:::mistake Setting the tension equal to the hanging weight
The tension equals the weight of a hanging mass only when that mass is at rest or moves at a steady velocity. While it accelerates downwards, the tension is smaller than its weight.
:::

:::summary
- A light string has the same tension all along it, and the objects it joins have accelerations of the same size.
- Step 1: treat the connected objects as one system. $a = \dfrac{F_{\text{net, external}}}{m_{\text{total}}}$.
- Step 2: draw one object alone and use $F_{\text{net}} = ma$ to find the tension.
- A frictionless pulley changes the direction of the tension, not its size.
- Hanging mass and block on a frictionless table: $a = \dfrac{m_{\text{hang}}\,g}{m_{\text{total}}}$. Two hanging masses: $a = \dfrac{(m_1 - m_2)g}{m_1 + m_2}$.
:::
