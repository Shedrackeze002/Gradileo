With 118 elements to keep track of, chemists need a way to organize them. The periodic table puts every element in its place, so that elements that behave alike sit together. It is the most useful single page in chemistry.

## Groups and periods

:::definition Periodic table
The **periodic table** lists the elements in order of their **atomic number**, the number of protons in each atom, starting with hydrogen (1).

- A **period** is a horizontal **row**.
- A **group** is a vertical **column**. Elements in the same group have **similar chemical properties**.
:::

The groups are numbered 1 to 18 from left to right. The first four periods hold these atomic numbers:

| Period | Atomic numbers | Elements |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | 1 to 2 | hydrogen, helium |
| 2 | 3 to 10 | lithium to neon |
| 3 | 11 to 18 | sodium to argon |
| 4 | 19 to 36 | potassium to krypton |

In periods 2 and 3 there are only eight elements, in groups 1, 2, 13, 14, 15, 16, 17 and 18. The gap between groups 2 and 13 is filled from period 4 onwards by metals such as iron and copper.

:::example Finding a place
In which period and group is the element with atomic number 16?
@step 16 is between 11 and 18, so it is in period 3.
@step Period 3 starts at 11 in group 1. Counting along 11, 12, 13, 14, 15, 16 through groups 1, 2, 13, 14, 15, 16 puts 16 in group 16.
@answer Period 3, group 16. It is sulfur.
:::

## How the table was built

:::definition Mendeleev's table
In 1869 the Russian chemist **Dmitri Mendeleev** arranged the 63 elements then known, mostly in order of atomic mass, so that elements with similar properties were in the same column. Where no known element fitted, he left a **gap** and predicted that a new element would be found to fill it.
:::

Mendeleev predicted an element below silicon, which he called eka-silicon. In 1886 germanium was discovered, and its properties matched his predictions closely. Correct predictions like this convinced scientists that his pattern was real.

Mendeleev sometimes broke his own order. Tellurium is heavier than iodine, but he put it first, so that iodine sat with the other halogens. Today we know why: the table really goes in order of **atomic number**, and in that order no swaps are needed.

:::example Why leave gaps?
Why did Mendeleev leave gaps in his table instead of filling every space?
@step He kept elements with similar properties in the same column.
@answer Some elements had not been discovered yet, and the gaps showed where they would go.
:::

:::summary
- The periodic table lists the elements in order of **atomic number**.
- **Periods** are rows; **groups** are columns. Elements in the same group have similar properties.
- Mendeleev (1869) grouped similar elements, left gaps for undiscovered ones, and predicted their properties.
:::
