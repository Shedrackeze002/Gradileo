A small piece of potassium dropped into water whizzes around and bursts into a lilac flame. Neon in a glass tube glows red but never reacts with anything. Both behaviours come from the family, or group, each element belongs to. In this lesson you will meet three famous families.

## The alkali metals

:::definition Alkali metals
The **alkali metals** are the elements of **group 1**: lithium, sodium, potassium, rubidium and caesium. They are soft metals that can be cut with a knife, shiny when freshly cut, with low densities, and they are **very reactive**.
:::

They are stored under oil to keep air and water away. When they react with water, they give off **hydrogen** gas and make a **hydroxide** that dissolves to give an **alkaline** solution, which is how they got their name.

$$\text{sodium} + \text{water} \rightarrow \text{sodium hydroxide} + \text{hydrogen}$$

| Metal | With water |
|---|---|
| lithium | fizzes steadily |
| sodium | melts into a ball and whizzes around |
| potassium | whizzes around and bursts into a lilac flame |

:::example The pattern in group 1
What does the table show about reactivity down group 1?
@step Lithium, then sodium, then potassium react more and more violently.
@answer The alkali metals get **more reactive** down the group.
:::

## Halogens and noble gases

:::definition Halogens and noble gases
The **halogens** are the elements of **group 17**: fluorine, chlorine, bromine and iodine. They are reactive non-metals, and they get **less reactive** down the group.

The **noble gases** are the elements of **group 18**: helium, neon, argon, krypton and xenon. They are colourless gases that are **very unreactive**.
:::

| Halogen | At room temperature |
|---|---|
| fluorine | pale yellow gas |
| chlorine | green gas |
| bromine | red-brown liquid |
| iodine | grey-black solid |

Down group 17 the halogens get darker, and their melting and boiling points rise, so they change from gases to a liquid to a solid. Chlorine kills bacteria, so it is used in small amounts to make drinking water and swimming pools safe.

The noble gases are useful precisely because they do not react. Helium fills balloons safely, argon fills light bulbs so the hot filament does not burn away, and neon glows red in advertising signs.

:::example Helium or hydrogen?
Both helium and hydrogen are less dense than air. Why are party balloons filled with helium?
@step Hydrogen is flammable and can explode.
@answer Helium is a noble gas: it is unreactive, so it cannot burn.

:::summary
- **Group 1, alkali metals**: soft, very reactive metals that give hydrogen and an alkaline solution with water; more reactive down the group.
- **Group 17, halogens**: reactive non-metals; darker and less reactive down the group.
- **Group 18, noble gases**: very unreactive gases, used in balloons, light bulbs and signs.
:::
