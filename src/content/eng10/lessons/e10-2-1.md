In six short lines written in 1851, Alfred, Lord Tennyson puts an eagle on its cliff so clearly that you can almost feel the wind:

> He clasps the crag with crooked hands;\
> Close to the sun in lonely lands,\
> Ring’d with the azure world, he stands.
>
> The wrinkled sea beneath him crawls;\
> He watches from his mountain walls,\
> And like a thunderbolt he falls.
>
> — Alfred, Lord Tennyson, “The Eagle” (1851)

An eagle does not have hands, and the sea does not crawl. Tennyson is using **figurative language**: words that mean more than their literal meaning. He is also using **imagery**: words that let us see the blue world around the bird and the sudden dive at the end. In this lesson you will learn to name the main figures of speech, find the sense an image appeals to, and explain what a comparison suggests.

## Imagery

:::definition Imagery
**Imagery** is language that appeals to the senses: sight, sound, smell, taste and touch. An image lets the reader experience something, not just be told about it.
:::

Most imagery is visual, but the best poems use every sense. Sara Teasdale’s “There Will Come Soft Rains”, written during the First World War, opens with a smell and a sound:

> There will come soft rains and the smell of the ground,\
> And swallows circling with their shimmering sound;
>
> — Sara Teasdale, “There Will Come Soft Rains” (1920)

“The smell of the ground” appeals to smell, and the swallows’ “shimmering sound” to hearing; the “soft rains” can be felt as well as heard. Together they make a quiet, living scene.

:::tip Ask what you would sense
To find the sense an image appeals to, imagine yourself in the scene and ask: what would I see, hear, smell, taste or feel? Colours and light are sight; noises are sound; heat, cold and texture are touch.
:::

## Similes and metaphors

Both similes and metaphors compare two things that are different in most ways but alike in one.

:::definition Simile and metaphor
A **simile** compares two things using **“like”** or **“as”**: *Her hands were as cold as ice.* A **metaphor** compares them by saying that one thing **is** the other: *Her hands were ice.* An **extended metaphor** carries one comparison on through several lines or a whole poem.
:::

Robert Burns uses two similes in the first stanza of “A Red, Red Rose” (“Luve” is Scots for “love”):

> O my Luve’s like a red, red rose,\
> That’s newly sprung in June:\
> O my Luve’s like the melodie,\
> That’s sweetly play’d in tune.
>
> — Robert Burns, “A Red, Red Rose” (1794)

Emily Dickinson uses a metaphor instead. She never says hope is *like* a bird; she says it *is* “the thing with feathers / That perches in the soul”. Because she keeps the comparison going for the whole poem (the bird sings, survives storms and never asks for a crumb), it becomes an extended metaphor.

:::mistake Not every “like” makes a simile
A simile compares two **different** kinds of things. “I like mangoes” uses “like” as a verb, and “Your bike is like mine” compares two things of the same kind. Neither is a simile. “The lake lay still, like a sheet of glass” is one: a lake and glass are very different things.
:::

## Personification, hyperbole and understatement

:::definition Personification, hyperbole and understatement
**Personification** gives human actions or feelings to something that is not human: *The wind whispered through the pines.* **Hyperbole** is deliberate, extreme exaggeration: *I have told you a million times.* **Understatement** makes something sound much smaller than it is: calling a hurricane “a bit of wind”.
:::

Emily Dickinson personifies death as a polite gentleman who stops his carriage for the speaker:

> Because I could not stop for Death,\
> He kindly stopped for me;
>
> — Emily Dickinson, “Because I could not stop for Death” (1890)

Burns ends his love song with hyperbole: he will love her “Till a’ the seas gang dry” (until all the seas go dry). No one expects the seas to dry up; the impossible length of time shows how strong the feeling is.

## What a figure of speech suggests

Naming a device is only the first step. The real question is **what the comparison suggests**: which qualities of the second thing the writer wants us to see in the first.

:::example What does the comparison suggest?
In “The Rime of the Ancient Mariner”, the wind has dropped and the ship cannot move:

> Day after day, day after day,\
> We stuck, nor breath nor motion;\
> As idle as a painted ship\
> Upon a painted ocean.
>
> — Samuel Taylor Coleridge, “The Rime of the Ancient Mariner” (1798)

What does the simile suggest?
@step Name the two things compared: the real ship and a ship in a painting.
@step Ask what a painted ship is like: it can never move, and its sea never changes.
@step Carry those qualities back to the real ship: it is utterly still, stuck “Day after day”, with “nor breath nor motion”.
@answer The simile suggests that the ship is completely, hopelessly still, as frozen as a picture, and the sailors are trapped.
:::

:::summary
- **Imagery** appeals to the senses: sight, sound, smell, taste and touch.
- A **simile** compares with “like” or “as”; a **metaphor** says one thing is another; an **extended metaphor** keeps one comparison going.
- **Personification** gives human qualities to something that is not human; **hyperbole** exaggerates; **understatement** makes something seem smaller than it is.
- After naming a device, explain what it suggests: what qualities of the second thing the writer wants us to see in the first.
