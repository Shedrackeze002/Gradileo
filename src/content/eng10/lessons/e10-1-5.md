This lesson discusses the endings of “The Gift of the Magi”, “The Story of an Hour”, “The Monkey’s Paw” and “The Open Window”.
:::

At the end of “The Gift of the Magi”, after a young couple have each sold their greatest treasure to buy a present for the other, O. Henry steps forward to tell us what he thinks of them:

> But in a last word to the wise of these days let it be said that of all who give gifts these two were the wisest.
>
> — O. Henry, “The Gift of the Magi” (1905)

Two people who end up with useless presents are called the *wisest*? That surprise is **irony**, and it points us to the story’s **theme**, its message about life. In this lesson you will learn to state a theme, interpret a **symbol** and recognize three kinds of irony.

## Theme

:::definition Theme
The **theme** of a story is its central message or insight about life or human nature. A **topic** is a word or phrase for what the story is about (love, freedom, greed); a theme is a complete sentence that says something about that topic.
:::

A strong **theme statement**:

- is a complete sentence, not a single word;
- is about people or life in general, not just the characters in this story;
- is supported by the events of the story;
- avoids orders (“You should …”) and absolutes (“always”, “never”, “everyone”).

:::example Writing a theme statement for “The Gift of the Magi”
Della sells her hair to buy a chain for Jim’s watch; Jim sells his watch to buy combs for Della’s hair. The narrator calls them the wisest of all who give gifts. Write a theme statement.
@step Name the topic: love and sacrifice.
@step Ask what the story says about it. Both gifts are useless, yet the narrator calls the couple wise, so what matters is not the gifts but the love that made each person give up a treasure.
@step Turn the idea into a general statement, without names, orders or absolutes.
@answer The love behind a sacrifice matters more than the gift itself.
:::

:::mistake Plot summaries, morals and absolutes
“Della and Jim sell their treasures to buy each other gifts” retells the plot. “You should never sell your treasures” is an order. “Poor people are always happier than rich people” overgeneralizes, and the story does not support it. None of these is a theme statement.
:::

## Symbol

:::definition Symbol
A **symbol** is an object, place, person or action that stands for something beyond itself, usually an idea or feeling. A symbol keeps its ordinary meaning in the story and also carries a deeper one.
:::

Writers signal a symbol by giving an object special attention: it appears at important moments, it is described in detail, or it changes when the character changes. Mrs. Mallard in “The Story of an Hour” realizes she is free while sitting at an **open window**, looking out at “the new spring life”; the window suggests the new life opening in front of her. In “The Gift of the Magi”, the **combs** and the **watch chain** stand for the love each partner showed by what they gave up.

:::tip Object, idea, evidence
To interpret a symbol, name the object, name the idea it stands for, and point to the moment in the story that links them. A detail that is merely true about the object (“the combs were tortoise shell”) is not evidence for what it means.
:::

## Irony

:::definition Three kinds of irony
- **Verbal irony**: a speaker says the opposite of what they mean, or something with a hidden second meaning. Sarcasm is a sharp kind of verbal irony.
- **Situational irony**: what happens is the opposite of what was expected.
- **Dramatic irony**: the reader or audience knows something that a character does not.
:::

In Poe’s “The Cask of Amontillado”, Montresor is leading Fortunato into the vaults where he plans to kill him. When Fortunato drinks a toast, Montresor answers:

> “I drink,” he said, “to the buried that repose around us.” “And I to your long life.”
>
> — Edgar Allan Poe, “The Cask of Amontillado” (1846)

Wishing a man long life while planning to end it is **verbal irony**.

“The Gift of the Magi” ends in **situational irony**: each gift was bought for the very treasure the other has just sold. In “The Monkey’s Paw”, the Whites wish for two hundred pounds and receive exactly that sum, as compensation for their son’s death.

“The Story of an Hour” closes with **dramatic irony**:

> When the doctors came they said she had died of heart disease — of the joy that kills.
>
> — Kate Chopin, “The Story of an Hour” (1894)

The doctors believe she died of joy at seeing her husband alive. The reader, who has just watched her rejoice at being free, knows better.

:::mistake Irony is not just bad luck
Rain on a picnic day is unlucky, not ironic. Situational irony needs a reversal of what was **expected**: a fire station burning down, or a gift made useless by the other gift. Ask what everyone expected, and whether the outcome is its opposite.
:::

:::summary
- A theme is a complete, general statement about life that the story supports; a topic is just a word or phrase. Avoid plot summaries, orders and absolutes.
- A symbol is an object that stands for an idea; support an interpretation with the moment in the story that links the object to the idea.
- Verbal irony says the opposite of what is meant, situational irony reverses what was expected, and dramatic irony lets the reader know what a character does not.
:::
