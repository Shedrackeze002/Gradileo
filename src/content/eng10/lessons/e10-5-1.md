Around 1831, the Japanese artist Katsushika Hokusai made a woodblock print of a gigantic wave curling over three small boats, its foam reaching out like claws. Far in the distance, under the curve of the wave, sits Mount Fuji, Japan’s highest mountain, looking tiny. *The Great Wave off Kanagawa* is one of the most famous images in the world, and almost everything in it was arranged on purpose. In this lesson you will learn to read an image the way you read a text: by noticing its parts, how they are arranged, and the effect of each choice.

## Where does the eye go first?

:::definition Focal point
The **focal point** of an image is the part the eye goes to first. Artists and photographers create it with size, brightness, sharp focus, strong colour, or lines that lead to it.
:::

Images have depth, like a stage. The **foreground** is the part that seems closest to the viewer, the **middle ground** is the part in between, and the **background** is the part that seems farthest away. In *The Great Wave*, the boats and the wave fill the foreground and middle ground, and Mount Fuji sits in the background.

:::example Reading a poster
> A poster for a music festival. In the foreground, a girl in a red jacket holds a guitar above her head, sharp and brightly lit. Behind her, a crowd of hundreds is blurred, and in the background a stage glows purple against a night sky.

What is the focal point, and how does the designer make it stand out?
@step Look for what is largest, brightest and sharpest: the girl is all three, and she is in the foreground.
@step Her red jacket stands out against the purple and black behind her, and the crowd is blurred so it does not compete.
@answer The **girl with the guitar** is the focal point. Size, sharp focus, bright light and a strong colour all pull the eye to her.
:::

## Composition

:::definition Composition
**Composition** is the way the parts of an image are arranged in the frame.
:::

- **Leading lines** are lines such as roads, rivers, rails or fences that draw the eye toward the subject.
- **Framing** surrounds the subject with something in the picture: a doorway, a window, branches. In *The Great Wave*, the curve of the wave frames Mount Fuji.
- **The rule of thirds** imagines the picture divided into thirds across and down, and places the subject on one of the lines or where they cross, rather than in the centre. Off-centre subjects often feel more lively.
- **Contrast** puts very different things side by side, such as light and dark, so that one stands out.
- **Symmetry** makes one half of the picture mirror the other, which feels balanced, calm or formal.

Breaking these habits can also be a choice. Caspar David Friedrich’s *Wanderer above the Sea of Fog* (about 1818) places a man in a dark coat right in the centre, his back to us, standing on a rocky peak above a sea of fog. Because we see what he sees, over his shoulder, the painting invites us to share his view.

## The effect of each choice

Every visual choice changes how the viewer feels. These are the usual effects; a skilled artist can play against them, so always check them against the whole image.

| Choice | Usual effect |
|---|---|
| warm colours (red, orange, yellow) | warmth, energy, comfort |
| cool colours (blue, green, grey) | calm, sadness, distance |
| bright, even light | cheerful, open, safe |
| darkness and deep shadow | mystery, fear, suspense |
| a small figure in a vast space | loneliness, smallness against nature |
| seen from below | power, importance |
| seen from above | weakness, vulnerability |
| black and white | timeless, serious, the past |

In Vermeer’s *Girl with a Pearl Earring* (about 1665), the background is almost black, and light falls on the girl’s face and the pearl at her ear. The contrast makes her face the focal point, and her glance over her shoulder makes it feel as if she has just noticed us.

:::mistake Describing without explaining
“The picture is dark” describes; “the darkness makes the house feel threatening, as if something is hiding there” analyses. Always connect a visual choice to its effect on the viewer.
:::

## Putting it together

A good analysis of an image moves from what you see to what it means: first the focal point, then the composition, then the effect of colour, light and space, and finally the message of the whole image.

:::example Analysing a photograph
> A single hiker, tiny, crosses a vast, empty desert under a pale sky. The hiker is placed a third of the way in from the left, and a line of footprints leads back toward the bottom of the picture.

Write a short analysis of the photograph.
@step **Focal point:** the hiker, the only figure in the empty landscape.
@step **Composition:** the hiker sits on a rule-of-thirds line, and the footprints act as leading lines that pull the eye to the figure and show how far it has walked.
@step **Effect:** the tiny figure in the vast, empty space makes the hiker seem small and alone.
@answer The photograph uses the rule of thirds and leading lines to draw the eye to a lone hiker, and the vast empty desert around the figure suggests how small and alone a person can be in nature.
:::

:::summary
- The focal point is where the eye goes first; size, light, sharp focus, colour and leading lines create it.
- The foreground seems closest to the viewer, the background farthest away.
- Composition arranges the parts of an image: leading lines, framing, the rule of thirds, contrast and symmetry.
- Colour, light, space and viewpoint each shape how the viewer feels. Always connect a choice to its effect.
:::
