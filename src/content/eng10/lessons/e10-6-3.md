Every day someone tries to persuade you: an advertisement, a friend who wants to see a different film, a petition taped to the school door. A **persuasive essay** does the same job in a careful, honest way. It takes a clear position, supports it with reasons and evidence, answers the other side fairly, and tells the reader what to do next. In this lesson you will plan each of those parts.

## Taking a position

:::definition Position statement
A **position statement** is the thesis of a persuasive essay: one sentence that takes a clear side on an issue and says what should happen. *Our school should start classes at 9:30 instead of 8:00.*
:::

Three kinds of sentence look like positions but are not:

- **A topic**: *School start times are an interesting topic.* It names the issue but takes no side.
- **The fence**: *There are good points on both sides.* A persuasive essay must choose.
- **A question**: *Should our school start later?* Your reader wants your answer, not the question.

:::tip Specific beats vague
*Our school should start later* is a position, but *Our school should start classes at 9:30 instead of 8:00* is stronger: the reader knows exactly what you are asking for.
:::

## Reasons and evidence

Each body paragraph gives one **reason** for the position and backs it with **evidence**: a fact, a statistic, an example, an expert’s view or the results of a survey. A reason without evidence (*Starting later would just be better for everyone*) is only an opinion repeated.

:::note Use real evidence
In your own essays, use evidence you can stand behind: a survey you really carried out, an observation you really made, or a reliable published source. Invented numbers, however convincing they sound, destroy a writer’s credibility the moment a reader checks them.
:::

Watch for reasons that do not help the argument at all: a fact that is true but beside the point (*Many students like cereal for breakfast*), an insult (*Only lazy teachers want school to start early*), or a point that actually supports the other side.

## Counterarguments and rebuttals

A persuasive essay is more convincing when it faces the strongest argument against it.

:::definition Counterargument and rebuttal
A **counterargument** is a reasonable view held by people on the other side. A **rebuttal** is the writer’s answer to it. A strong rebuttal **concedes** what is fair in the counterargument, then **answers** it with a reason or evidence.
:::

:::example Writing a rebuttal
Position: *The school library should stay open until 6:00 p.m. on weekdays.* Counterargument: *Paying staff to stay late would cost too much.* Write a strong rebuttal.
@step Concede what is fair: staff time really does cost money.
@step Answer with a reason or a practical solution: trained student volunteers could help run the evening hours.
@step Show how the answer meets the concern: only one paid member of staff would be needed.
@answer *Staff time does cost money, but trained student volunteers could help run the evening hours, so only one paid member of staff would be needed.*
:::

:::check e10-rebuttal 1
:::

Weak rebuttals fail in four ways:

- **Denying**: *That is simply not true.* No reason is given.
- **Attacking people**: *People who worry about the cost do not care about students.* This answers nobody’s argument.
- **Giving up**: *That is a fair point, so perhaps the library should close early after all.* The writer abandons their own position.
- **Ignoring**: *The library should also buy new chairs.* The counterargument is never answered.

:::mistake Arguing against a weaker version
It is tempting to answer the silliest form of the other side’s view, such as “Some people think students never study anyway.” Readers notice, and they trust you less. Choose the **strongest** counterargument and answer that.
:::

## Putting it in order, and ending with action

A common structure for a persuasive essay:

1. **Introduction**: a hook that grabs the reader, background on the issue, and the position statement.
2. **Body paragraphs**: one reason per paragraph, each backed by evidence, usually ending with the strongest.
3. **Counterargument and rebuttal**: often its own paragraph, either after the reasons or before them.
4. **Conclusion**: restate the position in fresh words and end with a call to action.

:::definition Call to action
A **call to action** tells the reader exactly what to do next: a specific, realistic step they can take soon. *Sign the student council’s petition this week, and ask your parents to write to the principal before the board meets next month.*
:::

“Think about it” is too vague to act on, and “If nothing changes, everyone will regret it forever” exaggerates so much that readers stop listening. A good call to action is concrete, possible and addressed to the people reading.

:::

:::summary
- A position statement takes one clear, specific side; a topic, a fence-sitting sentence or a question is not a position.
- Each reason needs real evidence, and reasons must support the position directly.
- A strong rebuttal concedes what is fair in the strongest counterargument, then answers it with a reason or evidence; denying, attacking people, giving up or ignoring it all weaken the essay.
- End with a call to action: a specific, realistic step the reader can take.
:::
