The clock on the wall says forty-five minutes. The prompt is on the desk. Some students start writing at once and run out of ideas halfway down the second page; others plan for so long that the conclusion never gets written. A timed essay rewards a different skill from a take-home essay: making good decisions quickly. In this lesson you will read a prompt exactly, plan in a few minutes, divide your time, and leave room to check your work.

## Reading the prompt

The most expensive mistake in timed writing is answering a question that was not asked. An essay that describes when the prompt says *evaluate* can be beautifully written and still score poorly.

:::definition Key verbs in essay prompts
| Verb | What it asks you to do |
|---|---|
| Analyse | Examine the parts or features of something and explain how they work and what effect they have |
| Compare | Show how two or more things are alike, and usually how they differ |
| Contrast | Show how two or more things are different |
| Evaluate | Judge how good, effective or important something is, and support the judgement with evidence |
| Explain | Make clear how or why something happens |
| Discuss | Consider an issue from more than one side and reach a reasoned conclusion |
| Describe | Give the details of what something is like |
| Argue | Take one side and defend it with reasons and evidence |
:::

:::tip Underline, then circle
Underline the key verb and circle exactly what it applies to. In *Evaluate how effectively Saki builds suspense in “The Open Window”*, the verb is *evaluate* and the target is *how effectively Saki builds suspense*. Your essay must give a judgement, not just a list of techniques.
:::

## Planning in five to ten minutes

A plan does not need to be neat; it needs to exist. Five to ten minutes of planning saves far more time than it costs, because you never stop halfway to wonder what comes next.

:::definition A quick timed-essay plan
1. **Read the prompt** carefully and mark its key words.
2. **Brainstorm** ideas and evidence: scenes, details and short quotations you remember.
3. **Choose your thesis** and the two or three points that prove it best.
4. **Order the points**, with a word or two of evidence beside each.
5. **Write**, following the plan.
6. **Proofread** in the last few minutes.
:::

:::example Dividing the time
You have 60 minutes for one essay. Plan your time.
@step Planning: about 10 minutes to read the prompt, brainstorm, choose a thesis and outline.
@step Proofreading: about 5 minutes at the end to catch errors and missing words.
@step Writing: everything in between.
@answer *10 minutes planning, 45 minutes writing, 5 minutes proofreading.*
:::

:::mistake Writing the essay twice
Some students write a full rough draft and then copy it out neatly. In a timed exam, that spends half the time copying instead of thinking. Write one careful draft from a good plan, and correct it neatly with a single line through any mistake.
:::

Here is the kind of plan a student might jot down in the first few minutes, for the prompt *Contrast the man and the dog in “To Build a Fire”*:

> Thesis: London contrasts the man’s confidence with the dog’s instinct to show that knowledge without imagination is not enough to survive. 1. Knowledge: the man knows the facts about the cold (fifty below) but not what they mean. 2. Instinct: the dog knows without being told that it is too cold to travel. 3. Fate: the man dies; the dog trots on toward the camp.

That plan took a few minutes, and it already contains the thesis, the order of the body paragraphs and the evidence for each.

## Writing and checking

- **Start with the thesis.** A short introduction that states your thesis clearly is better than a long, wandering one.
- **One point per paragraph.** Begin each body paragraph with a topic sentence that links back to the thesis.
- **Use short quotations or precise details.** You are not expected to remember long passages; a few exact words, or an accurate detail, are enough.
- **Watch the clock** at the end of each paragraph, not in the middle of a sentence.
- **If time runs short,** finish with a brief conclusion that restates your thesis, rather than stopping mid-paragraph.

:::tip The last five minutes
Read the essay through once, quickly, looking for missing words, unfinished sentences, spelling errors in names and titles, and run-on sentences. Fixing ten small errors in five minutes is the cheapest improvement you can make.
:::

:::summary
- Find the key verb of the prompt and do exactly what it asks: analyse, compare, contrast, evaluate, explain, discuss, describe or argue.
- Spend about five to ten minutes planning: ideas, a thesis, and points in order with their evidence.
- Divide the time: a short plan, most of the time writing, a few minutes at the end to proofread.
- Write one careful draft, one point per paragraph, and use the last minutes to correct errors.
:::
