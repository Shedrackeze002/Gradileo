Here is a poem by Edward Lear, from *A Book of Nonsense*:

> There was an Old Man with a beard,\
> Who said, “It is just as I feared!—\
> Two Owls and a Hen,\
> Four Larks and a Wren,\
> Have all built their nests in my beard!”
>
> — Edward Lear, “There was an Old Man with a beard” (1846)

Before you reach the end, you know it is going to be funny. Five lines, a bouncing rhythm, two short lines rushing toward a punchline: the **form** tells you what kind of poem you are reading. A sonnet, an elegy or a haiku would set up quite different expectations. In this lesson you will learn to name stanzas and the main forms of poetry, and to explain how a poem’s form shapes its meaning.

## Stanzas

:::definition Stanza
A **stanza** is a group of lines in a poem, set apart by a space, like a paragraph in prose. Stanzas are named by their number of lines:

- **couplet**: 2 lines
- **tercet**: 3 lines
- **quatrain**: 4 lines
- **sestet**: 6 lines
- **octave**: 8 lines
:::

Tennyson’s “The Eagle” is two tercets: three lines about the eagle perched on its cliff, then three about what it watches and its sudden dive. The space between them marks the change from stillness to action.

## The sonnet

:::definition Sonnet
A **sonnet** is a poem of fourteen lines, usually in iambic pentameter, with a set rhyme scheme. At some point it **turns** to a new thought or feeling; the turn is called the **volta**.
:::

There are two main kinds:

| | Shakespearean (English) sonnet | Petrarchan (Italian) sonnet |
|---|---|---|
| Parts | three quatrains and a couplet | an octave (8 lines) and a sestet (6 lines) |
| Rhyme scheme | ABAB CDCD EFEF GG | ABBAABBA, then CDECDE, CDCDCD or similar |
| The turn | often at line 9, or in the closing couplet | usually at line 9, where the sestet begins |

In a Shakespearean sonnet, the closing couplet often sums up the poem or gives it a twist. Sonnet 18 spends twelve lines praising someone who is lovelier than a summer’s day, then ends by claiming that the poem itself will keep that beauty alive:

> So long as men can breathe, or eyes can see,\
> So long lives this, and this gives life to thee.
>
> — William Shakespeare, “Sonnet 18” (1609)

:::example Finding the volta
Find the turn in Christina Rossetti’s Petrarchan sonnet “Remember”.

> Remember me when I am gone away,\
> Gone far away into the silent land;\
> When you can no more hold me by the hand,\
> Nor I half turn to go yet turning stay.\
> Remember me when no more day by day\
> You tell me of our future that you planned:\
> Only remember me; you understand\
> It will be late to counsel then or pray.\
> Yet if you should forget me for a while\
> And afterwards remember, do not grieve:\
> For if the darkness and corruption leave\
> A vestige of the thoughts that once I had,\
> Better by far you should forget and smile\
> Than that you should remember and be sad.
>
> — Christina Rossetti, “Remember” (1862)

@step The octave (lines 1 to 8) rhymes ABBAABBA. Three times the speaker begs, “Remember me”.
@step Line 9 begins with “Yet”, a turning word, and starts the sestet.
@step After it, the speaker says something new: if remembering would make her loved one sad, she would rather be forgotten.
@answer The volta comes at line 9, “Yet if you should forget me for a while”: the poem turns from asking to be remembered to freeing the loved one from grief.
:::

## Other forms

:::definition Forms of poetry
- **Haiku**: a three-line Japanese form that captures a single moment, often in nature. In English it usually has five, seven and five syllables.
- **Limerick**: a comic five-line poem rhyming AABBA, with two short middle lines.
- **Ballad**: a poem that tells a story, often in quatrains rhyming ABCB, first meant to be sung, often with a refrain.
- **Ode**: a formal poem that speaks directly to its subject and praises it.
- **Elegy**: a poem that mourns someone who has died.
- **Free verse**: poetry with no regular rhyme or metre, following the rhythms of speech.
:::

A haiku can be a single breath. Here is a famous one by the Japanese poet Matsuo Bashō (1686), in Gradileo’s own word-for-word translation, with a haiku written for this course beside it:

> The old pond—\
> a frog jumps in,\
> the sound of water.

> Morning frost on glass\
> a sparrow shakes the cold twig\
> sunlight finds the field

Samuel Taylor Coleridge’s “The Rime of the Ancient Mariner” is a literary ballad: it tells a long story in short, song-like stanzas such as “Water, water, every where, / Nor any drop to drink.” Percy Bysshe Shelley’s “Ode to the West Wind” speaks straight to its subject: “O wild West Wind, thou breath of Autumn’s being”. Walt Whitman’s “O Captain! My Captain!” is an elegy for President Abraham Lincoln, and his “When I Heard the Learn’d Astronomer” is free verse, its lines as long or short as the thought needs.

:::tip Why the form matters
When you write about a poem’s form, do not stop at naming it. Say what the form does: a couplet that clinches an argument, a refrain that keeps returning to a place, a haiku’s single image, free verse that sprawls like a tiring lecture and then falls quiet.
:::

:::summary
- Stanzas are named by their number of lines: couplet (2), tercet (3), quatrain (4), sestet (6), octave (8).
- A **sonnet** has fourteen lines. A Shakespearean sonnet is three quatrains and a couplet (ABAB CDCD EFEF GG); a Petrarchan sonnet is an octave and a sestet. The turn is the **volta**.
- A **haiku** captures a moment in three lines; a **limerick** is a comic AABBA poem; a **ballad** tells a story in song-like stanzas; an **ode** praises its subject; an **elegy** mourns; **free verse** has no regular rhyme or metre.
- Form shapes meaning: ask where the poem pauses, repeats and turns, and why.
:::
