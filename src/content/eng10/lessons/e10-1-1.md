On a stormy night in 1902, readers of W. W. Jacobs’s “The Monkey’s Paw” met the White family by their fire:

> Without, the night was cold and wet, but in the small parlour of Laburnam Villa the blinds were drawn and the fire burned brightly.
>
> — W. W. Jacobs, “The Monkey’s Paw” (1902)

Before the evening is over, a visitor has left them a dried monkey’s paw that grants three wishes, and the story has begun to move. In this lesson you will learn how a story’s events are shaped into a **plot**, what drives that plot forward (**conflict**), and how writers play with time to keep you reading.

## The shape of a plot

A **plot** is the sequence of events in a story, arranged so that each one leads to the next. Most short stories follow the same five stages, often drawn as a mountain: the tension climbs to a peak and then comes down.

:::definition The five stages of a plot
- **Exposition**: introduces the characters, the setting and the situation, before the main problem takes hold.
- **Rising action**: a series of complications that build the problem and raise the tension.
- **Climax**: the turning point, the moment of greatest tension, when the main conflict comes to a head and the outcome is decided.
- **Falling action**: the events that follow straight on from the climax, as the tension eases.
- **Resolution**: how things stand at the end; the loose ends are tied up, or deliberately left open.
:::

The event that starts the rising action is sometimes called the **inciting incident**: in “The Monkey’s Paw”, it is the moment Mr. White pulls the paw out of the fire and keeps it.

The table below gives away the ending of “The Gift of the Magi”.
:::

| Stage | In “The Gift of the Magi” |
|---|---|
| Exposition | On the day before Christmas, Della has only one dollar and eighty-seven cents to buy her husband, Jim, a present. |
| Rising action | Della sells her long hair for twenty dollars and buys a chain for Jim’s gold watch. |
| Climax | Della opens Jim’s present, combs for her long hair, and learns that he sold his watch to buy them. |
| Falling action | Jim lies back on the couch, smiles and asks Della to put the chops on for dinner. |
| Resolution | The narrator declares that, of all who give gifts, these two are the wisest. |

:::example Finding the climax of “The Monkey’s Paw”
After the first wish, the Whites receive two hundred pounds, as compensation for their son Herbert’s death at work. Ten days later, Mrs. White makes her husband wish Herbert alive again. That night a knock sounds at the door. As Mrs. White struggles with the bolt, Mr. White searches for the paw. Which event is the climax?
@step The climax is the turning point: after it, the outcome is decided. Look for the moment the whole story has been building toward.
@step The knocking builds unbearable tension: something is outside, and Mrs. White is about to let it in.
@step The bolt comes back at the same moment that Mr. White finds the paw and makes his third wish. After that, the knocking stops and the road outside is empty.
@answer The climax is Mr. White’s third wish, made as the bolt slides back. The knocking stopping is the falling action, and the empty road is the resolution.
:::

:::mistake The climax is not simply the most exciting event
A story can have several exciting moments. The climax is the one that **decides** the outcome: everything before it builds toward it, and everything after it follows from it. It also usually comes near the end, not in the middle.
:::

## Conflict

A plot moves because a character wants something and something stands in the way. That struggle is the **conflict**. Without it, nothing would happen.

:::definition Internal and external conflict
An **external conflict** is a struggle against an outside force: another person, nature, or society. An **internal conflict** happens inside a character’s mind: a fear, a doubt, a temptation or a hard choice.
:::

| Kind of conflict | The character struggles against | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Character vs. character | another person | Two rivals compete for the same job. |
| Character vs. nature | weather, land, animals, illness | In “To Build a Fire”, a man fights the deadly cold of the Yukon. |
| Character vs. society | laws, traditions, the expectations of a community | A student campaigns against an unfair school rule. |
| Character vs. self (internal) | their own fears, doubts or desires | A character must decide whether to keep money that is not theirs. |

Some writers add character against fate, the supernatural or technology. The Whites, who use magic to interfere with fate, face that kind of conflict.

Many stories combine conflicts. Jack London’s man in “To Build a Fire” fights the cold (external), but his real weakness is inside him:

> The trouble with him was that he was without imagination. He was quick and alert in the things of life, but only in the things, and not in the significances.
>
> — Jack London, “To Build a Fire” (1908)

:::tip Ask “against what?”
To name a conflict, finish the sentence “The character struggles against …”. If the answer is a person, a force of nature or a society, the conflict is external; if it is something inside the character, it is internal.
:::

## Playing with time: foreshadowing, flashback and suspense

Writers rarely tell events in a flat, straight line. They hint at what is coming, jump back into the past, and hold back the answer to keep you reading.

:::definition Techniques of plot
- **Foreshadowing**: an early hint of what will happen later.
- **Flashback**: a scene that interrupts the story to show events from the past.
- **Suspense**: the anxious uncertainty a reader feels while waiting to find out what will happen.
- **In medias res** (Latin for “into the middle of things”): opening the story in the middle of the action.
- **Cliffhanger**: ending a chapter or episode at a moment of danger or uncertainty, with the outcome unknown.
:::

“The Monkey’s Paw” is full of foreshadowing. Before anyone makes a wish, the old soldier who owned the paw explains:

> “The first man had his three wishes. Yes,” was the reply; “I don’t know what the first two were, but the third was for death. That’s how I got the paw.”
>
> — W. W. Jacobs, “The Monkey’s Paw” (1902)

Late in the story, Jacobs builds suspense by slowing down and holding back what is happening:

> At the foot of the stairs the match went out, and he paused to strike another; and at the same moment a knock, so quiet and stealthy as to be scarcely audible, sounded on the front door.
>
> — W. W. Jacobs, “The Monkey’s Paw” (1902)

Ambrose Bierce opens “An Occurrence at Owl Creek Bridge” in medias res, with a man about to be hanged, and only later uses a flashback to show how he got there:

> A man stood upon a railroad bridge in northern Alabama, looking down into the swift water twenty feet below. The man’s hands were behind his back, the wrists bound with a cord. A rope closely encircled his neck.
>
> — Ambrose Bierce, “An Occurrence at Owl Creek Bridge” (1890)

:::note Open endings
Some stories never give a resolution at all (this note gives away the ending of one of them). Frank R. Stockton’s “The Lady, or the Tiger?” ends as a young man opens one of two doors, behind one of which waits a tiger, and leaves the reader to decide what came out. An open ending invites you to argue from the evidence in the story.
:::

:::mistake Foreshadowing is only clear on a second look
A hint of the future usually looks like an ordinary detail the first time you read it. When you reach the end of a story, go back and look for the early details that pointed toward it.
:::

:::summary
- A plot has five stages: exposition, rising action, climax, falling action and resolution. The climax is the turning point that decides the outcome.
- Conflict drives the plot. External conflict sets a character against another person, nature or society; internal conflict happens inside the character’s mind.
- Foreshadowing hints at the future, a flashback returns to the past, suspense makes the reader wait, in medias res opens in the middle of the action, and a cliffhanger stops at a moment of danger.
:::
