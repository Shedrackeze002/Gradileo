In 1852, the orator Frederick Douglass, who had escaped from slavery, was invited to give an Independence Day speech in Rochester, New York. Instead of the usual praise, he asked his audience why they expected him to argue a point that should need no argument:

> Must I undertake to prove that the slave is a man? That point is conceded already. Nobody doubts it.
>
> — Frederick Douglass, “What to the Slave Is the Fourth of July?” (1852)

Douglass knew exactly how arguments are built, and he went on to support every point he made. In this lesson you will take an argument apart: its **claim**, the **reasons** and **evidence** behind it, and the way it answers the other side. You will also learn to tell **fact** from **opinion** and to judge evidence.

## The parts of an argument

:::definition The parts of an argument
- The **claim** is the writer’s main position: the point the whole argument sets out to prove.
- A **reason** explains *why* the writer holds the claim.
- **Evidence** backs up a reason with specific facts, numbers, examples or expert opinions.
- A **counterclaim** states an opposing view.
- A **rebuttal** answers the counterclaim and shows why it does not defeat the claim.
:::

Here is a short argument with each part labelled:

> **(1)** Our school day should start at 9:30 instead of 8:00. **(2)** Teenagers learn better when they are well rested. **(3)** When Lakeside Secondary moved its start time to 9:30, attendance rose by 8 percent in a single year. **(4)** Some parents argue that a later start would make it harder for them to get to work on time. **(5)** However, the school could open at 8:00 for supervised reading, so students could still be dropped off early.

Sentence 1 is the claim. Sentence 2 is a reason: it says *why*. Sentence 3 is evidence: a specific number from a specific school. Sentence 4 is the counterclaim, introduced with *Some parents argue*. Sentence 5, beginning *However*, is the rebuttal.

:::tip Signal words
Counterclaims often begin with *Some people argue*, *Critics say* or *Opponents claim*. Rebuttals often begin with *However*, *But* or *Yet*. Reasons often follow *because*.
:::

:::

:::mistake Mixing up a reason and its evidence
A reason is general (*teenagers learn better when they are rested*); evidence is specific and can be checked (*attendance rose by 8 percent at Lakeside Secondary*). If you could ask “How do you know?” about a sentence, it is probably a reason, and the evidence is the answer.
:::

:::check e10-argument-parts 2
:::

Why include the other side at all? A writer who states a counterclaim fairly, then answers it, looks thoughtful and confident. An argument that ignores obvious objections leaves the reader to raise them, unanswered.

:::check e10-argument-parts 3
:::

## Fact and opinion

:::definition Fact and opinion
A **fact** is a statement that can be checked and proved true or false: *Light travels faster than sound.* An **opinion** is a judgment, belief or feeling that people can reasonably disagree about: *Summer is the most enjoyable season.*
:::

Opinions often contain judging words such as *best*, *worst*, *beautiful*, *boring* or *should*. Facts can be checked against a measurement, a record or a reliable source. A statement can be a fact and still be false: *The Pacific is the smallest ocean* can be checked, and it is wrong.

:::check e10-evidence-types 1
:::

## Kinds of evidence

| Kind of evidence | What it is | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Statistic** | numbers or percentages | *In a survey of 300 students, 64 percent said their homes were too noisy for homework.* |
| **Anecdote** | a short personal story | *When my cousin started reading every week, her essays improved.* |
| **Expert testimony** | the view of someone with special knowledge | *Dr. Ruth Mensah, a professor of education, calls daily reading “the single best habit a student can build”.* |
| **Example** | a specific case that shows the point, not a personal story | *Lakeside Secondary, for example, added a daily reading period, and its results rose.* |

:::example Naming and judging evidence
A writer claims that planting trees keeps cities cool. Which kind of evidence is this, and is it strong?

> Measurements across one city found that streets lined with trees were up to 4 °C cooler on hot afternoons than streets without them.
@step The evidence is given in numbers (*4 °C*), from measurements: it is a statistic.
@step It is relevant, since it is about trees and temperature, and it is specific and can be checked.
@answer It is a **statistic**, and it is strong evidence for the claim.
:::

:::check e10-evidence-types 2
:::

Strong evidence is **relevant** (it is about the claim), **specific** (it gives details that can be checked) and **credible** (it comes from a trustworthy source). An anecdote can bring an argument to life, but one person’s story proves little on its own. “Everybody knows that reading is good for you” is not evidence at all: it is too vague to check.

:::check e10-evidence-types 3
:::

:::example Choosing the strongest evidence
A writer claims that regular exercise improves teenagers’ mood. Which piece of evidence supports the claim best?

1. *Running is the most boring sport there is.*
2. *In a study of 500 teenagers, those who exercised for an hour a day were half as likely to report feeling low.*
3. *Exercise is good for all kinds of things.*
@step Statement 1 is an opinion, and it is about running being boring, not about mood.
@step Statement 3 is too vague to check: good for which things, and how do we know?
@step Statement 2 is relevant (exercise and mood), specific (500 teenagers, an hour a day) and can be checked.
@answer Statement 2 is the strongest evidence.
:::

:::note Evidence in your own writing
When you write an argument, give each reason at least one piece of evidence, and say where the evidence comes from: “a survey of 300 students at our school” is more convincing than “a survey”.
:::

:::summary
- An argument has a claim, reasons that explain it, evidence that supports the reasons, and often a counterclaim and a rebuttal.
- Evidence is specific and can be checked; a reason is the general point the evidence supports.
- A fact can be checked and proved true or false; an opinion is a judgment people can disagree about.
- Evidence can be a statistic, an anecdote, expert testimony or an example. The strongest evidence is relevant, specific and credible.
:::
