*Walking to school in the storm, the umbrella blew inside out.* Read it literally and the umbrella was walking to school. Sentences like this make readers laugh at the wrong moment, and in an essay they make the writer look careless. In this lesson you will keep describing words next to what they describe, and you will give the items in a list or a comparison the same shape, which is called parallel structure.

## Modifiers

:::definition Modifier
A **modifier** is a word, phrase or clause that describes another word: *the cake **covered in candles***, ***Hoping to win**, Kwame trained every day*. A modifier describes the word it is closest to, or, if it opens the sentence, the subject that comes straight after the comma.
:::

Two errors happen when a modifier is in the wrong place or has nothing to describe.

:::definition Misplaced and dangling modifiers
A **misplaced modifier** is in the sentence with the word it describes, but too far from it, so it seems to describe something else: *The waiter brought a cake to the table **covered in candles**.* (Was the table covered in candles?)

A **dangling modifier** describes someone who is not in the sentence at all: ***While cooking dinner**, the smoke alarm went off.* (Who was cooking? Not the smoke alarm.)
:::

:::tip The comma test
When a sentence opens with a describing phrase such as *Walking home*, *To catch the train* or *Exhausted after the match*, look at the first noun or pronoun after the comma. It must be the person doing that action. If it is not, the modifier dangles.
:::

### Fixing modifier errors

A misplaced modifier is fixed by **moving it** next to the word it describes:

- *The waiter brought a cake covered in candles to the table.*
- *Ravi ate **almost** the whole pizza.* (Not *Ravi almost ate the whole pizza*, which means he nearly ate it but did not.)

A dangling modifier is fixed by **adding the missing person**, either as the subject after the comma or inside the opening phrase:

- *While cooking dinner, **Esi** heard the smoke alarm go off.*
- *While **Esi** was cooking dinner, the smoke alarm went off.*

:::example Fixing a dangling modifier
Fix: *After finishing the essay, the television was switched on.*
@step The opening phrase is *After finishing the essay*. Who finished it?
@step The first noun after the comma is *the television*, which did not finish an essay, and the person who did is missing.
@step Put the person in as the subject: *After finishing the essay, Noah switched on the television.*
@answer *After finishing the essay, Noah switched on the television.*
:::

:::mistake The passive does not rescue it
*After finishing the essay, the television was switched on **by Noah**.* Noah is now in the sentence, but the subject after the comma is still *the television*, so the modifier still dangles. The person doing the action must be the subject.
:::

## Parallel structure

:::definition Parallel structure
**Parallel structure** means giving items of equal importance the same grammatical form: all nouns, all adjectives, all *-ing* words, all *to* verbs, all clauses. It applies to lists, comparisons and pairs joined by *either … or*, *neither … nor*, *both … and* and *not only … but also*.
:::

Parallel sentences are easier to read and have a rhythm that makes them memorable. Compare:

- Not parallel: *In her free time, Leila enjoys hiking, swimming and to read mystery novels.*
- Parallel: *In her free time, Leila enjoys hiking, swimming and reading mystery novels.*

:::example Making a list parallel
Make this sentence parallel: *The job requires patience, honesty and that you work hard.*
@step Find the items in the list: *patience*, *honesty* and *that you work hard*.
@step The first two are nouns; the third is a clause.
@step Change the third item into a noun as well: *hard work*.
@answer *The job requires patience, honesty and hard work.*
:::

In a comparison, both sides take the same form: *It is better **to arrive** early than **to rush** at the last minute.* With paired words, what follows the first word must match what follows the second:

- *Daniel is not only **a talented singer** but also **a gifted songwriter**.*
- *Kenji wants to study either **medicine** or **law**.*

:::mistake Misplaced pair words
*Kenji either wants to study medicine or law* puts *either* before a verb but *or* before a noun, so the two halves do not match. Place each pair word just before the matching parts.
:::

:::summary
- A modifier describes the word it is next to; an opening phrase describes the subject after the comma.
- A misplaced modifier is too far from the word it describes: move it. A dangling modifier describes someone missing from the sentence: add that person as the subject.
- Parallel structure gives items in a list, a comparison or a pair the same grammatical form.
- With either…or, neither…nor, both…and and not only…but also, the parts after each word must match.
:::
