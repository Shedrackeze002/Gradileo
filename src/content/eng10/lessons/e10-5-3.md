An advertisement for running shoes shows a champion sprinter crossing a finish line in slow motion, a crowd roaring, and three words: *Run like them*. It never mentions the shoes’ price, their weight or how long they last. It sells a feeling instead. In this lesson you will learn the techniques advertisers use to persuade, and how to work out whom an advertisement is aimed at. Every product and person in this lesson’s advertisements is invented.

## Persuasive techniques

| Technique | How it persuades | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Bandwagon** | everyone else is doing it, so you should too | *The whole town is shopping at Greenleaf Market. Come and see why!* |
| **Testimonial** | a famous or expert person recommends the product | *Award-winning chef Luca Moretti says, “I cook with Golden Grove olive oil at home every day.”* |
| **Glittering generalities** | vague, attractive words that say nothing specific | *Pure. Natural. Freedom in every bottle.* |
| **Snob appeal** | the product is only for special or superior people | *Velvet Reserve watches: for the few who accept nothing less than perfection.* |
| **Plain folks** | the product or seller is ordinary and down-to-earth, like you | *At Miller’s Bakery, we’re just ordinary neighbours baking honest bread.* |
| **Fear** | something bad will happen if you do not buy | *Every night without a SafeGuard alarm, you are leaving your family at risk.* |
| **Statistics** | numbers make the product sound proven | *92 percent of users said GlowSkin made their skin feel softer.* |

:::example Analysing an advertisement
> Not everyone can own a Montclair. Can you?

Which technique does this advertisement use, and what does it leave out?
@step The advertisement suggests that only a few special people can own the product, and dares the reader to be one of them.
@step That is snob appeal: it makes the product feel exclusive.
@step It says nothing about what a Montclair is like: its quality, its price or why it is better than another.
@answer **Snob appeal.** Being exclusive does not make a product better, and the advertisement gives no facts at all.
:::

:::mistake Trusting the numbers
Statistics sound scientific, but ask where they came from. “Nine out of ten cat owners in our taste test” may mean ten owners, chosen by the company. “Up to 40 percent longer” may describe the best result, not the usual one.
:::

## The target audience

:::definition Target audience
An advertisement’s **target audience** is the group of people it is designed to reach. Advertisers choose the product details, words, images, music and even the time and place an advertisement appears to suit that group.
:::

Look for the words that speak directly to one group: their age (*Ages 13 to 17*), their worries (*Exams coming up?*), their stage of life (*first shoes for little feet*, *your golden years*) or their interests (*zero lag, custom buttons*).

> Stay sharp in your golden years. BrainBloom puzzle books keep your mind active, with large print that’s easy on the eyes.

“Golden years” is a gentle phrase for old age, and large print suits readers whose eyesight is changing, so this advertisement targets **older adults**.

:::tip Change the audience, change the advertisement
To see how much an advertisement depends on its audience, imagine selling the same product to someone else. Puzzle books for teenagers might boast about beating your friends’ scores on a phone app, not about large print.

## Reading an advertisement critically

When you meet an advertisement, ask five questions:

1. **Who made it, and what do they want?** Almost always, they want you to buy, join or vote.
2. **Who is it aimed at?** Look for the words and images that speak to one group.
3. **Which techniques does it use?** Name them: bandwagon, testimonial, fear and so on.
4. **What does it leave out?** Price, weaknesses, the size of the survey, what the famous person was paid.
5. **What would you need to know to decide for yourself?** Then look for that information from a source that does not gain from your choice.

:::example Putting it together
> Over a million teenagers have already switched to Zing Chat. Don’t be the last one left out!

Analyse the advertisement’s audience, technique and weakness.
@step **Audience:** “teenagers” names the group directly, and “left out” plays on a worry many teenagers share.
@step **Technique:** it tells the reader that everyone else is already using the app: the bandwagon technique.
@step **Weakness:** a million users says nothing about whether the app is safe, private or any good.
@answer Zing Chat targets **teenagers** with the **bandwagon** technique, but popularity is not evidence of quality.
:::

:::note Advertising you might not notice
Not every advertisement looks like one. A brand may pay for its product to appear in a film (**product placement**), pay an online personality to praise it (a **sponsored post**, often marked “ad”), or publish an article that looks like news but is written to sell (**native advertising**). The same five questions apply.
:::

:::summary
- Advertisements persuade with techniques such as bandwagon, testimonial, glittering generalities, snob appeal, plain folks, fear and statistics.
- Each technique leaves something out: popularity, fame, nice words and exclusivity are not evidence of quality, and numbers need a source.
- The target audience is the group an advertisement is designed to reach. Look for words about age, worries, stage of life and interests.
:::
