A café sign once read: “Try our desert special!” Customers may have wondered whether they were being offered a plate of sand. One letter changed the meaning. In this lesson you will choose between words that are easily confused, choose words for the feelings they carry and the situation you are writing for, and work out unfamiliar words from the sentences around them.

## Commonly confused words

Many confusions come from **homophones**, words that sound the same but have different spellings and meanings, and from contractions that sound like possessives.

| Word | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| their / there / they’re | belonging to them / that place / they are | *They’re leaving their bags over there.* |
| its / it’s | belonging to it / it is | *It’s clear the dog hurt its paw.* |
| your / you’re | belonging to you / you are | *You’re welcome to borrow your sister’s bike.* |
| whose / who’s | belonging to whom / who is | *Who’s at the door, and whose coat is this?* |
| then / than | time / comparison | *We ate, then we ran. Amara is taller than Kenji.* |
| affect / effect | verb: to influence / noun: a result | *The storm will affect the flight. It had a big effect.* |
| accept / except | to receive / not including | *Everyone except Omar accepted the invitation.* |
| lose / loose | to misplace or be beaten / not tight | *Don’t lose the loose screw.* |
| fewer / less | countable / not countable | *Fewer students, less noise.* |

:::tip Expand the contraction
If one of the choices has an apostrophe, say it in full: *they are*, *it is*, *you are*, *who is*. If the long form fits, use the apostrophe; if not, use the other word.
:::

Some pairs differ by a single letter, and a memory trick helps. **Dessert** has two s’s because you want a second helping; the **desert** has one because it has so little. **Stationery** with an e is for envelopes; **stationary** with an a means standing still. **Principal** is the head of a school, and the principal is your *pal*; a **principle** is a rule.

## Denotation and connotation

:::definition Denotation and connotation
A word’s **denotation** is its dictionary meaning. Its **connotation** is the feeling or judgement it carries. *Slender*, *thin* and *scrawny* all denote “not carrying much weight”, but *slender* sounds admiring, *thin* is neutral, and *scrawny* sounds unhealthy or unattractive.
:::

Writers choose words for their connotations to steer the reader. A character who will not change their mind can be *determined* (we admire them) or *stubborn* (we are annoyed by them). A neighbour who asks a lot of questions can be *inquisitive* or *nosy*.

:::example Choosing a word for its effect
A writer wants readers to think badly of a character’s decision: *Kwame’s decision to sail across the bay alone was (courageous / bold / reckless).*
@step All three words describe taking a risk.
@step *Courageous* approves of it, *bold* is fairly neutral, and *reckless* condemns it as foolish.
@answer *reckless*, the word with the negative connotation.
:::

## Register and tone

**Register** is how formal your language is. A message to a friend can be relaxed, but an essay, a report, a job application or a letter to a company calls for a **formal register**: complete sentences, precise words, no slang, and few or no contractions.

:::mistake Slipping into conversation
*The author totally goes for creepy vibes* may be how you would say it to a friend, but in a literary essay write *The author creates a sense of dread.* Words such as *totally*, *kind of*, *basically*, *super* and *a ton of* belong to conversation, not formal writing.
:::

## Vocabulary in context

You do not need a dictionary for every unfamiliar word. The sentence around it often gives a **context clue**.

:::definition Five kinds of context clue
- **Definition:** the sentence explains the word. *The hikers reached the **summit**, the highest point of the mountain.*
- **Synonym:** a nearby word means the same. *The puppy was **lethargic**; it was sluggish and slept all day.*
- **Antonym:** a contrast word (*but*, *unlike*, *instead of*, *although*) points to the opposite. *The first chapter was **tedious**, but the second was thrilling.*
- **Example:** examples follow *such as*, *including* or a colon. *Many **nocturnal** animals, such as owls and bats, hunt in the dark.*
- **Inference:** the situation as a whole tells you. *After the marathon, Ruth was so **famished** that she ate three plates of rice.*
:::

### Word parts

Many English words are built from Latin and Greek parts. Knowing a few unlocks hundreds of words.

| Part | Meaning | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| un-, in-, im- | not | unbreakable, invisible, impossible |
| mis- | wrongly | misunderstand, misplace |
| pre- | before | prehistoric, preview |
| re- | again, back | rewrite, rebuild |
| bene- | good, well | benefactor, benefit |
| mal- | bad, badly | malfunction, malnourished |
| chron | time | chronological, chronicle |
| port | carry | portable, import |
| spect | look | spectator, inspect |
| -ology | the study of | biology, geology |

:::example Using word parts
What does **malfunction** mean in *A malfunction in the engine delayed the flight*?
@step Split the word: *mal-* (badly) + *function* (work).
@step A part that “works badly” would explain a delay.
@answer A failure to work properly.
:::

:::summary
- Commonly confused words differ in meaning, not just spelling; expand contractions to test them.
- Denotation is a word’s dictionary meaning; connotation is the feeling it carries. Choose words whose connotation fits the effect you want.
- Use a formal register, without slang, for essays, reports and formal letters.
- Work out unfamiliar words from definition, synonym, antonym, example and inference clues, and from prefixes and roots.
:::
