A film opens on a vast, empty ocean. Slowly the camera moves closer, until a tiny boat appears, and then a single face, staring at the horizon. No one has said a word, yet you already know that this person is alone, far from help, and afraid. Film-makers tell stories with the camera, with sound, with editing and with light. In this lesson you will learn the names of these techniques and, more importantly, what each one does to an audience.

## Shots: how much we see

A **shot** is one continuous piece of film between two cuts. Shots are named by how much of the scene they show.

| Shot | What it shows | Usual effect |
|---|---|---|
| **Extreme long shot** | a vast area; people tiny or not visible | establishes the setting; can make people seem small and alone |
| **Long shot** | a character’s whole body and some surroundings | shows the character in their situation |
| **Medium shot** | a character from about the waist up | shows faces and gestures, like a conversation |
| **Close-up** | a face filling most of the screen | shows emotion; draws us close to the character |
| **Extreme close-up** | one small detail: an eye, a hand, a key | focuses all attention on one detail; builds tension |

A film often begins with an extreme long shot, called an **establishing shot**, to show where the story takes place, then moves closer as it introduces the characters.

## Angles and movement

The **camera angle** is where the camera is placed in relation to the subject.

- **High angle:** the camera looks down on the subject, which makes it seem small, weak or vulnerable.
- **Low angle:** the camera looks up at the subject, which makes it seem large, powerful or threatening.
- **Eye level:** the camera is at the height of the character’s eyes, which feels natural and neutral.
- **Bird’s-eye view:** the camera looks straight down from directly above, like a map.

The camera can also move:

- A **pan** turns the camera left or right from a fixed position, taking in a scene bit by bit.
- A **tilt** turns the camera up or down from a fixed position, often to reveal height.
- A **zoom** changes the lens so that the subject seems to come closer or move away, while the camera stays still.
- A **tracking shot** moves the camera along with the subject, on rails or a vehicle.

:::example Reading a shot
> The villain is filmed from a low angle as he steps out of the shadows.

What effect does this angle have on the audience?
@step In a low-angle shot, the camera looks up at the subject.
@step Looking up at someone makes them seem taller and stronger than we are.
@answer The villain seems **large, powerful and threatening**, and the audience feels small beside him.
:::

:::mistake Naming a technique without its effect
“The director uses a close-up” is only half an answer. Say what the close-up does: “The close-up shows the fear in her eyes, so we feel her panic with her.”
:::

## Sound

:::definition Diegetic and non-diegetic sound
**Diegetic sound** comes from inside the world of the story: dialogue, footsteps, a car radio, rain on the roof. The characters can hear it. **Non-diegetic sound** is added for the audience only: the background music score, a narrator’s voice-over, a dramatic chord. The characters cannot hear it.
:::

A band playing at a wedding in the film is diegetic, because the guests dance to it. Sad violin music that swells as two friends say goodbye at an airport, with no musicians anywhere nearby, is non-diegetic: it is there to tell the audience how to feel.

## Editing and lighting

**Editing** is how shots are joined together.

- A **cut** is an instant change from one shot to the next.
- **Cross-cutting** switches back and forth between two scenes happening at the same time, which builds suspense: will the hero arrive in time?
- A **montage** is a series of short shots edited together to show time passing or something changing, such as weeks of training in a few seconds.
- A **fade to black** slowly darkens the picture until the screen is black, often to close a scene or show an ending.

**Lighting** sets the mood. **High-key lighting** is bright and even, with few shadows, and suits comedies and cheerful scenes. **Low-key lighting** is dark, with strong shadows and small pools of light, and suits mysteries, thrillers and horror.

:::tip Ask “why here?”
When you notice a technique, ask why the director used it at this moment. A sudden cut in a quiet scene jolts us; a slow fade lets a sad ending settle.

:::summary
- Shots range from the extreme long shot, which shows a whole landscape, to the extreme close-up, which shows one detail.
- A high angle makes a subject seem weak; a low angle makes it seem powerful. A pan, tilt, zoom or tracking shot moves our view.
- Diegetic sound comes from the story world; non-diegetic sound, like the music score, is for the audience only.
- Cuts, cross-cutting, montages and fades join shots; high-key lighting feels cheerful and low-key lighting feels mysterious.
- Always explain the effect of a technique, not just its name.
:::
