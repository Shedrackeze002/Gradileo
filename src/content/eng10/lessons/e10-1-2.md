In O. Henry’s “The Gift of the Magi”, a young wife named Della has decided to sell the thing she is proudest of, her long hair, to buy her husband a Christmas present. O. Henry never writes “Della was torn”. He shows us instead:

> And then she did it up again nervously and quickly. Once she faltered for a minute and stood still while a tear or two splashed on the worn red carpet.
>
> — O. Henry, “The Gift of the Magi” (1905)

The pause, the hurry and the tears tell us more than any label could. In this lesson you will learn how writers build characters, the kinds of character a story contains, and how to read a character’s traits from the evidence.

## Direct and indirect characterization

**Characterization** is the way a writer reveals what a character is like.

:::definition Direct and indirect characterization
In **direct characterization**, the narrator or another character states a trait outright: “Ana was generous.” In **indirect characterization**, the writer shows the character through details, and the reader works out the trait.
:::

:::note Spoiler
The next quotation is the last line of “The Open Window”.
:::

Saki characterizes Vera, the teenager at the heart of “The Open Window”, directly in the story’s last line:

> Romance at short notice was her speciality.
>
> — Saki, “The Open Window” (1914)

(Here “romance” means invented, adventurous stories.) Most characterization, though, is indirect. A useful way to remember the five kinds is the word **STEAL**:

| Letter | Method | Ask yourself |
|---|---|---|
| S | **Speech** | What does the character say, and how? |
| T | **Thoughts** | What does the character think and feel? |
| E | **Effect on others** | How do other people react to the character? |
| A | **Actions** | What does the character do? |
| L | **Looks** | How does the character appear: face, clothes, manner? |

When Della reaches the shop that buys hair, she wastes no words: “Give it to me quick,” she says. That is speech, and it shows a woman who acts the moment she has made up her mind.

:::mistake A trait word is not always direct characterization
When O. Henry writes that Jim “looked thin and very serious”, he is describing how Jim **looks**, and we infer from it that Jim is poor and burdened. Direct characterization names a trait of personality as a fact: “Jim was generous.” If you have to work out the trait yourself, the characterization is indirect.
:::

## Kinds of character

:::definition Roles in a story
The **protagonist** is the main character, whose goal or problem the story follows. The **antagonist** works against the protagonist; it is usually a person, though some writers treat a force such as nature as the antagonist. A **foil** is a character whose contrasting traits make the protagonist’s traits stand out.
:::

:::definition Round or flat, static or dynamic
A **round** character is complex, with many traits, some of them contradictory. A **flat** character has only one or two traits. A **dynamic** character changes in an important way during the story; a **static** character stays the same.
:::

Minor characters are often flat and static, and that is not a weakness: a story of a few pages has no room to develop everyone. The protagonist of a good short story, though, is usually round, and often dynamic.

:::example Is Mrs. Mallard static or dynamic?
In Kate Chopin’s “The Story of an Hour”, Louise Mallard has just learned that her husband is dead. Alone in her room, she begins to feel something she did not expect. Read the passage and decide whether she is a static or a dynamic character.

> She breathed a quick prayer that life might be long. It was only yesterday she had thought with a shudder that life might be long.
>
> — Kate Chopin, “The Story of an Hour” (1894)
@step A dynamic character changes in an important way; a static character does not.
@step Yesterday, the thought of a long life made her shudder. Now she prays for one.
@step Her whole feeling about her own life has turned around within an hour.
@answer Mrs. Mallard is a dynamic character: her attitude to her own life changes completely. She is also round, since the story shows her grief, her fear and her joy.
:::

## Inferring a character’s traits

When characterization is indirect, you make an **inference**: a conclusion based on evidence and reasoning. A good inference is one the details actually support, not one that is merely possible.

> “The doctors agree in ordering me complete rest, an absence of mental excitement, and avoidance of anything in the nature of violent physical exercise,” announced Framton, who laboured under the tolerably widespread delusion that total strangers and chance acquaintances are hungry for the least detail of one’s ailments and infirmities, their cause and cure.
>
> — Saki, “The Open Window” (1914)

Framton tells a girl he has only just met all about his health, and the narrator mocks his belief that strangers want to hear it. We can infer that he is wrapped up in himself and his illnesses. We cannot infer that he is unkind or dishonest: nothing in the passage points that way.

:::tip Evidence, then trait, then explanation
Build every inference in three steps: quote or describe the detail, name the trait, and explain how the detail shows it. “Framton describes his illnesses to a stranger (detail), which suggests he is self-absorbed (trait), because he assumes others care about his health as much as he does (explanation).”
:::

:::mistake Choosing a trait the evidence does not support
A character who counts their money carefully could, in theory, also be generous or kind, but the detail does not show that. Choose the trait the evidence points to most strongly, and be ready to quote the detail that proves it.
:::

:::summary
- Direct characterization states a trait; indirect characterization shows it through speech, thoughts, effect on others, actions and looks (STEAL).
- The protagonist drives the story, the antagonist works against them, and a foil sets off the protagonist by contrast.
- Round characters are complex and flat characters simple; dynamic characters change and static characters do not.
- An inference about a character must be supported by a detail from the text: detail, trait, explanation.
:::
