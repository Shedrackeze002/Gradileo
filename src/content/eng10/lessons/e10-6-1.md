A friend asks what you thought of a film. “It was good” ends the conversation. “It reminded me of the year my family moved, and I finally understood why my little brother was so angry that summer” starts a real one. A **personal response** to a text works the same way: it tells the reader what the text made you think, feel or realize, and why. In this lesson you will tell a response from a summary, make connections that matter, and build a response around one clear controlling idea.

## Response, not summary

:::definition Personal response
A **personal response** explores your own thinking about a text: what it made you feel, what it reminded you of, what it made you realize or question. It refers to the text, but its subject is the meeting between the text and you.
:::

A **summary** only reports what happens. Your reader has usually read the story too, so retelling it adds nothing. Compare two sentences about “The Gift of the Magi”:

- *Della sells her long hair for twenty dollars so that she can buy Jim a chain for his watch.* This is a summary: every word is about the story.
- *When my cousin sold her bike to pay for my birthday trip, I understood Della at last: a gift means most when the giver has given up something they love.* This is a response: it brings in the writer’s own life and says what the writer learned.

:::tip The “I” test
A response usually contains *I*, *me* or *my*, because it is about your thinking. But *I* alone is not enough: “I think the story is about Della selling her hair” is still a summary with *I* in front of it.
:::

## Connections that matter

Most responses grow from a **connection** between the text and something else:

- **text to self**: an experience from your own life;
- **text to world**: something happening in your community or the wider world;
- **text to text**: another story, poem, film or song.

A connection on its own is not yet a response. “This story reminded me of my cousin” leaves the reader asking, *so what?* A strong connection does two jobs: it links something **specific** in the text to something specific in your life, and it says **why the link matters**: what it showed you about the text, about yourself or about people.

:::example Building a connection that matters
Turn this weak connection about “The Monkey’s Paw” into a strong one: *I have also had a wish that came true.*
@step Name something specific in the story: the Whites wish for two hundred pounds, and the money arrives as compensation for their son’s death.
@step Name something specific from your own life that it connects to: the phone you begged for, which then took over your evenings.
@step Say what the link shows: getting what you want can cost more than you expected.
@answer *The Whites’ first wish reminded me of the time I begged for a phone and then spent every evening glued to it instead of talking to my family; getting what you want can cost more than you expect.*
:::

:::mistake The connection that wanders off
A personal story can grow so long that the text disappears. If a paragraph about your grandmother’s cooking never comes back to the story, it has stopped being a response. Keep returning to a specific moment in the text, and keep asking what the connection shows.
:::

:::tip Questions that start a response
If you are not sure what to write about, ask yourself:
- Which moment in the text surprised me, moved me or annoyed me, and why?
- Which character would I most like to argue with, and what would I say?
- What does this text make me see differently about my own life or the world around me?
:::

## The controlling idea

:::definition Controlling idea
The **controlling idea** of a personal response is the one insight that holds it together: a complete sentence saying what the text made you think or realize. Every paragraph explores it. In an analytical essay, the same job is done by the thesis.
:::

A topic is not a controlling idea. “Freedom” is a topic. “Mrs. Mallard’s hour of freedom made me realize that even people who love us can stop us from living our own lives” is a controlling idea: it names the topic, says something about it, and links the text to the writer’s own thinking.

:::example A controlling idea for “To Build a Fire”
Write a controlling idea for a personal response to “To Build a Fire”, in which a man ignores an old-timer’s warning about the cold and dies.
@step Find the moment that struck you: the man ignoring advice because he is sure he knows better.
@step Ask what it made you think about in your own life: the times you have ignored good advice.
@step Put the two together as one complete sentence.
@answer *The man’s death made me think about how often I ignore good advice simply because I think I already know better.*
:::

Here is a body paragraph built on that controlling idea, written for Gradileo:

> Last spring I went hiking with only one small bottle of water because I was sure I knew the trail. Like the man in “To Build a Fire”, who brushes aside the old-timer’s warning never to travel alone in such cold, I trusted my own confidence more than other people’s experience. The man pays with his life; I only paid with a pounding headache and a long, slow walk home. But the story made me see the same mistake in both of us: we treated a warning as an insult instead of as information.

The paragraph keeps returning to the story, compares a specific moment with a specific experience, and ends by saying what the comparison shows.

## Shaping the whole response

A personal response is usually organized like any other essay:

1. **Introduction**: name the text and its author, and state your controlling idea.
2. **Body paragraphs**: each one explores one connection or moment, with a detail or short quotation from the text, your own experience or example, and a reflection on what it shows.
3. **Conclusion**: return to the controlling idea and say how your thinking has changed or grown.

:::note Your own voice
A personal response may use *I* and may sound like you, but it is still careful writing: complete sentences, a clear order and correct punctuation. Thoughtful does not mean formal; it means honest and specific.
:::

:::summary
- A personal response explores what a text made you think, feel or realize; a summary only retells what happens.
- A strong connection links something specific in the text to something specific in your life or the world, and says why the link matters.
- A controlling idea is a complete sentence stating the insight the whole response explores; a topic word is not enough.
- Keep returning to the text: every paragraph should connect a moment in it to your thinking.
:::
