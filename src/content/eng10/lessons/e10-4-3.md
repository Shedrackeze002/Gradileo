On 19 November 1863, at the dedication of a cemetery for soldiers killed at Gettysburg, Pennsylvania, Abraham Lincoln spoke for about two minutes. His speech had fewer than three hundred words, and it ends:

> … and that government of the people, by the people, for the people, shall not perish from the earth.
>
> — Abraham Lincoln, the Gettysburg Address (1863)

Why are those words still quoted more than a century and a half later? Part of the answer is **rhetoric**, the art of persuading with language. In this lesson you will learn the three appeals that persuasive writers use, and the devices that make their words memorable.

## Ethos, pathos and logos

More than two thousand years ago, the Greek philosopher Aristotle described three ways a speaker can persuade an audience. We still use his names for them.

:::definition The three appeals
- **Ethos** is an appeal to the speaker’s credibility or character: *trust me, because I know this subject and I am honest*.
- **Pathos** is an appeal to emotion: pity, fear, anger, pride or hope.
- **Logos** is an appeal to logic: facts, numbers, evidence and reasoning.
:::

Imagine a student asking the town to give more money to its animal shelter. Each appeal sounds different:

- **Ethos:** *I have volunteered at the shelter every weekend for six years, so I know its work first-hand.*
- **Pathos:** *Picture a frightened puppy, shivering in a cold cage, waiting for someone to love it.*
- **Logos:** *The shelter takes in about 900 animals a year but has only 40 kennels, so it has to turn animals away almost every week.*

Strong speakers combine all three. In his Independence Day speech of 1852, Frederick Douglass appealed to logic with a fact about the law of the time:

> There are seventy-two crimes in the state of Virginia, which, if committed by a black man (no matter how ignorant he be), subject him to the punishment of death; while only two of these same crimes will subject a white man to the like punishment.
>
> — Frederick Douglass, “What to the Slave Is the Fourth of July?” (1852)

The numbers, seventy-two against two, make the injustice impossible to deny. Earlier in the same speech, he had appealed just as powerfully to emotion:

> Fellow-citizens, above your national, tumultous joy, I hear the mournful wail of millions, whose chains, heavy and grievous yesterday, are to-day rendered more intolerable by the jubilant shouts that reach them.
>
> — Frederick Douglass, “What to the Slave Is the Fourth of July?” (1852)

The “mournful wail of millions”, set against the “jubilant shouts” of the celebration, is meant to make the audience feel grief and shame. (Douglass’s printed text spells *tumultuous* as “tumultous”, and *today* as “to-day”, as people often did then.)

:::mistake Choosing an appeal by its topic
An appeal is named by *how* it persuades, not by what it is about. A sentence about a sad subject can still be logos if it persuades with numbers; a sentence full of facts about the speaker’s career is ethos, because it asks you to trust the speaker.
:::

## Rhetorical devices

Rhetorical devices are patterns of language that make an argument striking and easy to remember.

:::definition Rhetorical devices
- A **rhetorical question** is asked for effect; its answer is meant to be obvious.
- **Repetition (anaphora)** repeats the same words at the start of several clauses or sentences.
- **Parallelism** puts several ideas in the same grammatical form, so they sound balanced.
- A **tricolon** is a series of three parallel words, phrases or clauses.
- **Antithesis** sets two opposite ideas side by side in a balanced sentence.
- **Hyperbole** is deliberate, obvious exaggeration.
- An **allusion** is a brief reference to a well-known person, story, event or text.
:::

The Gettysburg Address uses almost all of them. Lincoln repeats *we can not* three times:

> But, in a larger sense, we can not dedicate—we can not consecrate—we can not hallow—this ground.
>
> — Abraham Lincoln, the Gettysburg Address (1863)

He balances opposites in an antithesis, *what we say* against *what they did*:

> The world will little note, nor long remember what we say here, but it can never forget what they did here.
>
> — Abraham Lincoln, the Gettysburg Address (1863)

And his opening line is an allusion. “All men are created equal” echoes the Declaration of Independence of 1776, and “four score and seven years ago”, eighty-seven years, points back to that year:

> Four score and seven years ago our fathers brought forth, on this continent, a new nation, conceived in Liberty, and dedicated to the proposition that all men are created equal.
>
> — Abraham Lincoln, the Gettysburg Address (1863)

:::example Naming a device
> For it is not light that is needed, but fire; it is not the gentle shower, but thunder.
>
> — Frederick Douglass, “What to the Slave Is the Fourth of July?” (1852)

Which rhetorical device does Douglass use?
@step Look at the shape of the sentence: *not light … but fire*, *not the gentle shower, but thunder*.
@step Each half sets one idea against its opposite: gentle against violent, light against fire.
@answer **Antithesis**: Douglass sets opposite ideas side by side to say that gentle argument is no longer enough.
:::

:::tip Devices overlap
One sentence can use several devices at once. “Of the people, by the people, for the people” is a tricolon, it is parallel, and it repeats *the people*. When a question asks which device a passage uses, look for the one that stands out most, and read every choice before you decide.
:::

:::summary
- Ethos persuades through the speaker’s credibility, pathos through emotion and logos through facts and reasoning. The best arguments use all three.
- Name an appeal by *how* it persuades, not by its topic.
- Rhetorical devices make words memorable: rhetorical questions, repetition, parallelism, tricolon, antithesis, hyperbole and allusion.
- The Gettysburg Address and Douglass’s speech of 1852 show these devices at work in some of the most famous speeches in English.
:::
