Two newspapers report the same event. One headline reads: *Hundreds Gather to Protest New Tax*. The other reads: *Angry Mob Swarms City Hall*. Both stories are about the same people, standing in the same place, on the same afternoon, yet they leave readers with very different pictures. In this lesson you will learn to spot **faulty reasoning**, recognize **bias**, and decide which sources you can trust.

## Logical fallacies

:::definition Logical fallacy
A **logical fallacy** is a flaw in reasoning that makes an argument seem stronger than it really is. Fallacies are common in advertising, politics and everyday arguments, because they often work on people who do not notice them.
:::

| Fallacy | What goes wrong | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Ad hominem** | attacks the person, not the argument | *Why listen to Ravi’s plan for the science club? He failed a chemistry test.* |
| **Bandwagon** | says something is right because many people believe or do it | *Millions have downloaded this diet app, so it must work.* |
| **False dilemma** | offers only two choices when there are more | *Either you join the football team, or you don’t care about our school.* |
| **Hasty generalization** | draws a broad conclusion from too few examples | *I met two rude people from that town, so everyone there must be rude.* |
| **Slippery slope** | claims, without evidence, that one step will lead to disaster | *If we allow one food truck, soon the restaurants will close and the town centre will die.* |
| **Straw man** | twists the other side’s view into a weaker one, then attacks that | *Amara wants more vegetarian meals, so she wants to ban meat completely.* |
| **Appeal to a false authority** | trusts someone famous who has no expertise in the subject | *A famous footballer says this drink cures colds, so it must work.* |

:::check e10-fallacies 1
:::

:::example Finding the fallacy
> Kenji suggests closing Main Street to cars for one Sunday a month. Apparently he wants to ban cars from the whole town.

Which fallacy does this argument contain?
@step Compare what Kenji actually suggested with what the speaker attacks.
@step Kenji suggested one street, one day a month. The speaker attacks a ban on cars in the whole town, a far more extreme idea that Kenji never proposed.
@answer A **straw man**: the speaker twists Kenji’s modest proposal into an extreme one that is easier to attack.
:::

:::mistake Calling every bad argument a fallacy
An argument you disagree with is not automatically a fallacy. “The bridge has failed two safety inspections, so engineers recommend closing it for repairs” is reasonable: its conclusion follows from relevant evidence. Name a fallacy only when you can point to the flaw in the reasoning.
:::

## Bias and loaded language

:::definition Bias
**Bias** is a leaning toward one side that prevents a fair, balanced view. A biased text may still contain true facts, but it chooses and presents them to push the reader one way.
:::

The clearest sign of bias is **loaded language**: words that carry strong positive or negative feelings. *Gathered* is neutral; *swarmed* and *mobbed* make the same people sound dangerous. *Criticized* is neutral; *slammed* and *savaged* add drama and judgment.

| Neutral | Loaded |
|---|---|
| gathered | swarmed, mobbed, invaded |
| criticized | slammed, savaged, tore apart |
| reported | was forced to confess, finally admitted |
| rose | skyrocketed, exploded |

:::check e10-bias-sources 1
:::

Other signs of bias are harder to see, because they are about what a text leaves out:

- **One-sided sources.** An article about a new factory that quotes only the company that wants to build it.
- **Missing facts.** A report on a bus service that describes the three late days and leaves out the sixty days on time.
- **Words out of context.** A quotation cut short so that it seems to mean something else.
- **A hidden interest.** A review paid for by the company whose product it praises.

## Is this source reliable?

Before you trust a source, ask four questions:

1. **Who made it?** Does the writer or organization have real expertise in the subject?
2. **What is the evidence?** Does it give facts, data and sources that can be checked?
3. **When was it made?** Is it recent enough for the question you are asking?
4. **Why was it made?** Does it gain anything from persuading you, such as money or votes?

:::example Choosing the most reliable source
You want to know how many hours of sleep teenagers need. Which source is most reliable?

1. A blog post by a mattress company that sells beds for teenagers
2. A recent report by a national association of children’s doctors that reviews dozens of sleep studies
3. A magazine article about sleep from fifty years ago
@step The mattress company profits from telling you about sleep: its purpose is to sell.
@step The magazine article is fifty years old, so it may be out of date.
@step The doctors’ report comes from experts, rests on many studies and is recent.
@answer Source 2, the doctors’ report, is the most reliable.
:::

:::tip Check more than one source
Even a good source can be wrong. When something matters, check whether other reliable sources, written independently, agree.
:::

:::

:::summary
- A logical fallacy is a flaw in reasoning: ad hominem, bandwagon, false dilemma, hasty generalization, slippery slope, straw man and appeal to a false authority are common.
- Bias is a leaning to one side. Look for loaded language, one-sided sources, missing facts, words out of context and hidden interests.
- Judge a source by who made it, its evidence, when it was made and why.
:::
