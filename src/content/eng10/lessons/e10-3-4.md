At dawn, in the churchyard, the Prince stands over the bodies of Romeo, Juliet and Paris, and speaks to the two fathers:

> Where be these enemies? Capulet, Montague,\
> See what a scourge is laid upon your hate,\
> That heaven finds means to kill your joys with love!\
> And I, for winking at your discords too,\
> Have lost a brace of kinsmen. All are punish’d.
>
> — Prince, *Romeo and Juliet*, Act 5, Scene 3

“All are punish’d”: the feud has cost every family in Verona. Lines like these point to the play’s **themes**, the ideas about life that it explores. In this lesson you will state those themes as complete ideas, and support claims about the play with well-chosen quotations.

## The big ideas

A play as rich as *Romeo and Juliet* has several themes, and they are tangled together.

| Topic | What the play suggests | A line that shows it |
|---|---|---|
| Hatred and the feud | Hatred passed down through families destroys the innocent along with the guilty. | “A plague o’ both your houses.” (Mercutio, Act 3, Scene 1) |
| Love | Love can grow across the deepest divisions. | “My only love sprung from my only hate!” (Juliet, Act 1, Scene 5) |
| Haste | Acting in a rush, without thinking, can lead to disaster. | “Wisely and slow; they stumble that run fast.” (Friar Lawrence, Act 2, Scene 3) |
| Fate and chance | Bad luck can overturn the most careful plans. | “O, I am fortune’s fool!” (Romeo, Act 3, Scene 1) |
| Parents and children | When the old will not listen to the young, the young may turn to desperate measures. | “hang, beg, starve, die in the streets” (Capulet, Act 3, Scene 5) |
| Violence | Violence breeds more violence. | “And fire-ey’d fury be my conduct now!” (Romeo, Act 3, Scene 1) |

:::tip Fate or choice?
Is the tragedy caused by fate or by the characters’ choices? The play supports both readings. The Prologue calls the lovers “star-cross’d”, and a lost letter seals their deaths; but they also marry in haste, Romeo kills Tybalt in a rage, and the families keep their feud alive. The strongest essays weigh both sides, with evidence for each.
:::

## Writing a theme statement

:::definition Theme statement
A **theme statement** is a complete sentence that says what the play suggests about life or people in general. A topic (“hatred”) is not a theme; a theme says something about the topic: “Hatred passed down through families destroys the innocent.”
:::

A strong theme statement is general (no character names), supported by the play, and free of orders (“Never marry in a hurry”) and absolutes (“Feuds always end in death”).

:::example Writing a theme statement about haste
Write a theme statement about haste in *Romeo and Juliet*.
@step Collect the evidence: the lovers marry within a day of meeting; Juliet calls their vows “too rash, too unadvis’d, too sudden”; Friar Lawrence warns, “Wisely and slow; they stumble that run fast”; Capulet moves the wedding forward a day.
@step Ask what the play suggests: each rushed decision brings disaster closer, and there is never time to stop and think.
@step Turn the idea into a general statement, with no names, orders or absolutes.
@answer Acting in haste, without stopping to think, can lead to disaster.
:::

:::mistake Summaries and morals are not themes
“Romeo and Juliet marry the day after they meet, and everything goes wrong” retells the plot. “Get to know someone well before you marry them” is an order. “Every quick decision ends in tragedy” overgeneralizes. None of these is a theme statement.

## Using quotations as evidence

When you write about a play, every claim needs **evidence**, and the best evidence is a short quotation followed by your **explanation** of what it shows. A quotation that comes from the right scene, or from the right character, is not enough on its own: it must prove the claim.

:::definition Claim, quotation, explanation
1. **Claim**: what you are arguing. *At first, Capulet wants Juliet to have a say in her marriage.*
2. **Quotation**: the words that prove it, with the act and scene. *He tells Paris, “My will to her consent is but a part” (Act 1, Scene 2).*
3. **Explanation**: how the words prove the claim. *Capulet’s own wish is only “a part” of the decision: Juliet’s consent matters too.*
:::

:::example Choosing the best evidence
A student claims: “Capulet comes to treat Juliet as his property.” Which quotation proves it best: “My child is yet a stranger in the world” (Act 1, Scene 2), or “And you be mine, I’ll give you to my friend” (Act 3, Scene 5)?
@step Test the first quotation: Capulet is saying that Juliet is too young to marry. That shows care for her, not ownership.
@step Test the second: “I’ll give you to my friend” speaks of Juliet as something to hand over, and “And you be mine” (if you are mine) makes her place in the family depend on obeying him.
@answer The second quotation proves the claim. The first is about Capulet, but it shows the opposite side of him: it would suit a claim about how much he changes.
:::

The play ends with the Prince’s judgement, which names its own subject:

> For never was a story of more woe\
> Than this of Juliet and her Romeo.
>
> — Prince, *Romeo and Juliet*, Act 5, Scene 3

:::summary
- The themes of *Romeo and Juliet* include hatred and the feud, love, haste, fate and chance, parents and children, and violence.
- A theme statement is a complete, general sentence about life that the play supports: not a topic, a plot summary, an order or an absolute.
- Support each claim with a short quotation, its act and scene, and an explanation of how the words prove the claim.
- Choose evidence that proves the claim directly; a famous line, or a line from the right character, may still prove nothing.
:::
