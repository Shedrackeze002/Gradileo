No one writes a perfect first draft. Professional writers expect to change their work many times, and the difference between a rough draft and a strong essay is usually made after the first draft is finished. In this lesson you will connect ideas with transitions, cut words that do no work, and keep each paragraph focused on one idea.

## Revising and editing

:::definition Revising and editing
**Revising** means re-seeing the draft: improving the ideas, the order, the evidence and the connections between paragraphs. **Editing** means fixing the sentences: wordiness, word choice, grammar, spelling and punctuation. Revise first; there is no point polishing a sentence you are about to cut.
:::

:::tip Read it aloud
Read your draft aloud, slowly. Your ear catches what your eye skips: a sentence that runs out of breath, a word repeated three times, a jump between ideas with nothing to connect them.
:::

## Transitions

A **transition** is a word or phrase that shows how one idea relates to the next. Without transitions, a paragraph reads like a list of separate facts; with the right ones, the reader can follow your thinking.

| Relationship | Transitions |
|---|---|
| Contrast | however, even so, nevertheless, on the other hand |
| Cause and effect | as a result, therefore, consequently, for this reason |
| Addition | in addition, also, what is more |
| Example | for example, for instance |
| Sequence (time order) | first, next, then, after that, finally |
| Conclusion | in conclusion, overall, in short |

:::example Choosing a transition
Join these sentences with a transition: *The forecast promised sunshine. It rained all afternoon.*
@step Ask how the second idea relates to the first. The rain goes against what the forecast led us to expect.
@step That is contrast, so choose a contrast transition.
@answer *The forecast promised sunshine. However, it rained all afternoon.*
:::

:::mistake A transition that says the wrong thing
*The bridge was closed for repairs. However, we had to take the long way round.* “However” promises a contrast, but the second sentence is a result of the first. A wrong transition is worse than none, because it misleads the reader. Here the writer needs *As a result*.
:::

## Cutting wordiness

Concise writing says everything it needs to in as few words as possible. Every extra word makes the reader work harder for the same meaning.

- **Empty phrases**: *due to the fact that* (because), *at this point in time* (now), *in the event that* (if), *for the purpose of* (for), *it is important to note that* (cut it).
- **Redundant pairs**, where one word already contains the other: *final outcome*, *past history*, *free gift*, *return back*, *join together*, *unexpected surprise*, *new innovation*.
- **Needless repetition**: *The play was performed by the drama club, and the play was enjoyed by the audience.*

:::example Making a sentence concise
Revise: *Due to the fact that it was raining, we made the decision to postpone the match until a later date.*
@step *Due to the fact that* means *because*.
@step *Made the decision to postpone* means *postponed*.
@step *Until a later date* adds nothing: postponing already means moving to a later date.
@answer *Because it was raining, we postponed the match.*
:::

:::mistake Cutting the meaning too
Concise does not mean as short as possible. *We postponed the match* is shorter still, but it has lost the reason. Cut the padding, never the information.
:::

## Unity and order

:::definition Unity and coherence
A paragraph has **unity** when every sentence supports its topic sentence. It has **coherence** when the sentences come in a sensible order and are linked, so the reader moves easily from one to the next.
:::

To check unity, reread the topic sentence, then ask of every other sentence: *does this support that idea?* A sentence that is true and interesting but off the topic still has to go. In a paragraph about how cycling to school benefits students, *My uncle has a red bicycle with a broken bell* is about bicycles, but it supports nothing.

A good topic sentence covers everything in the paragraph: not just one detail, not something so general that it could introduce any paragraph, and never the opposite of what the paragraph shows.

:::tip Follow the time words
When a paragraph tells events in order, its time words (days, months, years, *first*, *later*, *by noon*) are a map. A sentence that is out of place usually shows itself by breaking that map.

:::summary
- Revise the ideas, order and evidence first; then edit the sentences.
- Choose transitions that show the real relationship between ideas: contrast, cause and effect, addition, example, sequence or conclusion.
- Cut empty phrases, redundant pairs and needless repetition, but never cut information.
- Every sentence in a paragraph should support its topic sentence, in an order the reader can follow.
:::
