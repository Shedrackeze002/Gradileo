Picture three texts about the same river. A news report says the river flooded two streets last night. A poster urges everyone to join Saturday’s clean-up. A travel journal paints the river at dawn, silver and still under the mist. The subject is the same, but each writer wants something different from you, and each is writing for different readers. In this lesson you will work out a text’s **purpose** and its **audience**, the first two questions to ask of any non-fiction text.

## Why was this written?

:::definition Purpose
A text’s **purpose** is what the writer wants it to do: what the reader should know, think, feel or do after reading it.
:::

Most non-fiction has one of five main purposes:

| Purpose | The writer wants the reader to … | Typical texts |
|---|---|---|
| **Inform** | know or understand the facts | news reports, encyclopedia entries, notices |
| **Persuade** | change what they think or do | editorials, speeches, advertisements, petitions |
| **Entertain** | enjoy, laugh or be gripped | stories, comic strips, funny anecdotes |
| **Instruct** | carry out a task, step by step | recipes, manuals, safety cards |
| **Describe** | picture a person, place or thing | travel writing, character sketches |

Clues to purpose sit on the surface of the text. Numbered steps and commands such as *first*, *then* and *stir* point to instructions. Dates, figures and a neutral tone point to information. A call to act (*sign the petition*, *buy a bag today*) points to persuasion. Sensory detail with no argument or story points to description.

:::example Finding the purpose of a passage
> First, rinse the rice until the water runs clear. Then add two cups of water for every cup of rice, bring it to the boil and turn the heat down low.

What is the main purpose of this passage?
@step Look at the verbs: *rinse*, *add*, *bring*, *turn*. They are commands to the reader.
@step The words *first* and *then* put the commands in order.
@answer The passage tells the reader how to do something, step by step, so its purpose is **to instruct**.
:::

## One main purpose

Many texts do more than one thing. An advertisement may tell a funny story; a news report may describe a flood vividly. Ask what the text is mainly *for*: what the writer wants to happen when the reader reaches the end. The funny advertisement exists to sell, so its main purpose is to persuade. The news report exists to give readers the facts they need, so its main purpose is to inform.

:::mistake Naming the technique instead of the purpose
“The advertisement tells a story” describes *how* it works, not *why* it was written. A story, a statistic or a striking image is a tool; the purpose is what the tool is used for.
:::

Some texts hide their purpose on purpose. In 1729, the Irish writer Jonathan Swift published an essay that begins in a calm, reasonable voice:

> I shall now therefore humbly propose my own thoughts, which I hope will not be liable to the least objection.
>
> — Jonathan Swift, “A Modest Proposal” (1729)

What he then “proposes” is monstrous: that the poorest families of Ireland sell their babies to the rich as food. Swift does not mean a word of it. “A Modest Proposal” is **satire**, writing that mocks a wrong by pretending to support it. By showing a cruel idea in its plainest form, Swift shocks readers into seeing how cruelly the poor were already being treated. His real purpose is to persuade.

:::definition Satire
**Satire** uses humour, exaggeration or irony to criticize people, ideas or institutions. Its surface purpose often seems to be to entertain or inform, but its real purpose is to persuade.
:::

## Who is it for?

:::definition Audience
A text’s **audience** is the group of readers or listeners it is written for. A writer chooses content, vocabulary, length and tone to suit that audience.
:::

The clues to audience are often plain once you look for them:

- **Who is addressed.** “Attention all residents of Elm Street” names its audience outright; so does “Ages 13 to 17”.
- **What the text assumes the reader knows.** A medical journal uses technical terms without explaining them, because its readers are doctors.
- **What the reader is expected to care about.** A leaflet on feeding and sleep schedules for babies is for new parents.
- **Vocabulary and sentence length.** Short, rhyming lines and a colouring activity are for young children.

## Register: formal and informal

:::definition Register
**Register** is how formal or informal language is. A **formal** register uses complete sentences, precise vocabulary and polite wording. An **informal** register uses everyday words, contractions and sometimes slang.
:::

The right register depends on the audience. A text message to a close friend can be informal: *hey, wanna grab food after school?* A letter to a principal, a city council or an employer needs a formal register, because the writer is asking someone they do not know well to take them seriously.

:::example Choosing the register
Hana is writing to the city council to ask for a pedestrian crossing outside her school. Which opening suits this audience?

1. *You guys seriously need to put a crossing by our school, it’s crazy out there.*
2. *I would like to request a pedestrian crossing outside Riverside School, where many students cross a busy road each morning.*
@step The audience is a city council: officials Hana does not know, who receive many requests.
@step Opening 1 uses slang (*you guys*, *crazy*) and sounds like a demand.
@step Opening 2 is polite, complete and specific: it says what she wants and why.
@answer Opening 2 suits the audience, because a formal, specific request is the one a council is most likely to act on.
:::

:::tip Imagine the reader
Before you write, picture one real person in your audience: their age, what they already know and what they need from you. Then read your draft as that person would.
:::

:::summary
- A text’s purpose is what the writer wants it to do: inform, persuade, entertain, instruct or describe.
- Many texts do several things; the main purpose is what the text is mainly for. Satire such as “A Modest Proposal” hides a persuasive purpose behind a straight face.
- A text’s audience is the readers it is written for. Look at who is addressed, what the reader is assumed to know and care about, and the vocabulary.
- Register should suit the audience: formal for people you need to take you seriously, informal for friends.
:::
