Juliet is alone in her room at night. In her hand is the vial Friar Lawrence has given her, and no one else knows what is in it:

> My dismal scene I needs must act alone.\
> Come, vial.\
> What if this mixture do not work at all?\
> Shall I be married then tomorrow morning?
>
> — Juliet, *Romeo and Juliet*, Act 4, Scene 3

She speaks to no one, yet the audience hears every fear. A play has no narrator to tell us what characters think, so playwrights use **dramatic devices**: ways of showing what is going on inside a character, of hinting at what is to come, and of letting the audience know more than the people on stage. In this lesson you will learn to recognize them and explain their effect.

## Who is listening?

:::definition Kinds of speech in a play
- **Dialogue**: a conversation between two or more characters.
- **Monologue**: a long speech by one character to other characters, who listen.
- **Soliloquy**: a speech in which a character alone on stage speaks their thoughts aloud, so that only the audience hears.
- **Aside**: a short remark to the audience that the other characters on stage do not hear.
:::

To tell them apart, ask who is on stage and who can hear the words. Juliet’s speech above is a **soliloquy**: she is alone. When Mercutio holds forth to his friends about Queen Mab, the fairy who brings dreams, he is giving a **monologue**:

> O, then, I see Queen Mab hath been with you.\
> She is the fairies’ midwife, and she comes\
> In shape no bigger than an agate-stone\
> On the fore-finger of an alderman,
>
> — Mercutio, *Romeo and Juliet*, Act 1, Scene 4

An **aside** is quick and secret. While Paris explains why his wedding to Juliet must be so soon, Friar Lawrence, who has already married her to Romeo, mutters a line Paris cannot hear:

> [*Aside.*] I would I knew not why it should be slow’d.
>
> — Friar Lawrence, *Romeo and Juliet*, Act 4, Scene 1

## Comic relief, puns and stage directions

Even a tragedy needs moments of laughter. **Comic relief** is a funny scene, speech or character that eases the tension. The Nurse provides much of it: when Lady Capulet mentions Juliet’s age, the Nurse rambles through a long story about Juliet as a baby while Lady Capulet tries to talk about marriage.

A **pun** is a joke that plays on two meanings of a word, or on words that sound alike. Mercutio is the play’s great punster, and he keeps joking even when Tybalt has wounded him:

> No, ’tis not so deep as a well, nor so wide as a church door, but ’tis enough, ’twill serve. Ask for me tomorrow, and you shall find me a grave man.
>
> — Mercutio, *Romeo and Juliet*, Act 3, Scene 1

A “grave” man is a serious one, and also a man in his grave. The joke makes his death more shocking, not less.

**Stage directions** are the instructions in square brackets that tell the actors what to do: *[They fight.]*, *[Drinks.]*, *[Aside.]*. They are not spoken, but they matter: sometimes the most important event in a scene is in a stage direction.

## Foreshadowing

**Foreshadowing** is a hint of what will happen later. *Romeo and Juliet* is full of it. On his way to the feast where he will meet Juliet, Romeo feels a strange dread:

> I fear too early: for my mind misgives\
> Some consequence yet hanging in the stars,\
> Shall bitterly begin his fearful date\
> With this night’s revels; and expire the term\
> Of a despised life, clos’d in my breast\
> By some vile forfeit of untimely death.
>
> — Romeo, *Romeo and Juliet*, Act 1, Scene 4

And as Romeo leaves her at dawn, Juliet looks down at him and sees a terrible picture of their next meeting:

> O God! I have an ill-divining soul!\
> Methinks I see thee, now thou art so low,\
> As one dead in the bottom of a tomb.
>
> — Juliet, *Romeo and Juliet*, Act 3, Scene 5

## Dramatic irony

:::definition Dramatic irony
**Dramatic irony** happens when the audience knows something important that a character on stage does not. We watch the character act on a mistake that we can see and they cannot.
:::

The Prologue makes the whole play ironic: we know from the first lines that the “star-cross’d lovers” will die, so every hopeful moment is shadowed. Some scenes sharpen the irony further. On her wedding morning, Juliet’s family find her cold on her bed, and Capulet grieves:

> Death lies on her like an untimely frost\
> Upon the sweetest flower of all the field.
>
> — Capulet, *Romeo and Juliet*, Act 4, Scene 5

We know she is only asleep from the Friar’s potion. Their grief is painful to watch, and we wait anxiously to see whether the Friar’s plan will work.

:::example Explaining dramatic irony
In Act 3, Scene 1, Tybalt calls Romeo a villain and challenges him. Romeo, who has just come from his secret wedding, answers: “Villain am I none; / Therefore farewell; I see thou know’st me not.” Explain the dramatic irony and its effect.
@step Ask what the audience knows: Romeo has just married Juliet, Tybalt’s cousin.
@step Ask what the characters on stage believe: Tybalt and Mercutio know nothing of the marriage, so Romeo’s gentleness looks like cowardice.
@step Ask what the gap does: we understand Romeo’s words, which they cannot, and we dread the fight we can see coming.
@answer The audience knows that Romeo is now Tybalt’s kinsman by marriage; Tybalt and Mercutio do not. The irony creates tension: Romeo’s refusal to fight, which we understand, provokes Mercutio into the duel that kills him.
:::

:::mistake Three kinds of irony
Dramatic irony is about what the **audience** knows that a character does not. **Verbal irony** is saying the opposite of what you mean. **Situational irony** is an outcome that is the opposite of what was expected. When Juliet tells the Nurse, “Well, thou hast comforted me marvellous much”, after the Nurse has advised her to marry Paris, she is using verbal irony.
:::

The tension of dramatic irony is greatest in the tomb. Romeo, about to drink the poison, notices that Juliet does not look dead:

> Beauty’s ensign yet\
> Is crimson in thy lips and in thy cheeks,\
> And death’s pale flag is not advanced there.
>
> — Romeo, *Romeo and Juliet*, Act 5, Scene 3

The audience knows why: she is about to wake. Romeo sees the signs but takes them for death’s mercy, and misses the truth by moments.

:::summary
- A soliloquy is spoken alone; an aside is a quick remark the other characters do not hear; a monologue is a long speech to other characters; dialogue is conversation.
- Comic relief eases the tension; a pun plays on two meanings of a word; stage directions tell the actors what to do.
- Foreshadowing hints at what is to come; the Prologue tells the ending from the start.
- Dramatic irony is a gap between what the audience knows and what a character believes; it creates tension, suspense and pity.
:::
