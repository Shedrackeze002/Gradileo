Before a single character appears, a speaker called the Chorus steps forward and tells the audience the whole story in fourteen lines:

> Two households, both alike in dignity,\
> In fair Verona, where we lay our scene,\
> From ancient grudge break to new mutiny,\
> Where civil blood makes civil hands unclean.
>
> — Chorus, *Romeo and Juliet*, the Prologue

William Shakespeare wrote *Romeo and Juliet* in about 1595, and it was first printed in 1597. Its English is more than four hundred years old, but it is still modern English: most of the words are ones you use every day. In this lesson you will learn the handful of old forms that cause most of the trouble, and a method for turning any line into plain modern English.

:::note Read the play
The whole play is in the public domain, so you can read it free on [Project Gutenberg](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/1513). Every quotation in this unit is taken from that edition, with its act and scene.

## How to read a play

A play is written to be performed, so read it as a script. The speaker’s name comes before each speech. Words in square brackets, such as *[Aside.]* or *[They fight.]*, are **stage directions**: instructions to the actors that are not spoken. The play is divided into five **acts**, and each act into **scenes**; a reference such as “Act 2, Scene 2” tells you where to find a passage.

:::tip Read to the punctuation, not to the end of the line
Shakespeare’s sentences often run over several lines. Read on until you reach a full stop, a question mark or a semicolon, and only then ask what the sentence means. Reading aloud helps: the rhythm carries the sense.
:::

## Thou, thee, thy and thine

Shakespeare’s characters have two words for “you”. **You** is polite or formal; **thou** is used between family, close friends and lovers, or to someone of lower rank. Juliet calls Romeo “thou”, and the change from “you” to “thou” can show a relationship growing closer, or an insult.

| Old form | Modern English | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **thou** | you (as the subject) | *Thou art thyself* (You are yourself) |
| **thee** | you (as the object) | *I take thee at thy word* (I take you at your word) |
| **thy** | your | *Deny thy father* (Reject your father) |
| **thine** | your (before a vowel sound), yours | *thine eye* (your eye) |

:::definition Verbs with thou, he and she
After **thou**, a verb ends in **-est** or **-st**: *thou knowest*, *thou hast*, *thou dost*, *thou wilt*, and “are” becomes **art**. After **he**, **she**, **it** or a singular noun, a verb often ends in **-eth**: *he knoweth*, *she hath*, *it doth*.
:::

> Thou knowest the mask of night is on my face,\
> Else would a maiden blush bepaint my cheek\
> For that which thou hast heard me speak tonight.
>
> — Juliet, *Romeo and Juliet*, Act 2, Scene 2

## Words that have changed

A few common words have dropped out of use or changed their meaning. These are the ones that trip readers up most often in *Romeo and Juliet*.

| Word | Meaning | Word | Meaning |
|---|---|---|---|
| **wherefore** | why | **ere** | before |
| **anon** | right away, “coming!” | **hie** | hurry |
| **alack** | alas (a cry of sorrow) | **fain** | gladly |
| **marry** (as an exclamation) | indeed (from “by the Virgin Mary”) | **soft** (as an exclamation) | wait; hush |
| **hence** | from here, away | **hither** | here, to this place |
| **methinks** | it seems to me | **coz** | cousin |
| **ay** | yes | **nay** | no |
| **’tis**, **ne’er**, **o’er**, **e’en** | it is, never, over, even | **an** or **and** (meaning “if”) | if |

:::mistake Wherefore does not mean where
When Juliet asks “wherefore art thou Romeo?”, she is not wondering where Romeo is. She is asking **why** he has to be Romeo, a Montague, the son of her family’s enemy.
:::

The apostrophe in a word such as *pierc’d*, *banish’d* or *’tis* marks a missing letter. In *pierc’d* and *banish’d* it tells the actor not to sound the -ed as an extra syllable, so that the word fits the rhythm of the line.

## Verse and prose

Most of the play is written in **blank verse**: lines of about ten syllables with a regular da-DUM rhythm (**iambic pentameter**) that do not rhyme. Try it on Romeo’s line in the garden:

> But soft, what light through yonder window breaks?\
> It is the east, and Juliet is the sun!
>
> — Romeo, *Romeo and Juliet*, Act 2, Scene 2

*But SOFT, what LIGHT through YONder WINdow BREAKS?* Ten syllables, five beats. Shakespeare also uses **rhyme** for special moments: the Prologue is a fourteen-line sonnet, and when Romeo and Juliet first speak to each other, their lines together form another sonnet. Servants, jokes and quick, informal talk are usually in **prose**, ordinary sentences with no fixed rhythm, like Mercutio’s last jokes:

> No, ’tis not so deep as a well, nor so wide as a church door, but ’tis enough, ’twill serve. Ask for me tomorrow, and you shall find me a grave man.
>
> — Mercutio, *Romeo and Juliet*, Act 3, Scene 1

## Paraphrasing a line

To **paraphrase** is to put a passage into your own words, keeping all of its meaning. A good paraphrase of Shakespeare is not a word-for-word swap: it follows the sense of the whole sentence.

:::example Paraphrasing Juliet at her window
Paraphrase these lines. Juliet is at her window at night and believes she is alone.

> O Romeo, Romeo, wherefore art thou Romeo?\
> Deny thy father and refuse thy name.\
> Or if thou wilt not, be but sworn my love,\
> And I’ll no longer be a Capulet.
>
> — Juliet, *Romeo and Juliet*, Act 2, Scene 2
@step Replace the old words: *wherefore* means “why”, *art thou* means “are you”, *thy* means “your”, *wilt* means “will”.
@step Find the meaning of each sentence. *Deny* here means “reject” or “disown”; *be but sworn my love* means “just swear that you love me”.
@step Use the context: Juliet has just learned that Romeo is a Montague, the son of her family’s enemy.
@answer “Romeo, why do you have to be Romeo, a Montague? Reject your father and your name; or, if you won’t, just swear you love me, and I’ll stop being a Capulet.”
:::

:::mistake Word order
Shakespeare often puts the verb or the object first, for rhythm or emphasis: *Fain would I dwell on form* means “I would gladly keep to good manners”, and *Villain am I none* means “I am no villain”. When a line seems back to front, find the subject and the verb, then rebuild the sentence in modern order.
:::

:::summary
- *Thou* and *thee* mean “you” (subject and object), *thy* and *thine* mean “your”; after *thou*, verbs end in -est or -st (*knowest*, *hast*), and after *he* or *she* they often end in -eth (*hath*, *doth*).
- Learn the words that have changed: *wherefore* means “why”, *ere* means “before”, *hie* means “hurry”, *anon* means “right away”, and *soft* means “wait”.
- Most of the play is blank verse, unrhymed lines of about ten syllables; prose is used for servants, jokes and informal talk; rhyme marks special moments.
- To paraphrase, read to the punctuation, replace the old words, rebuild the word order, and check the meaning against who is speaking and why.
:::
