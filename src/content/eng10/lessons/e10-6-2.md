An analytical essay is an argument about how a text works. It does not ask *what happens* in a story; your reader already knows that. It asks *how* the writer makes something happen to the reader, and *why* it matters. In this lesson you will write a thesis that makes such an argument, build body paragraphs from a point, evidence and analysis, and work quotations smoothly into your own sentences.

## The thesis

:::definition Thesis
A **thesis** is the one-sentence claim that a whole analytical essay sets out to prove. A strong thesis is **arguable** (a thoughtful reader could disagree), **specific** (it names what in the text you will examine) and **analytical** (it says *how* or *why*, not just *what*).
:::

Weak theses fail one of those tests in predictable ways:

| Weak thesis | What is wrong |
|---|---|
| “The Monkey’s Paw” was published in 1902. | A fact: nobody could argue with it. |
| In this essay, I will discuss the monkey’s paw. | An announcement of a topic, not a claim. |
| Stories teach readers many lessons about life. | Far too broad to prove in one essay. |
| The Whites wish for money and their son dies. | A plot summary. |
| “The Monkey’s Paw” is a scary story. | An opinion with no reason or focus. |

:::example Strengthening a thesis
Improve this thesis: *“The Monkey’s Paw” is a scary story.*
@step Ask what makes it scary. The reader is warned early that the paw brings sorrow, so every wish is filled with dread.
@step Name the specific technique and where it happens: the sergeant-major’s warnings, before any wish is made.
@step Say how it works on the reader, so the claim is analytical and arguable.
@answer *W. W. Jacobs builds suspense in “The Monkey’s Paw” by warning the reader early, through the sergeant-major, so that every wish the Whites make is filled with dread.*
:::

## Building a body paragraph

Each body paragraph proves one part of the thesis. A reliable pattern has three parts:

:::definition Point, evidence, analysis
- **Point**: a topic sentence stating what the paragraph will prove.
- **Evidence**: words or details from the text that support the point, usually a short quotation.
- **Analysis**: your explanation of *how* the evidence proves the point. Look closely at particular words, images or choices, and say what they show.
:::

Here is a model body paragraph on “To Build a Fire”. The point is in sentence 1, the evidence in sentence 2, and the analysis in sentences 3 and 4.

> (1) Jack London presents the man as practical but unable to imagine danger. (2) The narrator says that the cold “impressed him as being cold and uncomfortable, and that was all.” (3) The words “and that was all” show that the man notices the cold as a fact but never thinks about what it could do to him. (4) A reader who has been told that it is fifty degrees below zero feels the danger the man cannot see, and that gap between reader and character is what makes the story so tense.

Analysis is usually longer than the evidence. One quotation, explained well, proves more than three quotations dropped in a row.

:::

:::mistake “This quote shows that…” and then the quotation again
The commonest weak analysis repeats the quotation in other words: *This shows that the cold was only cold and uncomfortable to him.* That adds nothing. Ask instead *which words matter, and why?*, and explain the effect of those words on the reader.
:::

## Working quotations in

A quotation should never be dropped into an essay on its own. Introduce it with a **signal phrase** (*The narrator says that…*, *Della whispers…*) or, better still, **embed** the author’s words in your own sentence so that the whole sentence reads grammatically:

- *Dropped:* Della is unhappy. “She stood by the window and looked out dully at a gray cat walking a gray fence in a gray backyard.”
- *Embedded:* After her cry, Della “looked out dully at a gray cat walking a gray fence in a gray backyard” as if the whole world had lost its colour.

Two marks let you adjust a quotation honestly:

- **Square brackets** [ ] show words you have changed or added: *“[Jim] looked thin and very serious.”* replaces the original *He*. Round brackets would look like the author’s own punctuation.
- An **ellipsis** ( … ) shows words you have left out. What remains must be the author’s exact words, must keep the author’s meaning and must still read as a sentence.

:::example Shortening a quotation
Shorten this sentence from “The Tell-Tale Heart” so that it keeps only the narrator’s claim about his senses: *The disease had sharpened my senses—not destroyed—not dulled them.*
@step Decide what to keep: *The disease had sharpened my senses* and *not dulled them*.
@step Leave out *—not destroyed—* from the middle, and mark the gap.
@step Check that the meaning is unchanged and the words still read as a sentence.
@answer *“The disease had sharpened my senses … not dulled them.”*
:::

:::mistake Cutting that changes the meaning
An ellipsis may never twist what the author said. *“The disease had … destroyed … them”* uses only the narrator’s words, but it says the opposite of what he meant. That is misquoting.
:::

:::note Commas, periods and quotation marks
Style guides disagree about whether a comma or period goes inside or outside a closing quotation mark, and the rule also differs between countries. Follow the style your teacher or school uses, and be consistent.
:::

:::summary
- A thesis is an arguable, specific, analytical claim; a fact, an announcement, a broad statement, a plot summary or a bare opinion is not.
- A body paragraph makes a point, gives evidence from the text, and analyses it: it explains how particular words prove the point.
- Repeating a quotation in other words is not analysis.
- Embed quotations in your own grammatical sentences; use square brackets for changed words and an ellipsis for omitted ones, without changing the author’s meaning.
:::
