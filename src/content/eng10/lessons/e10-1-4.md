“The Tell-Tale Heart” begins with a voice talking straight at you:

> True!—nervous—very, very dreadfully nervous I had been and am; but why will you say that I am mad? The disease had sharpened my senses—not destroyed—not dulled them. Above all was the sense of hearing acute. I heard all things in the heaven and in the earth. I heard many things in hell. How, then, am I mad? Hearken! and observe how healthily—how calmly I can tell you the whole story.
>
> — Edgar Allan Poe, “The Tell-Tale Heart” (1843)

Who is speaking, and can we believe him? Every story is told by a **narrator**, and the narrator’s **point of view** decides what we learn, what stays hidden and whom we trust. In this lesson you will identify the five points of view and work out what each kind of narrator can know.

## First, second and third person

:::definition Narrator and point of view
The **narrator** is the voice that tells the story. The **point of view** is the position the story is told from: who the narrator is, and how much the narrator knows.
:::

- **First person**: the narrator is a character in the story and says “I”. Poe’s narrator in “The Tell-Tale Heart” is a first-person narrator.
- **Second person**: the story is told to “you”, as if the reader were the main character: “You open the door and step into the cold.” It is rare in short stories.
- **Third person**: the narrator stands outside the story and calls the characters by name and “he”, “she” or “they”.

:::mistake “You” and “I” in dialogue do not decide the point of view
Characters say “I” and “you” to each other in every kind of story. Look at the narration outside the quotation marks. A narrator who now and then speaks to the reader as “you” does not make a story second person: O. Henry’s narrator in “The Gift of the Magi” asks “Perhaps you have seen a pier glass”, but the story is told in the third person.
:::

## Three kinds of third person

| Point of view | Whose thoughts the narrator reveals | Like … |
|---|---|---|
| **Third person limited** | one character’s only | riding on one character’s shoulder |
| **Third person omniscient** | any character’s, and things no character knows | looking down from above, seeing everything |
| **Third person objective** | no one’s: only what can be seen and heard | a camera and a microphone |

Kate Chopin stays close to one character, Mrs. Mallard, and shows her thoughts:

> There would be no one to live for her during those coming years; she would live for herself.
>
> — Kate Chopin, “The Story of an Hour” (1894)

Jack London’s narrator knows more than his character does, and tells us so:

> The trouble with him was that he was without imagination. He was quick and alert in the things of life, but only in the things, and not in the significances.
>
> — Jack London, “To Build a Fire” (1908)

Ambrose Bierce opens “An Occurrence at Owl Creek Bridge” like a camera, recording only what could be seen:

> A man stood upon a railroad bridge in northern Alabama, looking down into the swift water twenty feet below. The man’s hands were behind his back, the wrists bound with a cord. A rope closely encircled his neck.
>
> — Ambrose Bierce, “An Occurrence at Owl Creek Bridge” (1890)

:::tip Count the minds
In third-person writing, count the characters whose thoughts or feelings the narrator reveals. One means limited; more than one, or knowledge beyond any character, means omniscient; none means objective.
:::

## What a narrator can know

Each point of view has limits. A first-person narrator knows their own thoughts but can only guess at other people’s. A limited narrator can enter one mind only. An objective narrator cannot enter any.

:::example Which sentence breaks the point of view?
A story is told in third person limited from Amara’s point of view. Which sentence could not appear in it?

1. Amara slid the letter across the kitchen table to Wei.
2. Amara had rehearsed what to say all morning, but now the words would not come.
3. Wei recognized the handwriting at once and felt his stomach drop.
@step A limited narrator can report actions, and the thoughts and feelings of the one viewpoint character, Amara.
@step Sentence 1 is an action anyone could see. Sentence 2 shows Amara’s own thoughts, which this narrator can enter.
@step Sentence 3 shows what Wei recognizes and feels inside, and the narrator cannot enter Wei’s mind.
@answer Sentence 3 could not appear. A story that told us Wei’s inner feelings as well as Amara’s would be omniscient.
:::

Each choice has an effect. First person makes a story intimate but one-sided. Limited third person lets the reader discover things alongside one character, which suits mysteries. Omniscient narration can show the whole picture, including dramatic irony, when the reader knows what a character does not. Objective narration makes the reader do the work of inferring feelings from words and actions.

## Unreliable narrators

:::definition Unreliable narrator
An **unreliable narrator** is one whose account the reader cannot fully trust, because they lie, misunderstand, are biased, or are not in their right mind.
:::

Poe’s narrator insists, again and again, that he is sane, while describing a plan to kill an old man because of his eye:

> Now this is the point. You fancy me mad. Madmen know nothing. But you should have seen me. You should have seen how wisely I proceeded—with what caution—with what foresight—with what dissimulation I went to work!
>
> — Edgar Allan Poe, “The Tell-Tale Heart” (1843)

The more carefully he argues, the less we believe him. Montresor in “The Cask of Amontillado” is unreliable in a quieter way: he never tells us what Fortunato’s “insult” actually was, so we have only his word that his revenge was deserved.

:::mistake An unreliable narrator is not a badly written one
Writers choose unreliable narrators on purpose. The gap between what the narrator says and what the reader can see is part of the story’s meaning, so look for contradictions between a narrator’s claims and their actions.
:::

:::summary
- First-person narrators say “I”; second-person narration speaks to “you”; third-person narrators stand outside the story.
- Third person limited reveals one character’s thoughts, omniscient reveals many and more, and objective reveals none.
- Every point of view decides what the reader can know; a sentence that enters a mind the narrator cannot enter breaks the point of view.
- An unreliable narrator cannot be fully trusted: look for contradictions between what they claim and what they show.
:::
