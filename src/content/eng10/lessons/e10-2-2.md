Read these lines from Edgar Allan Poe’s “The Raven” aloud, and listen:

> Once upon a midnight dreary, while I pondered, weak and weary,\
> Over many a quaint and curious volume of forgotten lore—\
> While I nodded, nearly napping, suddenly there came a tapping,\
> As of some one gently rapping, rapping at my chamber door.
>
> — Edgar Allan Poe, “The Raven” (1845)

Before you have worked out what is happening, the sounds have already set a mood: “dreary” chimes with “weary”, “nodded” with “nearly napping”, and the tapping and rapping come again and again, like the knock itself. Poems are made to be heard. In this lesson you will learn the devices of sound, how to write a **rhyme scheme**, and how to hear the beat of a line, its **metre**.

## Sound devices

:::definition Sound devices
- **Alliteration**: the same consonant sound at the start of nearby words: *silent snow slid slowly*.
- **Assonance**: the same vowel sound in nearby words that do not rhyme: *the low moan of the old boat*.
- **Consonance**: the same consonant sound inside or at the end of nearby words: *the old dog hid under the bed*.
- **Onomatopoeia**: a word that imitates the sound it names: *buzz*, *sizzle*, *creak*.
- **Internal rhyme**: words that rhyme within the same line: *midnight dreary … weak and weary*.
:::

Poe’s “The Bells” is full of onomatopoeia. The small silver bells of a sleigh ride go:

> How they tinkle, tinkle, tinkle,\
> In the icy air of night!
>
> — Edgar Allan Poe, “The Bells” (1849)

:::mistake Sounds, not letters
Alliteration and the other devices are about **sounds**, not spelling. “Knock” and “kneed” both begin with the letter k, but their first sound is “n”. “City” and “sun” begin with different letters but the same “s” sound, so they alliterate.
:::

## Rhyme and rhyme scheme

Words **rhyme** when their last stressed vowel and every sound after it match: *night* and *light*, *table* and *able*. Rhymes at the ends of lines are **end rhymes**, and their pattern is the poem’s **rhyme scheme**.

:::definition Rhyme scheme
A **rhyme scheme** is written with letters. The first line’s end sound is A; every line that rhymes with it is also A. The next new end sound is B, then C, and so on.
:::

:::example Writing a rhyme scheme
Write the rhyme scheme of the first stanza of John McCrae’s “In Flanders Fields”:

> In Flanders fields the poppies grow\
> Between the crosses, row on row\
> That mark our place: and in the sky\
> The larks still bravely singing, fly\
> Scarce heard amid the guns below.
>
> — John McCrae, “In Flanders Fields” (1915)

@step List the last words: grow, row, sky, fly, below.
@step “Grow” is A. “Row” rhymes with it, so it is also A.
@step “Sky” is a new sound: B. “Fly” rhymes with it: B.
@step “Below” rhymes with “grow”: A.
@answer The rhyme scheme is **AABBA**.
:::

:::

:::note Near rhyme and eye rhyme
Some poets use **near rhyme** (also called slant rhyme), where the sounds are close but not exact, as in *soul* and *all*. An **eye rhyme** looks like a rhyme but does not sound like one, such as *love* and *move*. Some eye rhymes were true rhymes when they were written, because pronunciation has changed. In this unit, only full rhymes get the same letter.
:::

## Stress and metre

Every word of more than one syllable has a **stressed** syllable, one that is said more strongly: *gar*-den, be-*lieve*, to-*mor*-row. In many poems, stressed and unstressed syllables fall in a regular pattern, the poem’s **metre**.

:::definition Feet and metre
A **foot** is one unit of the pattern. An **iamb** is unstressed then stressed (da-DUM: *a-WAY*); a **trochee** is stressed then unstressed (DUM-da: *GAR-den*); an **anapest** is da-da-DUM (*un-der-STAND*); a **dactyl** is DUM-da-da (*BEAU-ti-ful*). A line’s **metre** names its foot and its number of feet: trimeter (3), tetrameter (4), pentameter (5).
:::

**Iambic pentameter**, five iambs in a line, is the most common metre in English poetry. It is close to the rhythm of ordinary speech, which is one reason Shakespeare used it so much:

> That time of year thou mayst in me behold
>
> — William Shakespeare, “Sonnet 73” (1609)

Read with the stresses marked, it goes: that TIME of YEAR thou MAYST in ME be-HOLD. That is ten syllables and five stresses: five iambs.

:::tip Tap the beat
To find the stresses, read the line aloud in a natural voice and tap the table on the strong syllables. Count the syllables, then the taps: ten syllables with five taps on every second syllable is iambic pentameter.
:::

Not every line follows the pattern exactly. Poets often flip a foot for emphasis, and **free verse** has no regular metre at all. William Blake’s “Tiger, tiger, burning bright” begins on a stress: its feet are trochees, which give the line a heavy, drum-like beat.

:::summary
- **Alliteration** repeats first consonant sounds; **assonance** repeats vowel sounds; **consonance** repeats consonant sounds inside or at the end of words; **onomatopoeia** imitates a sound; **internal rhyme** rhymes within a line.
- Write a **rhyme scheme** with letters: the same letter for lines that rhyme, a new letter for each new end sound.
- An **iamb** is da-DUM; a **trochee** is DUM-da. **Iambic pentameter** is five iambs: ten syllables, five stresses.
- Sound and rhythm are part of the meaning: ask what each sound or beat adds to the poem.
:::
