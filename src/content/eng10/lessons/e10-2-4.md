In 1818 Percy Bysshe Shelley published a sonnet about a broken statue in the desert. Read it once for the story, then read it again slowly:

> I met a traveller from an antique land\
> Who said: Two vast and trunkless legs of stone\
> Stand in the desert...Near them, on the sand,\
> Half sunk, a shattered visage lies, whose frown,\
> And wrinkled lip, and sneer of cold command,\
> Tell that its sculptor well those passions read\
> Which yet survive, stamped on these lifeless things,\
> The hand that mocked them, and the heart that fed:\
> And on the pedestal these words appear:\
> ‘My name is Ozymandias, king of kings:\
> Look on my works, ye Mighty, and despair!’\
> Nothing beside remains. Round the decay\
> Of that colossal wreck, boundless and bare\
> The lone and level sands stretch far away.
>
> — Percy Bysshe Shelley, “Ozymandias” (1818)

On a first reading, it is a traveller’s tale. On a second, you notice the boast “Look on my works” and the reply that follows: “Nothing beside remains.” The proudest king’s works have vanished into the sand, and the poem quietly mocks his pride. That second reading is **close reading**. In this lesson you will learn to identify a poem’s **speaker** and **tone**, notice where the tone **shifts**, and support an interpretation with the poem’s own words.

## The speaker

:::definition Speaker
The **speaker** is the voice that talks in a poem. The speaker is not always the poet: a poet can speak as a soldier, a child, an animal or even an object. A poem written in the voice of a character is called a **persona** poem.
:::

In “Ozymandias”, there are layers of voices: the speaker tells us what a traveller said, and the traveller quotes the words carved on the statue. In “In Flanders Fields”, John McCrae, a doctor in the First World War, gives the poem to the soldiers buried in Flanders:

> We are the Dead. Short days ago\
> We lived, felt dawn, saw sunset glow,\
> Loved, and were loved, and now we lie\
> In Flanders fields.
>
> — John McCrae, “In Flanders Fields” (1915)

## Tone and shifts

:::definition Tone and shift
**Tone** is the speaker’s attitude toward the subject: tender, bitter, playful, mournful, admiring, sarcastic. A **shift** is a change in tone or direction, often marked by a word such as *but*, *yet* or *then*, a new stanza, or a change in what the poem describes.
:::

To name a tone, look closely at **diction** (word choice). Wilfred Owen describes young soldiers in the First World War as “Bent double, like old beggars under sacks” and “coughing like hags”: those words leave no room for glory, and the tone is grim and bitter.

:::example Finding a shift
Shakespeare’s Sonnet 29 begins in misery: the speaker is “in disgrace with fortune and men’s eyes”, weeps over his “outcast state” and envies everyone else. Then comes this:

> Haply I think on thee, and then my state,\
> Like to the lark at break of day arising\
> From sullen earth, sings hymns at heaven’s gate;
>
> — William Shakespeare, “Sonnet 29” (1609)

Where does the tone shift, and how?
@step Before the shift: words like “disgrace”, “beweep”, “outcast” and “curse my fate” make the tone despairing and envious.
@step The shift comes with “Haply I think on thee” (“haply” means by chance): the speaker remembers the person he loves.
@step After it: the lark rising at dawn and singing “hymns at heaven’s gate” make the tone joyful, even triumphant.
@answer The tone shifts from despair to joy when the speaker thinks of the one he loves.
:::

## Reading a poem closely

Close reading is a habit, not a trick. These steps work for any poem:

1. **Read the whole poem** for its general sense. Who is speaking, to whom, about what?
2. **Paraphrase** difficult lines in your own words.
3. **Notice** the details: images, figures of speech, sounds, form, and any shift.
4. **Ask** why the poet made each choice.
5. **Interpret**, and support every claim by quoting the poem’s own words.

:::mistake Reading in what is not there
An interpretation must rest on the words of the poem. If you claim that Dickinson’s speaker in “I’m nobody! Who are you?” is lonely and sad, look for evidence: she calls being somebody “dreary” and is delighted to find “a pair of us”. The evidence points to contentment, so the claim does not hold.
:::

:::summary
- The **speaker** is the voice in the poem, and is not always the poet.
- **Tone** is the speaker’s attitude; find it in the diction. A **shift** is a change in tone, often at a word such as *but*, *yet* or *then*.
- Close reading: read for sense, paraphrase, notice the details, ask why, and support your interpretation with the poem’s words.
:::
