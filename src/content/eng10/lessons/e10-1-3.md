Before anything at all happens in Edgar Allan Poe’s “The Fall of the House of Usher”, you already know that something terrible will:

> During the whole of a dull, dark, and soundless day in the autumn of the year, when the clouds hung oppressively low in the heavens, I had been passing alone, on horseback, through a singularly dreary tract of country; and at length found myself, as the shades of the evening drew on, within view of the melancholy House of Usher. I know not how it was—but, with the first glimpse of the building, a sense of insufferable gloom pervaded my spirit.
>
> — Edgar Allan Poe, “The Fall of the House of Usher” (1839)

No ghost has appeared and no one has been hurt. The feeling comes entirely from the **setting** and the words chosen to describe it. In this lesson you will learn how writers use setting to create **mood**, which details do the work, and how mood differs from **tone**.

## Setting

:::definition Setting
The **setting** is where and when a story takes place: the place, the time (the hour, the season, the historical period) and the social world the characters live in, with its customs, beliefs and rules.
:::

Setting is more than a backdrop. In Jack London’s “To Build a Fire”, the Yukon cold is the force that the main character struggles against, so the setting acts almost as an antagonist:

> It was a clear day, and yet there seemed an intangible pall over the face of things, a subtle gloom that made the day dark, and that was due to the absence of sun.
>
> — Jack London, “To Build a Fire” (1908)

Setting can also mirror a character’s situation. When Della in “The Gift of the Magi” has cried over her one dollar and eighty-seven cents, she looks out on a scene as dull as her prospects: “a gray cat walking a gray fence in a gray backyard.”

## Mood

:::definition Mood
**Mood** is the feeling a passage creates in the reader: gloom, fear, calm, joy, tension. It is also called the **atmosphere**.
:::

Writers build mood with:

- **diction**: the choice of words, such as “dreary”, “melancholy” and “gloom” in Poe’s opening;
- **sensory details**: what can be seen, heard, smelled, felt and tasted;
- **imagery and setting**: the weather, the light, the time of day;
- **sentence rhythm**: long, slow sentences or short, sharp ones.

Compare Poe’s gloom with the window that Mrs. Mallard looks through in Kate Chopin’s “The Story of an Hour”, an hour after hearing that her husband has died:

> She could see in the open square before her house the tops of trees that were all aquiver with the new spring life. The delicious breath of rain was in the air. In the street below a peddler was crying his wares. The notes of a distant song which some one was singing reached her faintly, and countless sparrows were twittering in the eaves.
>
> — Kate Chopin, “The Story of an Hour” (1894)

Spring life, the “delicious breath of rain”, song and birds create a fresh, hopeful mood, which is surprising in a scene of grief. Chopin is hinting at what her character is beginning to feel.

:::tip Name the mood with a feeling word
Describe mood with a word for a feeling: gloomy, eerie, tense, peaceful, joyful, chaotic, hopeful. “Dark” or “rainy” describe the setting; “gloomy” describes the mood the darkness and rain create.
:::

## The details that create the mood

To explain a mood, point to the exact words that create it. Not every word in a passage carries feeling: some only give facts about things, places or times.

:::example Which word creates the mood?
In “The Gift of the Magi”, Della has only one dollar and eighty-seven cents to buy her husband a Christmas present.

> Della finished her cry and attended to her cheeks with the powder rag. She stood by the window and looked out dully at a gray cat walking a gray fence in a gray backyard.
>
> — O. Henry, “The Gift of the Magi” (1905)

Which word does the most to create the dreary mood: “window”, “cat”, “gray” or “fence”?
@step Mood comes from words that carry a feeling, not from words that only name things.
@step “Window”, “cat” and “fence” are just objects in the scene.
@step “Gray” is repeated three times in one short phrase, draining the view of colour, and it comes after Della looks out “dully”.
@answer “Gray” creates the dreary mood: its repetition makes the whole world outside Della’s window seem dull and lifeless.
:::

## Tone and mood

:::definition Tone
**Tone** is the attitude of the writer or narrator toward the subject: amused, bitter, admiring, mocking, detached. Tone is how the narrator *sounds*; mood is how the reader *feels*.
:::

The two can be very different. Montresor, the narrator of Poe’s “The Cask of Amontillado”, describes his plan for revenge calmly, even with satisfaction:

> It must be understood, that neither by word nor deed had I given Fortunato cause to doubt my good will. I continued, as was my wont, to smile in his face, and he did not perceive that my smile *now* was at the thought of his immolation.
>
> — Edgar Allan Poe, “The Cask of Amontillado” (1846)

(*Immolation* means killing someone as a sacrifice.) The tone is calm and coldly satisfied, but the mood, for the reader, is chilling: the calmer he sounds, the more frightening he becomes.

:::mistake Mixing up tone and mood
If you are describing the reader’s feeling, you are describing mood. If you are describing the narrator’s attitude, you are describing tone. A narrator who sounds amused can still leave the reader horrified.
:::

:::summary
- Setting is the time, place and social world of a story; it can shape the conflict and mirror the characters.
- Mood is the feeling a passage creates in the reader, built from diction, sensory detail, imagery and rhythm. Name it with a feeling word.
- To explain a mood, point to the exact words that carry the feeling.
- Tone is the narrator’s attitude; mood is the reader’s feeling. They can differ.
:::
