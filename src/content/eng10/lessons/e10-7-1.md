Read this text message aloud: “missed the bus walking now be there soon”. You can follow it, but only because you can guess where each thought ends. In an essay, a reader cannot ask you what you meant, so every sentence has to show its own edges. In this lesson you will learn what makes a group of words a complete sentence, find the three most common sentence errors (fragments, run-ons and comma splices), fix them in more than one way, and name the four kinds of sentence.

## Clauses: the building blocks

A **clause** is a group of words with a subject and a verb. The subject is who or what the sentence is about; the verb is what the subject does or is.

:::definition Independent and dependent clauses
An **independent clause** has a subject and a verb and makes sense on its own: *The bus was late.* A **dependent clause** also has a subject and a verb, but it begins with a word such as *because*, *although*, *when*, *while*, *if*, *since*, *after*, *which* or *who*, so it leaves the reader waiting: *because the bus was late …*
:::

A **sentence** needs at least one independent clause. A dependent clause can be part of a sentence, but it cannot be a sentence by itself.

:::tip Test a clause
Read the words alone, out loud, as if someone had just walked into the room. If they make a complete statement (“Amara missed the bus.”), the clause is independent. If the listener would say “…and then what?” (“Because Amara missed the bus.”), it is dependent.
:::

## Fragments

:::definition Fragment
A **fragment** is a group of words punctuated as a sentence that does not contain an independent clause. It may be missing a subject, missing a verb, or be a dependent clause standing alone.
:::

Here are three fragments, each missing something different:

- *Running down the hall to catch the last bus.* There is no subject (who was running?), and *running* is not a complete verb on its own.
- *A tall glass of cold mango juice with ice.* There is no verb at all.
- *Because the power went out during the storm.* It has a subject and a verb, but *because* makes it dependent.

A fragment is not about length. *Stop!* is a complete sentence: it is a command, and its subject, *you*, is understood. *Kenji laughed.* is complete too. But a long description of a house with a broken gate and an overgrown garden is still a fragment if it never has a verb.

## Run-ons and comma splices

When two independent clauses meet, something strong has to stand between them.

:::definition Run-on sentence and comma splice
A **run-on (fused) sentence** joins two independent clauses with nothing between them: *The bus broke down on the bridge Wei was late for the test.* A **comma splice** joins them with only a comma: *The bus broke down on the bridge, Wei was late for the test.*
:::

A comma is too weak to hold two sentences together on its own. Words such as *however*, *therefore*, *then* and *as a result* are not strong enough either, so *The phone was brand new, however, its battery died by lunchtime* is still a comma splice.

:::note Two names for the same problem
Some textbooks use “run-on” for both errors. In this course, a **run-on** has no punctuation between the clauses and a **comma splice** has only a comma. Both are fixed in the same ways.
:::

## Four ways to fix a run-on or comma splice

Take the comma splice *The forecast promised sunshine, it rained all afternoon.* It can be fixed in four ways, and all four are correct:

1. **A period** makes two sentences: *The forecast promised sunshine. It rained all afternoon.*
2. **A semicolon** joins two closely related clauses: *The forecast promised sunshine; it rained all afternoon.*
3. **A comma and a coordinating conjunction** (for, and, nor, but, or, yet, so, which you can remember as FANBOYS): *The forecast promised sunshine, but it rained all afternoon.*
4. **A subordinating conjunction** makes one clause dependent: *Although the forecast promised sunshine, it rained all afternoon.*

:::example Fixing a comma splice
Fix this comma splice: *Nia forgot her umbrella, she got soaked on the walk home.*
@step Find the two independent clauses: *Nia forgot her umbrella* and *she got soaked on the walk home*. Each could stand alone.
@step Decide how the ideas are related. Forgetting the umbrella caused the soaking, so *so* or *because* fits.
@step Join them with a comma and *so*: *Nia forgot her umbrella, so she got soaked on the walk home.*
@answer *Nia forgot her umbrella, so she got soaked on the walk home.* Equally correct: *Because Nia forgot her umbrella, she got soaked on the walk home.* or two sentences, or a semicolon.
:::

:::mistake Fixes that are not fixes
A comma goes **before** the conjunction, not after it: *Nia forgot her umbrella so, she got soaked* is wrong. A comma on both sides (*, so,*) is wrong too. And splitting off a dependent clause just makes a new fragment: *She got soaked on the walk home. Because Nia forgot her umbrella.*
:::

:::

To fix a fragment, attach it to the sentence it belongs to. *The cake collapsed in the oven. Although the recipe looked simple.* becomes *Although the recipe looked simple, the cake collapsed in the oven.* When the dependent clause comes first, a comma follows it.

## Four kinds of sentence

Once you can see clauses, you can describe any sentence by the clauses it has.

| Kind | Clauses | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Simple | one independent clause | *The museum is free on Sundays.* |
| Compound | two or more independent clauses | *The museum is free on Sundays, but few people know about it.* |
| Complex | one independent clause and at least one dependent clause | *Although the museum is free on Sundays, few people know about it.* |
| Compound-complex | two or more independent clauses and at least one dependent clause | *Although the museum is free on Sundays, few people know about it, so the galleries are often quiet.* |

:::mistake Long does not mean compound
*Esi and her cousins swam in the lake and cooked dinner on the beach* is a **simple** sentence. It has a compound subject (*Esi and her cousins*) and a compound verb (*swam … and cooked*), but only one independent clause. Count clauses, not words.
:::

Mixing the kinds keeps writing lively. A paragraph made only of simple sentences sounds choppy; one made only of compound-complex sentences is hard to follow. Strong writers vary them, and sometimes use a short simple sentence for emphasis after a long one.

:::summary
- A sentence needs at least one independent clause: a subject and a verb that make sense alone.
- A fragment has no independent clause; a run-on joins two independent clauses with nothing; a comma splice joins them with only a comma.
- Fix a run-on or comma splice with a period, a semicolon, a comma and a coordinating conjunction (FANBOYS), or a subordinating conjunction. Fix a fragment by attaching it to a sentence.
- Simple: one independent clause. Compound: two or more. Complex: one independent and a dependent clause. Compound-complex: two or more independent and a dependent clause.
:::
