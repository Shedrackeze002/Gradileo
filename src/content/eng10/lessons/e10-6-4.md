O. Henry could have written *Della was sad.* Instead, after Della has counted her one dollar and eighty-seven cents and cried, he wrote:

> Della finished her cry and attended to her cheeks with the powder rag. She stood by the window and looked out dully at a gray cat walking a gray fence in a gray backyard.
>
> — O. Henry, “The Gift of the Magi” (1905)

We never read the word *sad*, yet we feel it in every gray thing she sees. In this lesson you will use the same tools in your own stories: showing instead of telling, sensory detail, pacing, a strong opening, and dialogue punctuated so that the reader always knows who is speaking.

## Show, don’t tell

:::definition Showing and telling
**Telling** names a feeling or trait outright: *Liam was nervous.* **Showing** lets the reader work it out from actions, body language, speech and details: *Liam wiped his damp palms on his jeans for the third time and read the first line of his speech again.*
:::

Showing involves the reader. Instead of being handed a label, they notice the evidence and draw the conclusion themselves, which is exactly what makes a character feel real.

:::example Turning telling into showing
Rewrite *Amara was exhausted after the long day* so that it shows instead of tells.
@step Ask what exhaustion looks like from the outside: heavy movements, carelessness, falling asleep.
@step Choose one or two specific actions rather than a list of every symptom.
@step Write the sentence without the word *exhausted* or any word that means the same.
@answer *Amara dropped her bag in the hall, kicked off her shoes and fell asleep on the sofa with the lights still on.*
:::

:::tip Telling has its place
Not every sentence should show. Telling is quicker, and it is useful for moments that matter less: *Two weeks passed.* Save showing for the feelings and moments you want the reader to experience.
:::

## Sensory detail and strong openings

A scene comes alive when the reader can see, hear, smell, taste or touch it. *The market was busy* gives the reader nothing to experience; *Traders shouted prices over the hiss of frying plantains, and the air smelled of smoke and ripe mangoes* puts them in the middle of it. Words such as *very* and *really* add emphasis but no detail.

Your first sentence decides whether the reader keeps going. Ambrose Bierce drops his reader straight into danger:

> A man stood upon a railroad bridge in northern Alabama, looking down into the swift water twenty feet below. The man’s hands were behind his back, the wrists bound with a cord. A rope closely encircled his neck.
>
> — Ambrose Bierce, “An Occurrence at Owl Creek Bridge” (1890)

Who is he, and why is he there? We read on to find out. Openings that introduce the writer (*Hi, my name is…*), list a morning routine, or quote a dictionary definition do the opposite.

## Pacing

**Pacing** is the speed at which a story moves. Writers hurry through moments that matter less and slow down the moments that matter most, stretching them into small actions and sensory details. In “The Tell-Tale Heart”, Poe stretches the narrator’s terror at the heartbeat:

> Meantime the hellish tattoo of the heart increased. It grew quicker and quicker, and louder and louder every instant.
>
> — Edgar Allan Poe, “The Tell-Tale Heart” (1843)

:::mistake Slowing down the wrong moment
Twenty minutes of a character choosing socks, followed by one quick sentence for the moment they open their exam results, puts the reader’s attention in the wrong place. Find your story’s key moment, and spend your words there.
:::

## Writing dialogue

Dialogue shows character and moves the story along, but only if the reader can follow it. Most of the rules are about where the commas and capital letters go.

:::definition Punctuating dialogue
- Put the spoken words inside quotation marks: *“The bus leaves in five minutes,” Ravi said.*
- When a tag such as *she said* follows the words, end the spoken sentence with a comma inside the closing quotation mark, and start the tag with a small letter.
- When the tag comes first, put a comma after it and start the speech with a capital letter: *Ravi said, “The bus leaves in five minutes.”*
- A question mark or exclamation mark replaces the comma: *“Watch out!” Mei shouted.*
- Start a **new paragraph** each time the speaker changes.
:::

:::example A tag in the middle
Punctuate: *if we leave now Wei said we can still catch the bus*
@step The spoken words make one sentence, *If we leave now we can still catch the bus*, interrupted by the tag *Wei said*.
@step Put a comma at the end of the first part, inside the quotation marks, and another comma after the tag.
@step Because it is still the same sentence, the second part begins with a small letter.
@answer *“If we leave now,” Wei said, “we can still catch the bus.”*
:::

If the speaker says two separate sentences, a period ends the tag and the second sentence starts with a capital letter: *“I’m not going,” Tomás said. “It’s far too cold.”*

:::note Single or double quotation marks
This course uses double quotation marks for speech. Many British and some other publishers use single ones (‘like this’). The rules for commas, capital letters and new paragraphs are the same either way; choose one style and keep it.
:::

:::summary
- Show feelings and traits through actions, body language and details instead of naming them; tell only what matters less.
- Use sensory details, not *very* and *really*, and open with something urgent, strange or mysterious.
- Slow down the key moment with small actions and details, and hurry through the rest.
- In dialogue, a comma (or a question or exclamation mark) ends the spoken words before a tag, a split sentence continues with a small letter, and each new speaker starts a new paragraph.
:::
