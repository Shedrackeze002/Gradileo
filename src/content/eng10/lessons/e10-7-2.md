Compare *Let’s eat, Grandma!* with *Let’s eat Grandma!* One comma is all that stands between a family dinner and a horror story. Punctuation marks are signals that tell a reader where to pause, what belongs together and what is extra. In this lesson you will use commas, apostrophes, semicolons and colons the way careful writers everywhere do.

## Commas

Most comma rules come down to five uses.

:::definition Five comma rules
1. **After an introduction.** A comma follows an introductory word, phrase or clause: *When the bell rang, the students hurried out.*
2. **Before a conjunction joining two independent clauses.** *The road was icy, so the buses stayed in the depot.*
3. **Around non-essential information.** *Lagos, the largest city in Nigeria, is home to millions of people.*
4. **Around direct address**, the name of the person or people being spoken to: *Thank you for your help, Omar.*
5. **Never between a subject and its verb**, however long the subject is: *The tall boy in the red jacket plays the trumpet.*
:::

The introductory comma prevents misreading. Without it, *While Mei was cooking the cat jumped onto the counter* seems, for a moment, to have Mei cooking the cat.

### Essential or non-essential?

Some information tells you **which** person or thing is meant. That is **essential** information, and it takes no commas: *Students who finish the test early may leave the room* (only those students). Other information just adds a detail about someone already identified. That is **non-essential**, and it is set off with a pair of commas: *Mount Kilimanjaro, which is the highest mountain in Africa, attracts climbers from around the world.* There is only one Mount Kilimanjaro, so the clause could be removed without changing who or what is meant.

:::example Essential or non-essential
Punctuate: *My oldest sister who is a nurse works night shifts.*
@step Ask whether the clause *who is a nurse* is needed to tell which sister is meant.
@step A person has only one oldest sister, so *my oldest sister* already identifies her. The clause just adds a detail: it is non-essential.
@step Put a comma before the clause and another after it.
@answer *My oldest sister, who is a nurse, works night shifts.*
:::

:::mistake Only one comma
Non-essential information needs a comma at **both** ends. *My oldest sister, who is a nurse works night shifts* leaves the second comma out, and the sentence now seems to say that “a nurse works night shifts”. A single comma between a subject (*sister*) and its verb (*works*) is always wrong.
:::

:::check e10-commas 2
:::

:::note A matter of style
The comma before *and* in a list (*red, white, and blue*) is called the serial comma. Some publishers always use it and some never do; both are correct. Choose one style and keep it throughout a piece of writing. This course leaves it out.
:::

## Apostrophes

An apostrophe does one of two jobs: it shows **possession**, or it marks **missing letters** in a contraction.

:::definition Possessive apostrophes
- For a singular noun, add **’s**: *the teacher’s desk*, *Amara’s bicycle*.
- For a plural noun that ends in s, add only **’**: *the teachers’ meeting*, *the babies’ blankets*.
- For a plural noun that does not end in s, add **’s**: *the children’s playground*, *the women’s team*.
:::

The trick is to write the owner first, then decide. For *the toys of several dogs*, the owner is *dogs*, a plural ending in s, so you add only the apostrophe: *the dogs’ toys*.

:::example Writing a possessive
Write *the playground used by the children* as a possessive phrase.
@step Find the owner: *the children*.
@step *Children* is plural, but it does not end in s.
@step So add an apostrophe and s, as for a singular noun.
@answer *the children’s playground*
:::

:::check e10-apostrophes 1
:::

In a contraction, the apostrophe stands where letters were removed: *do not* becomes *don’t*, *they are* becomes *they’re*, *it is* becomes *it’s*.

:::mistake Apostrophes that do not belong
- **Plain plurals** never take an apostrophe: *Fresh mangoes are on sale*, not *mango’s*.
- **Possessive pronouns** never take an apostrophe: *its*, *their*, *your*, *whose*, *hers*, *ours*. *The dog wagged its tail.* (*It’s* always means *it is* or *it has*.)
:::

:::tip Expand the contraction
If you are unsure between *it’s* and *its*, or *who’s* and *whose*, say the long form: *it is*, *who is*. If the long form makes sense, use the apostrophe; if not, leave it out.
:::

## Semicolons and colons

These two marks look alike but do different jobs.

:::definition Semicolon and colon
A **semicolon** ( ; ) joins two closely related independent clauses: *The first half was dull; the second half was thrilling.* It also goes before words such as *however*, *therefore* and *as a result* when they join two independent clauses, with a comma after them: *Ravi missed the bus; however, he still arrived on time.*

A **colon** ( : ) comes after a complete clause and introduces a list or an explanation: *Hana packed three things for the hike: a map, a compass and a water bottle.*
:::

:::example Choosing the mark
Which mark belongs in the gap? *Please bring the following items \_\_\_\_\_ a pen, a ruler and a calculator.*
@step Before the gap is a complete clause: *Please bring the following items*.
@step After the gap is a list.
@answer A colon: *Please bring the following items: a pen, a ruler and a calculator.*
:::

:::mistake Colons after verbs and prepositions
A colon must follow a **complete** clause. *The recipe needs: flour, eggs and milk* is wrong, because *the recipe needs* is not complete without its object. Leave the colon out (*The recipe needs flour, eggs and milk*) or complete the clause (*The recipe needs only a few ingredients: flour, eggs and milk*). The same goes for prepositions: not *We performed in: Nairobi, Accra and Cairo*.
:::

A semicolon never introduces a list, and it never joins a dependent clause to a sentence: *Zainab stayed indoors; because it was raining* is wrong. Write *Zainab stayed indoors because it was raining.*

:::summary
- Commas go after an introduction, before a conjunction joining two independent clauses, around non-essential information and around direct address, but never between a subject and its verb.
- Essential information (which tells you which one) takes no commas; non-essential information takes a comma at each end.
- Singular owners and plurals without s take ’s; plurals ending in s take ’ alone. Plain plurals and possessive pronouns (its, their, whose) take no apostrophe.
- A semicolon joins two related independent clauses; a colon follows a complete clause to introduce a list or explanation.
:::
