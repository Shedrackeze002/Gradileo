Tossing a coin tells you nothing about the roll of a die. But if you draw a marble from a bag and keep it, the next draw is from a different bag. Deciding whether one event affects another is the key to finding the probability that both happen.

## Independent events

:::definition Independent events
Two events are **independent** if the outcome of one does not change the probability of the other. For independent events,
$$P(A \text{ and } B) = P(A) \times P(B)$$
:::

:::example A coin and a die
A coin is tossed and a die is rolled. What is the probability of heads and a 5?
@step The coin does not affect the die, so the events are independent.
@step $P(\text{heads}) = \frac{1}{2}$ and $P(5) = \frac{1}{6}$.
@answer $P(\text{heads and } 5) = \frac{1}{2} \times \frac{1}{6} = \frac{1}{12}$
:::

Drawing **with replacement** (putting the item back before the next draw) also gives independent events, because each draw starts with the same items.

## Repeated trials and "at least one"

:::example Free throws
Kiona makes 80% of her free throws, and each shot is independent. She takes 3 shots. Find the probability that she makes all three, and the probability that she makes at least one.
@step All three: $0.8 \times 0.8 \times 0.8 = 0.8^3 = 0.512$
@step She misses a shot with probability $1 - 0.8 = 0.2$, so she misses all three with probability $0.2^3 = 0.008$.
@answer $P(\text{all three}) = 0.512$ and $P(\text{at least one}) = 1 - 0.008 = 0.992$
:::

:::check m302-independent 2
:::

## Dependent events

:::definition Dependent events and conditional probability
Two events are **dependent** if one happening changes the probability of the other. The **conditional probability** $P(B \mid A)$ is the probability of $B$ given that $A$ has happened. For any two events,
$$P(A \text{ and } B) = P(A) \times P(B \mid A)$$
:::

:::example Without replacement
A bag holds 5 red and 3 blue marbles. Two marbles are drawn one after the other without replacement. What is the probability that both are red?
@step $P(\text{first red}) = \frac{5}{8}$
@step After a red marble is removed, 4 of the 7 marbles left are red: $P(\text{second red} \mid \text{first red}) = \frac{4}{7}$.
@answer $P(\text{both red}) = \frac{5}{8} \times \frac{4}{7} = \frac{20}{56} = \frac{5}{14}$
:::

## Two-way tables and conditional probability

Rearranging the rule gives $P(B \mid A) = \dfrac{P(A \text{ and } B)}{P(A)}$. In a two-way table, "given that" means you use only one row or one column.

:::figure

:::

:::example Part-time jobs
The table shows whether 120 students at a high school in Ashford have a part-time job. Given that a student is in Grade 12, what is the probability that the student has a job? Are "in Grade 12" and "has a job" independent?
@step Use only the Grade 12 row: 36 of the 60 have a job, so $P(\text{job} \mid \text{Grade 12}) = \frac{36}{60} = 0.6$.
@step Among all students: $P(\text{job}) = \frac{54}{120} = 0.45$.
@answer $P(\text{job} \mid \text{Grade 12}) = 0.6$. This is not equal to $P(\text{job}) = 0.45$, so the events are dependent.
:::

:::tip Testing for independence
Events are independent when $P(B \mid A) = P(B)$, which is the same as $P(A \text{ and } B) = P(A) \times P(B)$.
:::

:::summary
- Independent: $P(A \text{ and } B) = P(A) \times P(B)$. Drawing with replacement gives independent events.
- Dependent: $P(A \text{ and } B) = P(A) \times P(B \mid A)$. Drawing without replacement gives dependent events.
- $P(\text{at least one}) = 1 - P(\text{none})$.
- $P(B \mid A) = \dfrac{P(A \text{ and } B)}{P(A)}$: in a table, use only the given row or column.
- Events are independent when $P(B \mid A) = P(B)$.
:::
