"If you live in Tokyo, then you live in Japan." Statements like this one appear in rules, contracts, computer code and mathematics. Knowing when such a statement is true, and what can and cannot be concluded from it, is the heart of logical reasoning.

## If–then statements

:::definition Conditional statement
A **conditional statement** has the form "If $p$, then $q$." The part after "if" ($p$) is the **hypothesis**, and the part after "then" ($q$) is the **conclusion**.
:::

A conditional statement is false only when the hypothesis is true and the conclusion is false. An example of that case is a **counterexample**, and one counterexample is enough to show that a statement is false.

:::example Is it true?
"If a number is prime, then it is odd." Identify the hypothesis and the conclusion, and decide whether the statement is true.
@step Hypothesis $p$: a number is prime. Conclusion $q$: it is odd.
@step Look for a prime number that is not odd: 2 is prime, and 2 is even.
@answer The statement is false, and 2 is a counterexample.
:::

## The converse

:::definition Converse
The **converse** of "If $p$, then $q$" is "If $q$, then $p$." The hypothesis and the conclusion are switched.
:::

:::example Tokyo and Japan
The statement "If a person lives in Tokyo, then that person lives in Japan" is true. Write its converse and decide whether it is true.
@step Switch the parts: "If a person lives in Japan, then that person lives in Tokyo."
@step Look for a counterexample: a person who lives in Osaka lives in Japan but not in Tokyo.
@answer The converse is false.
:::

In the Venn diagram, the Tokyo circle sits inside the Japan circle, but not the other way around. That is why the statement is true and its converse is false.

:::mistake A true statement can have a false converse
Never assume a converse is true just because the original statement is. Test it separately by looking for a counterexample.
:::

## The inverse and the contrapositive

:::definition Inverse and contrapositive
- The **inverse** of "If $p$, then $q$" is "If not $p$, then not $q$." Both parts are negated.
- The **contrapositive** is "If not $q$, then not $p$." The parts are switched **and** negated.
:::

| Name | Form | Example | True? |
|---|---|---|---|
| Statement | If $p$, then $q$ | If a number is a multiple of 10, then it is a multiple of 5. | yes |
| Converse | If $q$, then $p$ | If a number is a multiple of 5, then it is a multiple of 10. | no: 15 |
| Inverse | If not $p$, then not $q$ | If a number is not a multiple of 10, then it is not a multiple of 5. | no: 15 |
| Contrapositive | If not $q$, then not $p$ | If a number is not a multiple of 5, then it is not a multiple of 10. | yes |

:::tip Pairs that always agree
A statement and its contrapositive are always both true or both false. The converse and the inverse also always agree with each other.
:::

:::example Writing the contrapositive
Write the contrapositive of "If a quadrilateral is a square, then it has four right angles." Is it true?
@step $p$: a quadrilateral is a square. $q$: it has four right angles.
@step Switch and negate: "If a quadrilateral does not have four right angles, then it is not a square."
@answer The contrapositive is true, because the original statement is true.
:::

:::mistake Inverse or contrapositive?
Negating both parts without switching them gives the **inverse**, which can be false when the statement is true. The contrapositive needs both steps: switch **and** negate.
:::

## Biconditional statements

:::definition Biconditional statement
When a conditional statement and its converse are both true, they can be combined into a **biconditional** statement: "$p$ if and only if $q$."
:::

:::example A true biconditional
Can "If a triangle has three equal sides, then it has three equal angles" be written as a true biconditional?
@step The statement is true: an equilateral triangle has three $60°$ angles.
@step The converse, "If a triangle has three equal angles, then it has three equal sides," is also true.
@answer Yes: "A triangle has three equal sides if and only if it has three equal angles."
:::

:::example A false biconditional
Is "A number is even if and only if it is divisible by 4" true?
@step "If a number is divisible by 4, then it is even" is true.
@step "If a number is even, then it is divisible by 4" is false: 6 is even but not divisible by 4.
@answer The biconditional is false, because one direction has a counterexample.
:::

:::check m302-conditional 3
:::

:::summary
- "If $p$, then $q$": $p$ is the hypothesis and $q$ the conclusion. One counterexample makes it false.
- Converse: if $q$, then $p$. Inverse: if not $p$, then not $q$. Contrapositive: if not $q$, then not $p$.
- A statement and its contrapositive have the same truth value; so do the converse and the inverse.
- "$p$ if and only if $q$" is true only when the statement and its converse are both true.
:::
