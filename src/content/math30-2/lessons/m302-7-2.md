A graphing calculator can fit a sinusoidal curve to real data, such as the hours of daylight through a year or the water level at a dock through a day. The result is a regression equation. In this lesson the equation is given: your job is to read it, use it to make predictions, and decide how far to trust them.

## Reading a sinusoidal regression equation

Technology gives sinusoidal regression in the form
$$y = a\sin(bx + c) + d$$
with the angle in **radians**. The features are the ones you already know: the amplitude is $a$, the midline is $y = d$, the maximum and minimum are $d + a$ and $d - a$, and the period is $\dfrac{2\pi}{b}$.

:::example Daylight in Pinewood
A student used sunrise and sunset times to model the hours of daylight in Pinewood, a northern town, on day $n$ of the year ($n = 1$ is January 1):
$$L(n) = 5.07\sin(0.0172n - 1.39) + 12.16$$
Find the longest and shortest amounts of daylight and the period, and interpret them.
@step Longest: $12.16 + 5.07 = 17.23$ hours. Shortest: $12.16 - 5.07 = 7.09$ hours.
@step Period: $\dfrac{2\pi}{0.0172} = 365.30\ldots$, about 365 days.
@answer The model gives about 17.2 h of daylight on the longest day and 7.1 h on the shortest, and the pattern repeats every year (365 days).
:::

:::definition Interpreting the parameters
In context, $a$ is half the difference between the highest and lowest values, $d$ is the average of the highest and lowest values (the midline), and $\dfrac{2\pi}{b}$ is the time for one complete cycle.
:::

## Interpolating and extrapolating

To predict a value, substitute into the equation with your calculator in **radian mode**. A prediction inside the range of the data is **interpolation**; outside the data it is **extrapolation**.

:::example Daylight on April 10
Use the Pinewood model to estimate the daylight on April 10 (day 100).
@step $L(100) = 5.07\sin(0.0172(100) - 1.39) + 12.16$
@step The angle is $1.72 - 1.39 = 0.33$ radians, and $\sin(0.33) = 0.3240\ldots$.
@step $L(100) = 5.07(0.3240\ldots) + 12.16 = 13.80\ldots$
@answer About 13.8 hours of daylight. Day 100 is inside the data, so this is interpolation.
:::

:::example Water level at the dock
Water levels at a dock were recorded every hour for one day ($0 \le t \le 24$, in hours after midnight). Regression gives $h(t) = 2.85\sin(0.506t - 1.21) + 4.62$, in metres. Estimate the water level at 2:30 p.m.
@step 2:30 p.m. is 14.5 hours after midnight, so $t = 14.5$.
@step The angle is $0.506(14.5) - 1.21 = 6.127$, and $\sin(6.127) = -0.1555\ldots$.
@step $h(14.5) = 2.85(-0.1555\ldots) + 4.62 = 4.176\ldots$
@answer About 4.18 m.
:::

Whether an extrapolation makes sense depends on the situation. Daylight follows the same pattern every year, so the model for day 400 (February 4 of the next year) is reasonable: $L(400) \approx 8.5$ hours. But a Ferris wheel model says nothing about a time after the ride has ended.

:::mistake Degree mode
With the calculator in degree mode, $\sin(0.33)$ gives $0.0058$ instead of $0.3240$, and the model gives between 12.0 and 12.6 hours of daylight on every day of the year. Regression equations from technology use radians: always switch to radian mode first.
:::

## When does the maximum happen?

The sine of an angle is greatest, $1$, when the angle is $\dfrac{\pi}{2}$, and least, $-1$, when the angle is $\dfrac{3\pi}{2}$ (plus any multiple of $2\pi$). Set the angle equal to $\dfrac{\pi}{2}$ to find a maximum.

:::example The longest day
On which day of the year does the Pinewood model give the most daylight?
@step Set the angle equal to $\dfrac{\pi}{2}$: $0.0172n - 1.39 = \dfrac{\pi}{2}$.
@step $n = \dfrac{\pi/2 + 1.39}{0.0172} = 172.1\ldots$
@answer Day 172, which is June 21. (The same method with $\dfrac{3\pi}{2}$ gives the shortest day, day 355, December 21.)
:::

:::example The top of the wheel
A rider's height on a Ferris wheel is $h(t) = 12.1\sin(0.1047t - 1.57) + 14.3$, in metres, $t$ seconds after the ride starts. When is the rider first at the top, and how high is that?
@step $0.1047t - 1.57 = \dfrac{\pi}{2}$, so $t = \dfrac{\pi/2 + 1.57}{0.1047} = 29.99\ldots$
@step The height at the top is $14.3 + 12.1 = 26.4$ m.
@answer At about 30.0 s the rider reaches the top, 26.4 m above the ground.
:::

If the time you get is before the data starts (negative, for example), add the period $\dfrac{2\pi}{b}$ until it is inside the data.

:::tip Check with a graph
Graph the regression equation and use the calculator's maximum feature. It should agree with your answer.
:::

:::summary
- Sinusoidal regression gives $y = a\sin(bx + c) + d$ in radians: maximum $d + a$, minimum $d - a$, midline $y = d$, period $\dfrac{2\pi}{b}$.
- Interpret each parameter in context: $a$ is half the difference between the highest and lowest values, $d$ is their average.
- Substitute in radian mode to interpolate (inside the data) or extrapolate (outside the data).
- Extrapolation is reasonable only if the situation keeps repeating the same way.
- A maximum happens when the angle $bx + c$ equals $\dfrac{\pi}{2}$ (plus multiples of $2\pi$); a minimum when it equals $\dfrac{3\pi}{2}$.
:::
