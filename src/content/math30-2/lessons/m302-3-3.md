A card drawn from a deck cannot be both a king and a queen, but it can be both a king and a heart. Whether two events can happen together decides how you find the probability that one **or** the other happens.

## Mutually exclusive events

:::definition Mutually exclusive events
Two events are **mutually exclusive** if they cannot happen at the same time: they have no outcomes in common. In a Venn diagram, their circles do not overlap.
:::

:::example Can they happen together?
A fair six-sided die is rolled. Are "rolling a 2" and "rolling a 5" mutually exclusive? What about "rolling an even number" and "rolling a number greater than 3"?
@step A single roll cannot be both 2 and 5, so the first pair is mutually exclusive.
@step Even numbers: 2, 4, 6. Greater than 3: 4, 5, 6. Both happen with a 4 or a 6.
@answer The first pair is mutually exclusive; the second pair is not.
:::

:::check m302-mutually-exclusive 1
:::

## Adding probabilities

For mutually exclusive events, the favourable outcomes do not overlap, so add the probabilities:
$$P(A \text{ or } B) = P(A) + P(B)$$

:::example A king or a queen
One card is drawn from a standard deck of 52 cards. What is the probability that it is a king or a queen?
@step There are 4 kings and 4 queens, and no card is both.
@step $P(\text{king or queen}) = \frac{4}{52} + \frac{4}{52} = \frac{8}{52}$
@answer $P = \frac{2}{13}$
:::

:::example Two dice
Two dice are rolled. What is the probability that the sum is 7 or that doubles are rolled?
@step Sum of 7: (1, 6), (2, 5), (3, 4), (4, 3), (5, 2), (6, 1), so 6 of the 36 outcomes.
@step Doubles: 6 of the 36 outcomes. Doubles always have an even sum, so no outcome is in both events.
@answer $P = \frac{6}{36} + \frac{6}{36} = \frac{12}{36} = \frac{1}{3}$
:::

## Events that overlap

When events are not mutually exclusive, adding $P(A)$ and $P(B)$ counts the outcomes in both events twice. Subtract them once:
$$P(A \text{ or } B) = P(A) + P(B) - P(A \text{ and } B)$$
This is the same idea as $n(A \cup B) = n(A) + n(B) - n(A \cap B)$ from Lesson 1.2.

## Working backward and "neither"

The formula has four parts. If you know any three, you can find the fourth. "Neither" is the complement of "$A$ or $B$".

:::example Sports and music
At a school in Westbury, the probability that a student plays a sport is 0.45, the probability that a student plays an instrument is 0.30, and the probability that a student does at least one of these is 0.63. Find the probability that a student does both, and the probability that a student does neither.
@step $P(A \text{ and } B) = P(A) + P(B) - P(A \text{ or } B) = 0.45 + 0.30 - 0.63 = 0.12$
@step $P(\text{neither}) = 1 - P(A \text{ or } B) = 1 - 0.63 = 0.37$
@answer Both: $0.12$. Neither: $0.37$.
:::

:::summary
- Mutually exclusive events cannot happen together; they share no outcomes.
- Mutually exclusive: $P(A \text{ or } B) = P(A) + P(B)$.
- In general: $P(A \text{ or } B) = P(A) + P(B) - P(A \text{ and } B)$.
- $P(\text{neither}) = 1 - P(A \text{ or } B)$.
:::
