Real data rarely fall exactly on a line or a curve, but they often follow one closely. Graphing technology can find the polynomial that fits best; your job is to choose the right kind of model, use its equation, and decide when to trust its predictions.

## Choosing a type of model

:::definition Regression
A method that finds the equation of the line or curve of best fit for a set of data. A graphing calculator or spreadsheet gives a **linear**, **quadratic** or **cubic** regression equation. In this course the equation is always given to you.
:::

Look at the overall shape of the scatter plot and count how many times the pattern changes direction.

| Pattern in the scatter plot | Turning points | Best model |
|---|---|---|
| Rises or falls at a steady rate | none | linear |
| Rises then falls, or falls then rises | one | quadratic |
| Changes direction twice (up, down, up or down, up, down) | two | cubic |

:::example Fuel use and speed
Kalinda tested a car on a test track and recorded its fuel consumption at speeds from 40 km/h to 120 km/h. Which type of regression suits the data?
@step The fuel use falls as the speed rises to about 70 km/h, then rises again.
@step The pattern changes direction once, like a parabola that opens up.
@answer Quadratic regression. It gives $y = 0.0015x^2 - 0.21x + 13.4$, the curve on the graph.
:::

:::mistake Chasing single points
A model describes the overall trend. One point slightly above or below its neighbours is scatter, not a turning point. Look at the shape the points make together.

## Interpolating and extrapolating

:::definition Interpolation and extrapolation
**Interpolation** estimates a value inside the range of the data. **Extrapolation** predicts a value outside that range.
:::

:::example Iced drinks
A café in Ashford recorded daily high temperatures, $x$ (°C), from 12°C to 32°C and the number of iced drinks sold, $y$. Linear regression gives $y = 4.6x + 38.2$. Estimate the sales on a 25°C day and predict the sales on a 40°C day.
@step $x = 25$: $y = 4.6(25) + 38.2 = 153.2$, about 153 drinks. $25$ is inside 12 to 32, so this is interpolation.
@step $x = 40$: $y = 4.6(40) + 38.2 = 222.2$, about 222 drinks. $40$ is outside the data, so this is extrapolation.
@answer About 153 drinks (interpolation, reliable) and about 222 drinks (extrapolation, less reliable).
:::

The slope of a linear model has a meaning in context: here, each 1°C rise in temperature goes with about 4.6 more iced drinks sold.

:::tip Keep every digit
Substitute into the equation exactly as given and round only the final answer. Rounding along the way can change the answer.
:::

## Quadratic and cubic models

A quadratic model has a maximum or a minimum at its vertex, on the axis of symmetry $x = -\dfrac{b}{2a}$.

:::example The most efficient speed
For $y = 0.0015x^2 - 0.21x + 13.4$, find the speed with the lowest fuel consumption and the speeds where the model predicts 7 L/100 km.
@step $a > 0$, so the vertex is a minimum: $x = -\dfrac{-0.21}{2(0.0015)} = 70$, and $y = 0.0015(70)^2 - 0.21(70) + 13.4 = 6.05$.
@step For $y = 7$: $0.0015x^2 - 0.21x + 6.4 = 0$. The quadratic formula gives $x = \dfrac{0.21 \pm \sqrt{0.0057}}{0.003}$.
@answer Lowest fuel use: about 6.05 L/100 km at 70 km/h. The model gives 7 L/100 km at about 45 km/h and about 95 km/h.
:::

A cubic model is used the same way: substitute the $x$-value and evaluate.

:::example Active players
A game company in Brookfield models its active players, $y$ (thousands), $x$ months after launch, for the first 18 months with $y = 0.06x^3 - 1.6x^2 + 13x + 3$. Estimate the number of players after 10 months.
@step $y = 0.06(10)^3 - 1.6(10)^2 + 13(10) + 3 = 60 - 160 + 130 + 3$
@answer $y = 33$, about 33 thousand players.
:::

## Is the prediction reasonable?

A regression equation only describes the data it came from. Far outside that range, the curve keeps going but the real situation may not.

:::example Two extrapolations
Use the models to predict (a) the players 24 months after launch with $y = 0.06x^3 - 1.6x^2 + 13x + 3$, and (b) the height of a ball 4 s after it is thrown, using $y = -4.9x^2 + 15x + 1.5$ (data for the first 2.5 s).
@step (a) $y = 0.06(24)^3 - 1.6(24)^2 + 13(24) + 3 \approx 222.8$ thousand players, more than three times the highest value in the data. The cubic's right end shoots up, but a game cannot keep growing like that forever.
@step (b) $y = -4.9(4)^2 + 15(4) + 1.5 = -16.9$ m. A ball cannot be below the ground: it has already landed.
@answer Neither prediction is reasonable. Both use the model far outside the data.
:::

:::mistake Trusting a good fit everywhere
A curve that fits the data closely can still give impossible predictions outside the data, such as negative heights, populations or sales. Check that every prediction makes sense in the situation.
:::

:::

:::summary
- Choose linear, quadratic or cubic regression by counting the turning points in the scatter plot: none, one or two.
- Interpolation (inside the data) is usually reliable; extrapolation (outside the data) is less reliable the further it goes.
- Substitute into the given equation and round only at the end; for a quadratic, the maximum or minimum is at $x = -\dfrac{b}{2a}$.
- Reject predictions that are impossible in context, such as negative heights or populations.
:::
