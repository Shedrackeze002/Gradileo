How many kilometres do students at your school travel each week? Is the price of a used truck related to its age? A mathematics research project lets you choose a question that matters to you, collect real data, and use mathematics to answer it.

## Choosing a research question

:::definition Research question
A **research question** is the specific question your project will answer by collecting and analyzing data. A good research question is:
- **specific**: it names who or what is being studied,
- **measurable**: it can be answered with numerical data, and
- **realistic**: you can get the data in the time you have.
:::

:::example Improving a question
Kwame wants to study screen time. Which question works best: "How does technology affect people?", "Is screen time bad?", or "Is there a relationship between daily screen time and hours of sleep for Grade 12 students at my school?"
@step "How does technology affect people?" is far too broad.
@step "Is screen time bad?" asks for an opinion, not a measurement.
@answer The third question is best: it names a population and two quantities that can be measured and compared.
:::

## Collecting data

**Primary data** are collected by you, through surveys, counts, measurements or experiments. **Secondary data** were collected by someone else, such as tables from a government website or results in a published study. Data can be **quantitative** (numbers you measure or count) or **qualitative** (categories, such as favourite sport).

When you cannot ask everyone in a population, you study a **sample**. In a **random sample**, every member of the population has the same chance of being chosen, which makes the sample more likely to represent the population.

:::definition Bias
**Bias** is a problem in how data are collected that pushes the results in one direction.
- **Sampling bias**: the sample does not represent the population.
- **Non-response bias**: the people who answer differ from those who do not.
- **Response bias**: the wording or setting of a question pushes people toward an answer.
- **Measurement bias**: the tool or method gives values that are consistently off.
:::

:::example Spotting bias
To find out whether residents of Lakeside want a new arena, Sienna surveys fans leaving a hockey game. What kind of bias is this, and how could she fix it?
@step Hockey fans are more likely than other residents to want an arena, so the sample does not represent the town.
@answer This is sampling bias. Choosing residents at random from the whole town would reduce it.
:::

:::mistake More data does not remove bias
Surveying 500 hockey fans instead of 50 gives a bigger sample with the same bias. Fix how the data are collected, not just how much.
:::

## Analyzing and presenting data

Choose the display that fits what you want to show.

| What you want to show | Best display |
|---|---|
| Change over time | Line graph |
| Parts of a whole | Circle graph |
| Comparing categories | Bar graph |
| The relationship between two variables | Scatter plot |
| How one numerical variable is distributed | Histogram |

:::example Mean or median?
The salaries (in thousands of dollars) of seven employees at a small business are 51, 54, 57, 59, 60, 62 and 301. Which value best represents a typical salary?
@step Mean: $\frac{644}{7} = 92$. Six of the seven salaries are below the mean, because the outlier, 301, pulls it up.
@step Median: the middle value of the ordered list is 59.
@answer The median, 59 thousand dollars, is the better measure of a typical salary.
:::

## Evaluating claims

Your presentation should state what the data show and what they do not show. Ask these questions about any claim, including your own:
- **Is the sample large enough, and was it chosen fairly?**
- **Correlation or cause?** Two variables can rise together because a third variable affects both. Towns with more ice rinks have more pharmacies because both depend on population.
- **How precise is it?** A poll result of 54% with a margin of error of 4 percentage points, 19 times out of 20, means the population percent is likely between 50% and 58%.
- **Is the graph misleading?** A vertical axis that does not start at 0 can make a small change look large.

:::example A misleading graph
A bar graph shows sales rising from 92 to 96 thousand units. Its vertical axis starts at 90, so the second bar looks 3 times as tall as the first. By what percent did sales actually rise?
@step Percent increase $= \frac{96 - 92}{92} \times 100 \approx 4.3\%$
@answer Sales rose by only about 4.3%. The bars compare $96 - 90 = 6$ with $92 - 90 = 2$, which exaggerates the change.
:::

:::summary
- A good research question is specific, measurable and realistic.
- Primary data are collected by you; secondary data by someone else. Random samples help avoid sampling bias.
- Watch for sampling, non-response, response and measurement bias. More data collected the same way keeps the same bias.
- Match the graph to the purpose, and use the median when outliers distort the mean.
- Correlation is not causation; report margins of error; check graph axes.
:::
