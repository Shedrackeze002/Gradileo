A smoothie bar, a phone case store and a video game all brag about how many choices they offer. Is "over 1000 combinations!" a fair claim? In this lesson you will learn to check numbers like that by counting the choices in an organized way.

## Tree diagrams and organized lists

A **tree diagram** shows each choice as a branch. Every path from "Start" to the right-hand end is one outcome.

An **organized list** (or a table) shows the same outcomes in rows. The trick is to keep one choice fixed while you run through the others, so nothing is missed or repeated.

| Size | Yogurt base | Juice base |
|---|---|---|
| Small | Small, yogurt | Small, juice |
| Medium | Medium, yogurt | Medium, juice |
| Large | Large, yogurt | Large, juice |

:::example Adding a third choice
The smoothie bar also lets you add one of 3 add-ins (protein, spinach or oats). How many smoothies are possible now?
@step The tree shows $3 \times 2 = 6$ size-and-base outcomes.
@step Each of those 6 branches splits again into 3 add-ins.
@answer $6 \times 3 = 18$ smoothies.
:::

## The fundamental counting principle

Trees get large quickly. You do not need to draw one to count its branches.

:::definition Fundamental counting principle
If one choice can be made in $m$ ways and a second choice in $n$ ways, then the two choices together can be made in $m \times n$ ways. For more choices, keep multiplying.
:::

:::example A video game character
A game lets you choose one of 5 hairstyles, one of 4 outfits and one of 3 pets. How many different characters are possible?
@step Three choices, made together: hairstyle **and** outfit **and** pet.
@answer $5 \times 4 \times 3 = 60$ characters.
:::

:::example Guessing on a quiz
A quiz has 10 true-or-false questions. In how many ways can it be answered?
@step Each question has 2 choices, and there are 10 questions.
@answer $2^{10} = 1024$ ways. Only one of them gets every question right.
:::

## Add or multiply?

Read the problem for the words **and** and **or**.

- Choices made together ("a drink **and** a muffin") are **multiplied**.
- Choices that replace each other ("a hot drink **or** a cold drink") are **added**.

:::example A breakfast deal
A café in Hillcrest sells 3 hot drinks and 4 cold drinks, and 5 kinds of muffins. A deal is one drink and one muffin. How many deals are possible?
@step A drink is hot **or** cold: $3 + 4 = 7$ drinks.
@step A deal is a drink **and** a muffin: $7 \times 5$.
@answer $35$ deals.
:::

:::mistake Multiplying choices that cannot happen together
$3 \times 4 \times 5 = 60$ would count deals with a hot drink **and** a cold drink, which is not allowed. Before you multiply, ask whether each outcome really includes one choice from every group.
:::

:::

## Counting what you do not want

Sometimes it is easier to count the outcomes you do **not** want and subtract them from the total.

:::example At least one digit
A 3-character code uses the letters A to Z and the digits 0 to 9, and characters may repeat. How many codes contain at least one digit?
@step All codes: each character has $26 + 10 = 36$ choices, so $36^3 = 46\,656$.
@step Codes with no digits: $26^3 = 17\,576$.
@answer $46\,656 - 17\,576 = 29\,080$ codes have at least one digit.
:::

:::example Two dice in a board game
A red die and a blue die are rolled. A player moves ahead on a sum of 7 or 11. How many of the 36 outcomes let the player move?
@step Organized list for a sum of 7: (1, 6), (2, 5), (3, 4), (4, 3), (5, 2), (6, 1): 6 outcomes.
@step Sum of 11: (5, 6), (6, 5): 2 outcomes.
@answer The sums cannot both happen, so add: $6 + 2 = 8$ outcomes.
:::

:::tip Order matters with two dice
(2, 5) and (5, 2) are different outcomes, because the red die and the blue die can be told apart. That is why there are $6 \times 6 = 36$ outcomes in all.
:::

:::check m302-counting-principle 3
:::

:::summary
- Tree diagrams and organized lists show every outcome; they are good for small problems and for checking.
- Fundamental counting principle: multiply the number of choices at each stage.
- "And" means multiply; "or" (choices that replace each other) means add.
- For "at least one", count everything and subtract the outcomes with none.
:::
