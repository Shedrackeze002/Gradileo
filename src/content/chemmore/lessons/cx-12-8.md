An MRI scanner in a hospital and an NMR spectrometer in a chemistry lab work on the same idea: hydrogen nuclei in a magnetic field absorb radio waves, and exactly which frequency they absorb depends on their surroundings. That lets chemists read the structure of a molecule.

In this lesson you will interpret ¹³C and ¹H NMR spectra, and use chromatography to separate and identify substances.

## NMR basics

A peak’s position, its **chemical shift** δ in ppm, depends on the atom’s **environment**: what it is bonded to and what is nearby. Atoms in the same environment give one peak.

- **TMS**, tetramethylsilane $\ce{Si(CH3)4}$, is the standard at δ = 0. Its 12 hydrogens are all equivalent, giving one strong peak; it is inert and volatile, so it is easy to remove.
- Samples are dissolved in solvents without ¹H atoms, such as $\ce{CDCl3}$ or $\ce{CCl4}$, so the solvent gives no peaks of its own.

## ¹³C NMR

Count the different **carbon environments**. Ethanol, $\ce{CH3CH2OH}$, has 2. Propan-2-ol, $\ce{CH3CH(OH)CH3}$, also has 2, because its two $\ce{CH3}$ carbons are equivalent.

## ¹H NMR

Three things in a ¹H spectrum help you:

1. The **number of peaks**: the number of hydrogen environments.
2. The **relative area** (integration) under each peak: the relative number of hydrogens in it.
3. The **splitting**: by the **n + 1 rule**, hydrogens next to a carbon with $n$ hydrogens are split into $n + 1$ lines.

| H on the neighbouring carbon(s) | 0 | 1 | 2 | 3 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Splitting | singlet | doublet | triplet | quartet |

:::example The ¹H NMR spectrum of ethyl ethanoate
Predict the spectrum of $\ce{CH3COOCH2CH3}$.
@step Three environments: the $\ce{CH3}$ next to C=O, the $\ce{CH2}$, and the end $\ce{CH3}$.
@step Ratio of areas: 3 : 2 : 3.
@step The $\ce{CH3CO}$ has no H on its neighbour, so it is a singlet. The $\ce{CH2}$ is next to a $\ce{CH3}$, so it is a quartet. The end $\ce{CH3}$ is next to a $\ce{CH2}$, so it is a triplet.
@answer Three peaks: a singlet (3H), a quartet (2H) and a triplet (3H).
:::

## Chromatography

All chromatography uses a **stationary phase** and a **mobile phase**. Components separate because they are adsorbed onto the stationary phase, or dissolve in the mobile phase, to different extents.

**Thin-layer chromatography (TLC)** uses a plate coated with silica or alumina. Each spot is identified by its **Rf value**:

$$R_f = \frac{\text{distance moved by the spot}}{\text{distance moved by the solvent front}}$$

Colourless spots, such as amino acids, can be shown with UV light or by spraying with ninhydrin.

:::example Calculating an Rf value
The solvent front moved 8.0 cm and a spot moved 3.4 cm. Find the Rf value.
@step $R_f = \dfrac{3.4}{8.0}$
@answer $R_f = 0.43$ (to 2 decimal places)
:::

**Gas chromatography (GC)** passes a vaporised sample through a long column in an unreactive carrier gas. Each component has its own **retention time**, and the peak areas show the relative amounts. GC is often joined to a mass spectrometer (GC–MS) to identify each component.

:::mistake Rf has no units and is never above 1
The spot cannot travel further than the solvent, so Rf is always between 0 and 1. If you get more than 1, you have divided the wrong way round.
:::

:::summary
- Each environment gives one NMR peak; TMS is the standard at δ = 0; solvents like $\ce{CDCl3}$ have no ¹H.
- ¹³C NMR: count carbon environments.
- ¹H NMR: number of peaks, area ratio, and the n + 1 splitting rule.
- $R_f = \dfrac{\text{spot distance}}{\text{solvent distance}}$; GC separates by retention time.
:::
