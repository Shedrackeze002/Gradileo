Iron girders hold up bridges. Sodium is kept under oil because it reacts with the moisture in the air. Both are metals, but they belong to very different parts of the periodic table, and their properties show it.

In this lesson you will compare the transition metals with the alkali metals, and recognise the coloured compounds, ions of different charges and catalysts that are typical of the transition metals.

## Where the transition metals are

:::definition Transition metals
The **transition metals** are the large block of metals in the middle of the periodic table, between Group 2 and Group 3. Iron, copper, nickel, chromium, manganese and cobalt are examples.
:::

The **alkali metals** are in Group 1, at the left-hand side: lithium, sodium and potassium are examples.

## Comparing their properties

| Property | Transition metals | Alkali metals |
|---|---|---|
| Melting point | High (iron: about 1500 °C) | Low for a metal (sodium: about 100 °C) |
| Density | High | Low: lithium, sodium and potassium float on water |
| Strength and hardness | Strong and hard | Soft enough to cut with a knife |
| Reaction with cold water | Slow, if at all | Vigorous: they fizz and move about |
| Reaction with oxygen | Slow | Fast: a fresh surface tarnishes at once |

## Compounds of the transition metals

Transition metals share three properties that the alkali metals do not have.

- **They form coloured compounds.** Copper(II) sulfate is blue, iron(II) compounds are pale green and iron(III) compounds are orange-brown. The alkali metals form white compounds that dissolve to give colourless solutions.
- **They form ions with different charges.** Iron forms $\ce{Fe^2+}$ and $\ce{Fe^3+}$, and copper forms $\ce{Cu+}$ and $\ce{Cu^2+}$. Alkali metals only ever form 1+ ions.
- **They and their compounds are useful catalysts.** Iron is the catalyst in the Haber process, and manganese(IV) oxide speeds up the decomposition of hydrogen peroxide.

:::definition Roman numerals in names
The **Roman numeral** in a name such as iron(III) oxide gives the charge on the metal ion: iron(III) contains $\ce{Fe^3+}$.
:::

:::example Writing a formula from a name
Write the formula of copper(II) chloride. A chloride ion is $\ce{Cl-}$.
@step Copper(II) means the ion is $\ce{Cu^2+}$.
@step Two $\ce{Cl-}$ ions (2−) are needed to balance one $\ce{Cu^2+}$ ion (2+).
@answer $\ce{CuCl2}$
:::

:::mistake The numeral is the charge, not the number of atoms
Iron(III) oxide is not $\ce{FeO3}$. The III tells you the iron ion is 3+, and the charges must then balance: two $\ce{Fe^3+}$ (6+) with three $\ce{O^2-}$ (6−) gives $\ce{Fe2O3}$.
:::

:::summary
- The **transition metals** sit in the middle block of the periodic table.
- Compared with the alkali metals, they have higher melting points and densities, are stronger and harder, and react much more slowly with water and oxygen.
- Transition metals form **coloured compounds** and **ions with different charges**, and they are useful **catalysts**.
- A Roman numeral in a name gives the charge on the metal ion; balance the charges to write the formula.
:::
