A chemist has made a colourless liquid and needs to know what it is. Is it an alcohol, an aldehyde or an acid? What is its formula? Test tubes, an infrared spectrometer and a mass spectrometer can answer each question in turn.

In this lesson you will identify functional groups with chemical tests and infrared spectra, and find molecular formulas from precise masses.

## Chemical tests for functional groups

| Functional group | Test | Positive result |
|---|---|---|
| Alkene, C=C | shake with bromine water | orange → colourless |
| Primary or secondary alcohol | warm with acidified potassium dichromate(VI) | orange → green |
| Aldehyde | warm with Tollens’ reagent | a silver mirror |
| Aldehyde | warm with Fehling’s solution | blue → brick-red precipitate |
| Carboxylic acid | add sodium hydrogencarbonate (or carbonate) | fizzing: $\ce{CO2}$ |

Aldehydes and ketones both contain C=O, but only aldehydes are oxidised. So Tollens’ reagent and Fehling’s solution tell them apart.

## Infrared spectroscopy

Bonds absorb infrared radiation at frequencies that make them vibrate more. Each type of bond absorbs in its own range of **wavenumbers**, in cm⁻¹, and the spectrum shows dips where radiation was absorbed.

| Bond | Where | Wavenumber / cm⁻¹ |
|---|---|---|
| O–H | alcohols | 3230–3550, broad |
| O–H | carboxylic acids | 2500–3000, very broad |
| C=O | aldehydes, ketones, acids, esters | 1680–1750, strong and sharp |
| C–H | most organic compounds | 2850–3300 |

The **fingerprint region**, below about 1500 cm⁻¹, has a complicated pattern that is unique to each compound. Matching it against a database of known spectra identifies the compound exactly.

The same absorption explains **global warming**: the C=O bonds in carbon dioxide, and the bonds in methane and water vapour, absorb infrared radiation given off by the Earth.

## Mass spectrometry of organic compounds

In a mass spectrum, the peak with the largest $m/z$ is usually the **molecular ion**, $\ce{M+}$, and its $m/z$ is the relative molecular mass.

Several formulas can share the same whole-number mass: $\ce{CO2}$, $\ce{C3H8}$ and $\ce{C2H4O}$ are all 44. A **high-resolution** mass spectrometer measures $m/z$ to four or more decimal places, and because precise atomic masses are not whole numbers, each formula has a different precise mass.

:::example Which formula?
The molecular ion has $m/z = 44.0261$. Precise masses: H = 1.0078, C = 12.0000, O = 15.9949. Is the compound $\ce{CO2}$, $\ce{C3H8}$ or $\ce{C2H4O}$?
@step $\ce{CO2}$: $12.0000 + 2(15.9949) = 43.9898$
@step $\ce{C3H8}$: $3(12.0000) + 8(1.0078) = 44.0624$
@step $\ce{C2H4O}$: $2(12.0000) + 4(1.0078) + 15.9949 = 44.0261$
@answer $\ce{C2H4O}$
:::

:::mistake Read the data you are given
The wavenumber ranges and precise masses are given in the question or on the data sheet. Use those values, and round only at the end.
:::

:::summary
- Bromine water decolourises for alkenes; acidified dichromate turns green for primary and secondary alcohols and aldehydes.
- Tollens’ (silver mirror) and Fehling’s (brick-red) identify aldehydes; hydrogencarbonate fizzes with carboxylic acids.
- In IR spectra: broad O–H at 3230–3550 (alcohol) or 2500–3000 (acid); sharp C=O at 1680–1750 cm⁻¹.
- The fingerprint region identifies a compound by matching a known spectrum.
- The molecular ion gives $M_r$; high-resolution masses tell apart formulas with the same whole-number mass.
:::
