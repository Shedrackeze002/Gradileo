A bar of soap, a bag of cement, a fertiliser spray and a bottle of baking soda all depend on the compounds of three metals: sodium, calcium and copper. Iron and aluminium, the two most used metals, also have reactions that every chemist should know, from rusting to aluminium's surprising reaction with alkalis.

In this lesson you will describe the properties and uses of sodium and calcium and their compounds, the compounds of copper, and the reactions of iron and aluminium with air, water, acids and alkalis.

## Sodium and its compounds

:::definition Water of crystallisation
**Water of crystallisation** is water that is chemically bound in the crystals of a salt, such as the five water molecules in $\ce{CuSO4.5H2O}$.
:::

Sodium is a soft, very reactive metal stored under paraffin oil. In water it floats, melts into a ball, fizzes and gives an alkaline solution: $\ce{2Na + 2H2O -> 2NaOH + H2}$.

| Compound | Common name | Use |
|---|---|---|
| $\ce{NaOH}$ | caustic soda | making soap and paper |
| $\ce{Na2CO3.10H2O}$ | washing soda | softening water, making glass |
| $\ce{NaHCO3}$ | baking soda | making dough rise; antacids |

Sodium hydroxide is **deliquescent**, absorbing water from the air until it dissolves, while washing soda is **efflorescent**, losing water to the air and crumbling to a powder.

## Calcium and its compounds

Heating limestone gives **quicklime**, $\ce{CaCO3 -> CaO + CO2}$. Adding water to quicklime is very exothermic and gives **slaked lime**, $\ce{CaO + H2O -> Ca(OH)2}$, which is spread on fields to neutralise acid soils. Its solution, limewater, detects carbon dioxide. **Plaster of Paris**, $\ce{CaSO4.1/2H2O}$, sets hard with water and is used for casts and moulds.

## Copper compounds

Blue copper(II) sulfate crystals, $\ce{CuSO4.5H2O}$ (blue vitriol), turn white when heated as the water of crystallisation is driven off; water turns the white powder blue again, which is a test for water. Copper(II) sulfate is used as a fungicide in Bordeaux mixture and in electroplating.

:::example Finding water of crystallisation
1.248 g of hydrated copper(II) sulfate leaves 0.798 g of the anhydrous salt (M = 159.5) on heating. Find $x$ in $\ce{CuSO4} \cdot x\,\ce{H2O}$.
@step Water = 1.248 − 0.798 = 0.450 g = 0.450 ÷ 18 = 0.0250 mol.
@step Salt = 0.798 ÷ 159.5 = 0.00500 mol.
@answer x = 0.0250 ÷ 0.00500 = 5
:::

## Iron and aluminium

Iron rusts when both oxygen and water are present; salt speeds it up. Hot iron with steam gives $\ce{3Fe + 4H2O -> Fe3O4 + 4H2}$, and with dilute hydrochloric acid gives iron(II) chloride and hydrogen.

Aluminium is protected by a thin, tough oxide layer, so it seems less reactive than it is. Like its oxide, it is amphoteric: it reacts with acids and with alkalis.

$$\ce{2Al + 2NaOH + 6H2O -> 2NaAl(OH)4 + 3H2}$$

So aluminium pans should not be used for alkaline solutions.
