An organic chemist who makes a new compound has two questions: is it pure, and what is it made of? The first is answered by purifying it and checking its melting or boiling point. The second starts with tests for the elements it contains, and with burning a weighed sample to find its empirical formula.

In this lesson you will choose a method to purify an organic compound, test its purity, detect nitrogen, sulfur and the halogens by Lassaigne's test, and find an empirical formula from combustion analysis.

## Purifying organic compounds

:::definition Pure substance
A **pure substance** contains only one compound. A pure solid has a sharp melting point and a pure liquid a fixed boiling point; impurities lower the melting point and make it melt over a range.
:::

| Method | Used to |
|---|---|
| Simple distillation | obtain a liquid from a non-volatile impurity |
| Fractional distillation | separate miscible liquids with close boiling points |
| Recrystallisation | purify a solid with a small amount of a more soluble impurity |
| Solvent extraction | move a compound from water into a solvent it dissolves in better |
| Chromatography | separate the parts of a mixture, such as dyes |
| Steam distillation | extract oils from plants below their boiling point |

In **recrystallisation**, the solid is dissolved in the least amount of hot solvent; as the solution cools, the compound crystallises while the impurities stay dissolved. The crystals are filtered, washed with a little cold solvent and dried.

## Detecting the elements

**Carbon and hydrogen** are shown by heating the compound with copper(II) oxide: carbon dioxide turns limewater milky and water turns anhydrous copper(II) sulfate blue.

In **Lassaigne's (sodium fusion) test**, the compound is heated with sodium, which turns nitrogen into sodium cyanide, sulfur into sodium sulfide and halogens into sodium halides. The fused mass is boiled with water and filtered to give the extract.

| Element | Test on the extract | Result |
|---|---|---|
| Nitrogen | boil with iron(II) sulfate, then acidify | Prussian blue |
| Sulfur | add lead(II) ethanoate | black precipitate |
| Halogen | boil with dilute nitric acid, then add silver nitrate | white (Cl), cream (Br) or yellow (I) precipitate |

The nitric acid is boiled with the extract first to remove cyanide and sulfide ions, which would also give precipitates with silver nitrate.

## Combustion analysis

A weighed sample is burned completely, and the carbon dioxide and water formed are weighed.

$$\text{mass of C} = \text{mass of CO}_2 \times \frac{12}{44} \qquad \text{mass of H} = \text{mass of H}_2\text{O} \times \frac{2}{18}$$

Any oxygen is found by difference: mass of sample − mass of C − mass of H.

:::example An empirical formula from combustion
0.600 g of a compound of C, H and O gives 0.880 g of CO₂ and 0.360 g of water. Find its empirical formula.
@step C = 0.880 × 12 ÷ 44 = 0.240 g; H = 0.360 × 2 ÷ 18 = 0.040 g; O = 0.600 − 0.240 − 0.040 = 0.320 g.
@step Moles: C 0.240 ÷ 12 = 0.020; H 0.040 ÷ 1 = 0.040; O 0.320 ÷ 16 = 0.020.
@answer Ratio 1 : 2 : 1, so the empirical formula is CH₂O.
:::

:::summary
- Choose distillation, fractional distillation, recrystallisation, solvent extraction, chromatography or steam distillation by the mixture.
- Pure solids melt sharply; impurities lower and widen the melting range.
- Lassaigne's test: N gives Prussian blue, S gives black PbS, halogens give silver halide precipitates.
- Combustion analysis: C from CO₂ (×12/44), H from H₂O (×2/18), O by difference.
:::
