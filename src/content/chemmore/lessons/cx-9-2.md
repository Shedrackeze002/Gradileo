Halogenoalkanes are useful because they are reactive: the carbon–halogen bond can be swapped for an OH, a CN or an NH₂ group, making them a starting point for many other compounds. But one family of them, the CFCs, was so unreactive at ground level that it drifted up to the ozone layer and began to destroy it.

In this lesson you will predict the reactions of halogenoalkanes, compare how fast they react, and explain how CFCs deplete ozone.

## Classifying halogenoalkanes

:::definition Primary, secondary and tertiary
A halogenoalkane is **primary**, **secondary** or **tertiary** when the carbon carrying the halogen is bonded to one, two or three other carbon atoms.
:::

1-bromopropane, $\ce{CH3CH2CH2Br}$, is primary; 2-bromopropane, $\ce{CH3CHBrCH3}$, is secondary; and 2-chloro-2-methylpropane, $\ce{(CH3)3CCl}$, is tertiary.

## Substitution and elimination

| Reagent and conditions | Role of the reagent | Product | Example from 1-bromopropane |
|---|---|---|---|
| NaOH(aq), warm | nucleophile | alcohol | propan-1-ol |
| KCN in ethanol, warm | nucleophile | nitrile (one carbon longer) | butanenitrile |
| Excess $\ce{NH3}$ in ethanol, heat, pressure | nucleophile | primary amine | propylamine |
| KOH in ethanol, hot | base | alkene | propene |

The same hydroxide ion does two different jobs. In **water** it acts as a nucleophile and substitutes; in **ethanol**, heated, it acts as a base and eliminates.

:::example Lengthening a carbon chain
How could you make butanenitrile, $\ce{CH3CH2CH2CN}$, from 1-bromopropane?
@step The product has four carbons; 1-bromopropane has three. A nitrile group adds one carbon.
@step Warm 1-bromopropane with potassium cyanide in ethanol. The cyanide ion, a nucleophile, replaces the bromine.
@answer $\ce{CH3CH2CH2Br + CN- -> CH3CH2CH2CN + Br-}$
:::

## Which halogenoalkane reacts fastest?

The C–F bond is the most polar, so you might expect fluoroalkanes to be attacked fastest. In fact, the rate depends on the **bond enthalpy**: how hard the bond is to break.

| Bond | C–F | C–Cl | C–Br | C–I |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Bond enthalpy / kJ mol⁻¹ | 467 | 346 | 290 | 228 |

So iodoalkanes react fastest and fluoroalkanes slowest. You can see this by warming them with aqueous silver nitrate in ethanol: the halide ions released form precipitates, and yellow silver iodide appears first.

## CFCs and the ozone layer

**Ozone**, $\ce{O3}$, in the upper atmosphere absorbs much of the Sun’s harmful ultraviolet radiation, which causes skin cancer and cataracts. **CFCs** (chlorofluorocarbons, such as $\ce{CCl3F}$) were once used in fridges and aerosols because they are unreactive and non-toxic.

High in the atmosphere, UV light breaks the weakest bond, C–Cl, making **chlorine radicals**:

$$\ce{CCl3F -> ^.CCl2F + Cl^.}$$

These then destroy ozone in two steps:

$$\ce{Cl^. + O3 -> ClO^. + O2}$$
$$\ce{ClO^. + O3 -> 2O2 + Cl^.}$$

Overall: $\ce{2O3 -> 3O2}$. The chlorine radical is **regenerated**, so it acts as a **catalyst**, and one radical can destroy thousands of ozone molecules.

:::mistake Fluorine is not the culprit
CFCs contain fluorine, but the C–F bond is too strong for UV light to break. It is the **chlorine** radicals that destroy ozone, which is why chlorine-free HFCs replaced them.
:::

Scientists’ evidence of ozone depletion led governments to agree to phase out CFCs, and chemists developed alternatives such as HFCs, which contain no chlorine.

:::summary
- Halogenoalkanes are primary, secondary or tertiary by the number of carbons on the C–X carbon.
- Aqueous $\ce{OH-}$ gives alcohols; $\ce{CN-}$ gives nitriles; excess $\ce{NH3}$ gives amines; hot $\ce{OH-}$ in ethanol gives alkenes.
- The rate depends on C–X bond enthalpy: iodoalkanes react fastest.
- UV light breaks C–Cl in CFCs; $\ce{Cl^.}$ catalyses $\ce{2O3 -> 3O2}$ and is regenerated.
- CFCs were phased out and replaced by chlorine-free HFCs.
:::
