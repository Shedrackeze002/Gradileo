An equation shows what goes in and what comes out of a reaction, but not what happens in between. A **mechanism** tells the story: which bonds break, which form, and where the electrons go, step by step.

In this lesson you will meet the four mechanisms of first-year organic chemistry and draw them with curly arrows.

## The cast: nucleophiles, electrophiles and radicals

:::definition Curly arrow
An arrow showing the movement of a **pair of electrons**. It starts where the pair is (a lone pair or a bond) and points to where it goes (an atom, or the space where a new bond forms).
:::

- A **nucleophile** is an **electron pair donor**, such as $\ce{OH-}$, $\ce{CN-}$ or $\ce{NH3}$. It attacks a δ+ carbon.
- An **electrophile** is an **electron pair acceptor**, such as $\ce{H+}$ or a δ+ bromine atom. It is attacked by the electrons of a C=C double bond.
- A **free radical** is a species with an **unpaired electron**, written with a dot, such as $\ce{Cl^.}$.

## Free-radical substitution

Methane and chlorine react in **ultraviolet light**. The mechanism is a chain reaction in three stages.

| Stage | Example | What happens |
|---|---|---|
| Initiation | $\ce{Cl2 -> 2Cl^.}$ | UV light splits a molecule into two radicals |
| Propagation | $\ce{Cl^. + CH4 -> HCl + ^.CH3}$ and $\ce{^.CH3 + Cl2 -> CH3Cl + Cl^.}$ | a radical reacts and a new radical forms, so the chain continues |
| Termination | $\ce{^.CH3 + Cl^. -> CH3Cl}$ | two radicals join, ending the chain |

Further substitution gives $\ce{CH2Cl2}$, $\ce{CHCl3}$ and $\ce{CCl4}$, so the product is a mixture.

## Nucleophilic substitution and elimination

In a halogenoalkane, the halogen is more electronegative than carbon, so the C–X bond is polar: $\ce{C^{\delta+}-Br^{\delta-}}$.

- **Nucleophilic substitution.** A curly arrow goes from the nucleophile’s lone pair to the δ+ carbon, and a second from the C–Br bond to the bromine, which leaves as $\ce{Br-}$. With warm **aqueous** sodium hydroxide, bromoethane becomes ethanol.
- **Elimination.** With hot potassium hydroxide dissolved in **ethanol**, the hydroxide ion acts as a **base**. It removes an $\ce{H+}$ from the carbon next to the C–Br, the C–H bond pair forms a C=C double bond, and $\ce{Br-}$ leaves. 2-bromopropane becomes propene.

## Electrophilic addition

The C=C double bond is a region of high electron density, so it attracts electrophiles.

:::example Hydrogen bromide and ethene
Describe the mechanism of $\ce{CH2=CH2 + HBr -> CH3CH2Br}$.
@step A curly arrow from the C=C bond to the δ+ hydrogen of HBr; a second from the H–Br bond to the bromine.
@step This leaves a **carbocation**, $\ce{CH3CH2+}$, and a bromide ion.
@step A curly arrow from a lone pair on $\ce{Br-}$ to the positive carbon.
@answer Bromoethane, by electrophilic addition
:::

With an unsymmetrical alkene, such as propene, two carbocations are possible. A carbocation with more alkyl groups on the positive carbon is **more stable**, because alkyl groups release electron density towards it: tertiary > secondary > primary. The more stable carbocation forms more often, so it gives the **major product**. Propene and HBr give mainly 2-bromopropane.

:::mistake Arrows show electron pairs, not atoms
A curly arrow always starts at electrons: a lone pair or a bond. It never starts at a positive charge or an atom with no electrons to give.
:::

:::summary
- Curly arrows show the movement of electron pairs, from a lone pair or bond to an atom or new bond.
- **Free-radical substitution** (alkane + halogen in UV): initiation, propagation, termination.
- **Nucleophilic substitution** (halogenoalkane + aqueous $\ce{OH-}$, $\ce{CN-}$ or $\ce{NH3}$): the nucleophile attacks the δ+ carbon.
- **Elimination** (halogenoalkane + hot $\ce{OH-}$ in ethanol): $\ce{OH-}$ acts as a base, forming an alkene.
- **Electrophilic addition** (alkene + HBr, $\ce{Br2}$ or $\ce{H2SO4}$): via a carbocation; the more stable carbocation gives the major product.
:::
