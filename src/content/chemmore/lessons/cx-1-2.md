Almost every metal object around you began as rock. A few metals, such as gold, lie in the ground as the metal itself, but most are locked in compounds and have to be won back with chemistry. How hard that is depends on one thing: where the metal sits in the reactivity series.

In this lesson you will choose how a metal is extracted from its place in the series, explain the choice, name what is oxidised and reduced, and calculate how much metal an ore can give.

## Ores and the reactivity series

:::definition Ore
An **ore** is a rock that contains enough of a metal, or of a metal compound, to make extracting the metal worthwhile. Most ores contain the metal as an oxide, or as a compound that is easily turned into an oxide.
:::

:::definition Extraction
**Extraction** is the process of getting a metal out of its compound. Because the metal in an oxide must lose its oxygen, extraction is a **reduction**: reduction is the loss of oxygen, and oxidation is the gain of oxygen.
:::

The reactivity series, with carbon placed in it, decides the method:

| Place in the series | Metals | How the metal is obtained |
|---|---|---|
| Above carbon | potassium, sodium, calcium, magnesium, aluminium | Electrolysis of the molten compound |
| Below carbon | zinc, iron, tin, lead, copper | Heating the oxide with carbon |
| At the very bottom | gold, platinum | Found **native**, as the metal itself |

Carbon is more reactive than the metals below it, so it can take their oxygen away. In a furnace, zinc oxide and carbon react: $\ce{2ZnO + C -> 2Zn + CO2}$. Carbon cannot do this to aluminium oxide, because aluminium holds on to oxygen more strongly than carbon does. The only way to pull such a compound apart is with electricity (lesson 1.4).

:::example Choosing a method
How are lead and magnesium extracted from their compounds?
@step Lead is below carbon in the series, so carbon is reactive enough to take the oxygen from lead(II) oxide: $\ce{2PbO + C -> 2Pb + CO2}$.
@step Magnesium is above carbon, so carbon cannot take the oxygen from magnesium oxide. Electricity is needed.
@answer Lead: heating its oxide with carbon. Magnesium: electrolysis of its molten compound.
:::

## Why not use electrolysis for everything?

Electrolysis works for any metal compound, so why is iron not made that way? The answer is cost. The compound must be kept molten, often at many hundreds of degrees, and a very large current must flow all the time. Heating an oxide with carbon needs only a furnace and coke, which is cheap. So electrolysis is kept for the metals that carbon cannot reduce, and that is one reason aluminium costs more than iron even though aluminium ores are plentiful.

In the blast furnace, the carbon first burns to carbon monoxide, and it is mostly the carbon monoxide that takes the oxygen from the iron ore:

$\ce{Fe2O3 + 3CO -> 2Fe + 3CO2}$

The iron(III) oxide loses oxygen, so it is **reduced**. The carbon monoxide gains oxygen, so it is **oxidised**.

:::mistake Reduced means losing oxygen
The word "reduced" is easy to attach to the wrong substance. Follow the oxygen. The substance that gives its oxygen away is reduced, and the substance that takes the oxygen is oxidised.
:::

## Getting copper from poor ores

Rich copper ores are running out. Two newer methods collect copper from ores, and from waste heaps, that contain too little copper to be worth smelting.

- **Phytomining:** plants are grown on the low-grade ore. They take up copper compounds through their roots. The plants are harvested and burned, and the ash is rich in copper compounds.
- **Bioleaching:** bacteria feed on the ore and produce a solution of copper ions, called a leachate.

Either way, the copper is then obtained from its compounds by electrolysis, or by adding scrap iron, which displaces the copper because iron is the more reactive metal. Both methods are slow, but they need far less digging and far less energy.

## How much metal is in an ore?

A metal oxide is only partly metal by mass. The formula and the relative atomic masses tell you how much.

:::example Percentage of iron in iron(III) oxide
What percentage of the mass of $\ce{Fe2O3}$ is iron? (Relative atomic masses: Fe = 56, O = 16.)
@step Relative formula mass of $\ce{Fe2O3}$: $(2 \times 56) + (3 \times 16) = 160$.
@step The iron in one formula unit: $2 \times 56 = 112$.
@step Percentage of iron $= \dfrac{112}{160} \times 100 = 70\%$.
@answer 70.0%
:::

The same fraction gives the greatest mass of metal a given mass of the oxide can yield.

:::example Mass of metal from an ore
An ore sample has a mass of $500\ \text{kg}$. By mass, 40% of it is $\ce{Fe2O3}$ and the rest is rock. What is the greatest mass of iron it can give?
@step Mass of $\ce{Fe2O3}$: 40% of $500 = 200\ \text{kg}$.
@step Fraction of $\ce{Fe2O3}$ that is iron: $\dfrac{112}{160}$.
@step Mass of iron $= 200 \times \dfrac{112}{160} = 140\ \text{kg}$.
@answer $140.0\ \text{kg}$
:::

:::summary
- An **ore** is a rock with enough of a metal compound to be worth processing. Extracting a metal from its oxide is a **reduction**: the oxide loses oxygen.
- Metals **below carbon** (zinc, iron, copper) are extracted by heating their oxides with carbon. Metals **above carbon** (aluminium, magnesium, sodium) need electrolysis. Gold is found **native**.
- Electrolysis is expensive because of the energy needed to melt the compound and supply the current, so it is used only when carbon will not work.
- **Phytomining** (plants) and **bioleaching** (bacteria) obtain copper from low-grade ores.
- Mass of metal = mass of compound × (mass of metal in the formula ÷ relative formula mass).
:::
