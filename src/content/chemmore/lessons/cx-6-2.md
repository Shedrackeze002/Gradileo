The air around you is mostly nitrogen, and every plant needs nitrogen to grow, but plants cannot use the nitrogen gas in the air directly. Turning that gas into ammonia, and the ammonia into fertiliser, is one of the most important reactions in the chemical industry.

In this lesson you will describe how ammonia is made in the Haber process and explain its compromise conditions, and say what NPK fertilisers supply and how their compounds are made.

## The Haber process

:::definition The Haber process
The **Haber process** makes ammonia from nitrogen and hydrogen in a reversible reaction:
$\ce{N2 + 3H2 <=> 2NH3}$
:::

- The **nitrogen** comes from the air, which is about 78% nitrogen.
- The **hydrogen** is usually made from natural gas (methane) reacting with steam.

The gases are purified and passed over an **iron catalyst** at a high temperature (about 450 °C) and a high pressure (about 200 atmospheres). Only some of the gases react on each pass. On cooling, the ammonia liquefies and is removed, and the unreacted nitrogen and hydrogen are **recycled**.

## Why these conditions are a compromise

:::definition Compromise conditions
**Compromise conditions** balance a good yield against a fast rate and a reasonable cost, when no single choice gives all three.
:::

The forward reaction is **exothermic**, and 4 molecules of gas become 2.

| Condition | Effect on yield | Effect on rate and cost | The choice |
|---|---|---|---|
| Temperature | A lower temperature gives a higher yield | A lower temperature makes the reaction too slow | About 450 °C |
| Pressure | A higher pressure gives a higher yield (fewer gas molecules on the right) | Very high pressures need expensive, strong plant and more energy, and are less safe | About 200 atmospheres |
| Catalyst | No effect on yield | Speeds up the reaction | Iron |

:::example Explaining a condition
Why is the Haber process not run at a low temperature, which would give a higher yield?
@step The forward reaction is exothermic, so a low temperature does favour ammonia.
@step But at a low temperature the reaction is far too slow.
@answer A moderate temperature is a compromise: a lower yield, but ammonia is made fast enough to be worthwhile.
:::

## NPK fertilisers

:::definition NPK fertiliser
An **NPK fertiliser** is a formulation that supplies the three elements plants need most: **nitrogen** (N), **phosphorus** (P) and **potassium** (K).
:::

Plants take these elements in as dissolved compounds through their roots, and fertilisers replace what crops remove from the soil.

- **Nitrogen** compounds such as ammonium nitrate and ammonium sulfate are made from ammonia, by neutralising it with nitric acid or sulfuric acid.
- **Potassium chloride** and **potassium sulfate** are mined and used directly.
- **Phosphate rock** is mined too, but it does not dissolve in water, so it is treated with nitric, sulfuric or phosphoric acid to make soluble salts.

## The percentage of nitrogen

:::example Nitrogen in ammonium nitrate
What percentage of the mass of ammonium nitrate, $\ce{NH4NO3}$, is nitrogen? Relative atomic masses: N = 14, H = 1, O = 16.
@step Relative formula mass: $(2 \times 14) + (4 \times 1) + (3 \times 16) = 80$.
@step Nitrogen in one formula unit: $2 \times 14 = 28$.
@step Percentage $= \dfrac{28}{80} \times 100$.
@answer 35%
:::

:::summary
- The **Haber process** makes ammonia: $\ce{N2 + 3H2 <=> 2NH3}$, with nitrogen from the air and hydrogen from natural gas.
- It uses an iron catalyst, about 450 °C and about 200 atmospheres, and recycles the unreacted gases.
- The temperature and pressure are **compromises** between yield, rate and cost.
- **NPK fertilisers** supply nitrogen, phosphorus and potassium. Ammonia is neutralised by acids, potassium salts are mined, and phosphate rock is treated with acid.
:::
