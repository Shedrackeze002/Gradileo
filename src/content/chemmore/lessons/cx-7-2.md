At GCSE, electrons sit in shells: 2, 8, 8. That model explains a lot, but not why iron forms both $\ce{Fe^2+}$ and $\ce{Fe^3+}$, or why sulfur is easier to ionise than phosphorus. For that, each shell has to be split into **sub-shells**.

In this lesson you will write electron configurations in s, p and d sub-shells, and use ionisation energies as evidence for them.

## Shells, sub-shells and orbitals

:::definition Orbital
A region around the nucleus that can hold up to **two electrons**, with opposite spins. Orbitals of the same type and energy make up a **sub-shell**.
:::

| Sub-shell | Orbitals | Electrons it holds |
|---|---|---|
| s | 1 | 2 |
| p | 3 | 6 |
| d | 5 | 10 |

The first shell has only a 1s sub-shell; the second has 2s and 2p; the third has 3s, 3p and 3d. Sub-shells fill in order of energy:

$$\mathrm{1s \to 2s \to 2p \to 3s \to 3p \to 4s \to 3d \to 4p}$$

Notice that **4s fills before 3d**, because it is slightly lower in energy. Within a sub-shell, electrons occupy orbitals singly before they pair up.

:::example The electron configuration of sulfur
Sulfur has 16 electrons. Write its electron configuration.
@step Fill in order: 1s holds 2, 2s 2, 2p 6, 3s 2. That is 12 electrons.
@step The remaining 4 go into 3p.
@answer $\mathrm{1s^2\,2s^2\,2p^6\,3s^2\,3p^4}$
:::

## The d-block, chromium and copper

From scandium onwards, 3d fills after 4s. We usually write the configuration in shell order, with 3d before 4s, and shorten the argon core to [Ar], which stands for $\mathrm{1s^2\,2s^2\,2p^6\,3s^2\,3p^6}$. So iron, with 26 electrons, is $\mathrm{[Ar]\,3d^6\,4s^2}$.

Two elements break the pattern:

- **Chromium** is $\mathrm{[Ar]\,3d^5\,4s^1}$, not $\mathrm{3d^4\,4s^2}$.
- **Copper** is $\mathrm{[Ar]\,3d^{10}\,4s^1}$, not $\mathrm{3d^9\,4s^2}$.

A half-full or full d sub-shell, with one electron in 4s, is the more stable arrangement.

:::

## Ions of d-block elements

:::mistake 4s in first, 4s out first
4s fills before 3d, and it is also **emptied** first. So $\ce{Fe^2+}$ is $\mathrm{[Ar]\,3d^6}$, not $\mathrm{[Ar]\,3d^4\,4s^2}$.
:::

:::example The iron(III) ion
Write the electron configuration of $\ce{Fe^3+}$.
@step Iron is $\mathrm{[Ar]\,3d^6\,4s^2}$.
@step Remove three electrons: both 4s electrons first, then one 3d electron.
@answer $\mathrm{[Ar]\,3d^5}$
:::

:::check cx-electron-config 3
:::

## Ionisation energies

:::definition First ionisation energy
The energy needed to remove one mole of electrons from one mole of **gaseous** atoms, forming one mole of gaseous 1+ ions: $\ce{X(g) -> X+(g) + e-}$.
:::

Three things decide how hard an electron is to remove: the **nuclear charge**, the **distance** of the electron from the nucleus, and the **shielding** by inner electrons.

- **Down a group** the first ionisation energy falls. Each element has an extra shell, so the outer electron is further away and more shielded.
- **Across a period** it generally rises. The nuclear charge increases, while the outer electrons stay in the same shell with the same shielding.

:::check cx-ionisation-energy 1
:::

Across Period 3 there are two small dips, and both are evidence for sub-shells:

- **Aluminium** is lower than magnesium: its outer electron is in 3p, higher in energy than magnesium’s 3s.
- **Sulfur** is lower than phosphorus: its fourth 3p electron shares an orbital, and the repulsion between the pair makes one easier to remove.

:::check cx-ionisation-energy 2
:::

## Successive ionisation energies

Removing electrons one by one, each takes more energy than the last, since it is pulled from an ever more positive ion. A **big jump** comes when the next electron is in a shell closer to the nucleus. The number of electrons removed before the first big jump is the number of outer electrons, which gives the group.

:::example Finding the group
The first five ionisation energies of an element, in kJ mol⁻¹, are 738, 1451, 7733, 10 543 and 13 630. Which group is it in?
@step The big jump is between the 2nd (1451) and the 3rd (7733).
@step So there are 2 outer electrons.
@answer Group 2 (it is magnesium)
:::

:::check cx-ionisation-energy 3
:::

:::summary
- Sub-shells fill in the order 1s, 2s, 2p, 3s, 3p, 4s, 3d, 4p; s holds 2 electrons, p 6 and d 10.
- Chromium is $\mathrm{[Ar]\,3d^5\,4s^1}$ and copper $\mathrm{[Ar]\,3d^{10}\,4s^1}$.
- d-block atoms lose their **4s** electrons first when they form ions.
- First ionisation energy falls down a group and generally rises across a period, with dips at Group 3 and Group 6.
- A big jump in successive ionisation energies shows a new, inner shell, and gives the group.
:::
