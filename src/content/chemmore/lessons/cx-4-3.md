The water in your glass may once have fallen as rain on a hillside, run through a peat bog and sat in a reservoir with ducks on it. It is safe to drink, and yet a chemist would not call it pure. Making water fit to drink is a matter of taking out what harms you, and leaving in the rest.

In this lesson you will tell potable water from pure water, describe how fresh water, sea water and waste water are each made safe to drink, and compare the methods.

## Potable water

:::definition Potable water
**Potable water** is water that is safe to drink. It contains low enough levels of dissolved salts and of microbes.
:::

Potable water is not **pure** in the chemist's sense, because it still contains dissolved substances. Pure water is the compound water and nothing else: it boils at exactly $100^\circ\text{C}$, and it leaves no solid behind when it is evaporated.

## Potable water from fresh water

Where rain is plentiful, water is taken from a river, a lake, a reservoir or the rocks underground. It is already low in dissolved salts, so it needs only two things done to it.

1. **Filtering.** The water passes through filter beds of sand and gravel, which hold back solid particles.
2. **Sterilising.** Harmful microbes are killed with chlorine, ozone or ultraviolet light.

:::definition Sterilising
**Sterilising** water means killing the microbes in it that could cause disease.
:::

## Potable water from salty water

Where fresh water is scarce, potable water is made from sea water.

:::definition Desalination
**Desalination** is the removal of dissolved salts from salty water.
:::

| Method | How it works |
|---|---|
| Distillation | The water is boiled; the steam is cooled and condensed, and the salt is left behind |
| Reverse osmosis | The water is forced at high pressure through a membrane that lets water molecules through but not the dissolved ions |

Both need a great deal of energy, so desalinated water is expensive. That is why desalination is used only where fresh water cannot meet the demand.

:::mistake Filtering does not remove salt
A filter holds back solid particles. Dissolved salt passes straight through with the water, and chlorine does not remove it either. Sea water that has been filtered and sterilised is still sea water.
:::

:::

:::example Testing water for dissolved solids
An evaporating basin has a mass of $50.00\ \text{g}$. $100\ \text{cm}^3$ of water is heated in it until all the water has gone, and the basin then has a mass of $50.04\ \text{g}$. What mass of dissolved solid is in $1000\ \text{cm}^3$ of the water?
@step Mass of solid $= 50.04 - 50.00 = 0.04\ \text{g}$ in $100\ \text{cm}^3$.
@step Scale up: $0.04 \times \dfrac{1000}{100}$.
@answer $0.40\ \text{g}$
:::

## Treating waste water

Sewage and the waste water from farms and factories must be treated before the water goes back into a river. Sewage contains organic matter and harmful microbes; industrial waste water may contain harmful chemicals as well.

1. **Screening** removes large objects, such as rags and grit.
2. **Sedimentation** lets the solids settle. The solid that sinks is the **sludge**, and the liquid above it is the **effluent**.
3. The **effluent** is treated **aerobically**: air is bubbled through it, and bacteria that need oxygen break down the organic matter.
4. The **sludge** is digested **anaerobically**: in closed tanks, bacteria break it down without oxygen. This gives **methane**, which is burned as a fuel, and a solid that can be used as a fertiliser.

## Comparing the three sources

| Source | What must be done | Energy needed |
|---|---|---|
| Fresh water | Filter and sterilise | Least |
| Waste water | Many steps, then filter and sterilise | More |
| Sea water | Desalinate | Most |

Treated waste water can be made as safe as any other potable water, and it needs less energy than desalination. Many people still dislike the idea of drinking it.

:::summary
- **Potable water** is safe to drink. It is not pure: it contains dissolved substances.
- Fresh water is **filtered** to remove solids and **sterilised** (chlorine, ozone or ultraviolet light) to kill microbes.
- Salty water is **desalinated**, by distillation or reverse osmosis. Both need a great deal of energy.
- Sewage is **screened**, then left to settle (**sedimentation**). The effluent is treated **aerobically** and the sludge is digested **anaerobically**, giving methane.
- Fresh water needs the least treatment, waste water more, and sea water the most energy.
:::
