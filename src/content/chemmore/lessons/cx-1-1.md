Drop a piece of potassium into water and it bursts into a lilac flame. Drop in a gold ring and nothing happens, however long you wait. Metals differ enormously in how readily they react, and chemists rank them in a list that predicts which reactions will happen and which will not.

In this lesson you will put metals in order of reactivity from what is seen when they meet water and dilute acids, use the order to predict whether one metal pushes another out of its compound, and describe those reactions as the loss and gain of electrons.

## The reactivity series

:::definition Reactivity series
The **reactivity series** is a list of metals in order of how readily they react, with the most reactive at the top. A metal reacts by losing electrons to form a positive ion, so the higher a metal is in the series, the more easily its atoms form ions.
:::

| Metal | With cold water | With dilute acid |
|---|---|---|
| Potassium | Melts, catches fire, darts about | Too dangerous to try |
| Sodium | Melts into a ball, fizzes quickly | Too dangerous to try |
| Calcium | Fizzes steadily | Fizzes violently |
| Magnesium | A few bubbles over several days | Fizzes quickly |
| Aluminium | No change | Fizzes once its oxide coating is gone |
| *Carbon* | | |
| Zinc | No change | Fizzes steadily |
| Iron | No change | A few bubbles form slowly |
| *Hydrogen* | | |
| Copper | No change | No change |
| Silver | No change | No change |
| Gold | No change | No change |

Carbon and hydrogen are not metals, but they are placed in the series because they compete with metals: carbon for oxygen (next lesson), and hydrogen in acids.

When a metal does react, the products follow two patterns:

- metal + water → metal hydroxide + hydrogen
- metal + acid → salt + hydrogen

Only a metal **above hydrogen** in the series reacts with a dilute acid. That is why copper, silver and gold do not fizz in acid.

:::example Reading a results table
Four metals are placed in dilute hydrochloric acid. Metal A fizzes steadily, metal D does nothing, metal G fizzes violently and metal X gives a few slow bubbles. Put them in order, most reactive first.
@step The more reactive the metal, the more vigorous the reaction.
@step G fizzes violently, A steadily, X slowly, and D not at all.
@answer G, A, X, D
:::

:::tip A fair comparison
To compare metals fairly, change only the metal. Use pieces of the same size, and acid of the same concentration, volume and temperature.
:::

## Displacement reactions

:::definition Displacement reaction
In a **displacement reaction**, a more reactive metal takes the place of a less reactive metal in a compound. The less reactive metal is pushed out as the element.
:::

Put an iron nail into blue copper(II) sulfate solution. A pink-brown coating of copper grows on the nail, and the blue colour fades as iron(II) sulfate forms:

iron + copper(II) sulfate → iron(II) sulfate + copper

Iron is above copper in the series, so it displaces the copper. Put a piece of copper into iron(II) sulfate solution, and nothing happens: a less reactive metal cannot displace a more reactive one.

The same rule works for a metal heated with the oxide of another metal. Aluminium is above iron, so it takes the oxygen from iron(III) oxide: $\ce{2Al + Fe2O3 -> Al2O3 + 2Fe}$. The reaction gives out so much heat that the iron is molten, and it has been used to weld railway lines.

:::example Will it react?
Zinc is placed in magnesium sulfate solution. Does a reaction happen?
@step Find both metals in the series: magnesium is above zinc.
@step Zinc is the less reactive metal, so it cannot displace magnesium.
@answer No reaction happens.
:::

Displacement also lets you work out an order. If metal G displaces both A and T from their solutions, G is the most reactive of the three. If T displaces neither, T is the least reactive.

## Oxidation and reduction as electron transfer

When zinc displaces copper from copper(II) sulfate, the sulfate ions do nothing: they are in the solution before and after. Leaving them out gives the **ionic equation**, which shows only what changes:

$\ce{Zn + Cu^2+ -> Zn^2+ + Cu}$

Each zinc atom loses two electrons and becomes a $\ce{Zn^2+}$ ion. Each $\ce{Cu^2+}$ ion gains two electrons and becomes a copper atom.

:::definition Oxidation and reduction
**Oxidation** is the loss of electrons, and **reduction** is the gain of electrons. A way to remember it: OIL RIG, Oxidation Is Loss, Reduction Is Gain. The two always happen together, because the electrons one particle loses are gained by another.
:::

In this reaction zinc is oxidised and the copper ions are reduced. In every displacement, the more reactive metal is the one that is oxidised.

:::example Balancing the charge
Write the ionic equation for iron displacing silver from silver nitrate solution. Iron forms $\ce{Fe^2+}$ and silver ions are $\ce{Ag+}$.
@step Iron atoms become $\ce{Fe^2+}$, losing 2 electrons each. Each $\ce{Ag+}$ gains only 1 electron.
@step Two silver ions are needed for each iron atom, so that the electrons lost equal the electrons gained.
@answer $\ce{Fe + 2Ag+ -> Fe^2+ + 2Ag}$. The charge is 2+ on each side.
:::

:::mistake The ion is reduced, not the metal that appears
It is tempting to say "copper is reduced" and point at the copper metal that forms. The particle that gains the electrons is the copper **ion**, $\ce{Cu^2+}$. Name what is oxidised and what is reduced from the left-hand side of the equation.
:::

:::summary
- The **reactivity series** lists metals from the most reactive to the least: potassium, sodium, calcium, magnesium, aluminium, (carbon), zinc, iron, (hydrogen), copper, silver, gold.
- The more reactive a metal, the more vigorously it reacts with water and with dilute acids. Only metals above hydrogen react with dilute acids.
- A more reactive metal **displaces** a less reactive metal from its compound. The reverse does not happen.
- An ionic equation leaves out the ions that do not change: $\ce{Zn + Cu^2+ -> Zn^2+ + Cu}$.
- **Oxidation** is the loss of electrons and **reduction** is the gain of electrons. In a displacement, the more reactive metal is oxidised and the ions of the less reactive metal are reduced.
:::
