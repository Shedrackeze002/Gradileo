In a WASSCE practical, you may be handed a solution labelled only with a letter and asked what it contains. Each ion gives a set of results with a few reagents, and putting the results together identifies it. This is qualitative analysis: finding what is present, not how much.

In this lesson you will identify cations with sodium hydroxide and ammonia solutions, identify anions with dilute acid, barium and silver salts, and identify common gases by their tests.

## Cations with sodium hydroxide and ammonia

:::definition Qualitative analysis
**Qualitative analysis** is finding which substances or ions are present in a sample, from the observations they give with test reagents.
:::

Add the reagent a few drops at a time, then in excess.

| Ion | With NaOH(aq) | With NH₃(aq) |
|---|---|---|
| $\ce{NH4+}$ | no precipitate; on warming, a gas that turns damp red litmus blue | no visible change |
| $\ce{Ca^2+}$ | white precipitate, insoluble in excess | no precipitate |
| $\ce{Pb^2+}$ | white precipitate, soluble in excess | white precipitate, insoluble in excess |
| $\ce{Zn^2+}$ | white precipitate, soluble in excess | white precipitate, soluble in excess |
| $\ce{Al^3+}$ | white precipitate, soluble in excess | white precipitate, insoluble in excess |
| $\ce{Cu^2+}$ | blue precipitate, insoluble in excess | blue precipitate, dissolves in excess to a deep blue solution |
| $\ce{Fe^2+}$ | green precipitate, slowly turning brown at the surface | green precipitate, insoluble in excess |
| $\ce{Fe^3+}$ | reddish-brown precipitate, insoluble in excess | reddish-brown precipitate, insoluble in excess |

Lead(II) and aluminium ions give the same results with both reagents; they are told apart by adding potassium iodide, which gives a yellow precipitate of lead(II) iodide, or dilute hydrochloric acid, which gives white lead(II) chloride. Iron(III) is confirmed by potassium thiocyanate, which gives a blood-red colour.

## Anions

| Ion | Test | Result |
|---|---|---|
| $\ce{CO3^2-}$ | add dilute acid | effervescence; gas turns limewater milky |
| $\ce{SO3^2-}$ | add dilute acid and warm | choking gas that turns acidified potassium dichromate(VI) from orange to green |
| $\ce{SO4^2-}$ | add dilute HCl, then barium chloride | white precipitate |
| $\ce{Cl-}$ | add dilute nitric acid, then silver nitrate | white precipitate, soluble in dilute ammonia |
| $\ce{NO3-}$ | add iron(II) sulfate, then carefully pour concentrated sulfuric acid down the side | brown ring where the liquids meet |

The acid is added first in the sulfate and chloride tests to remove carbonate and sulfite ions, which would also give precipitates.

## Gases

| Gas | Test |
|---|---|
| Hydrogen | burns with a squeaky pop |
| Oxygen | relights a glowing splint |
| Carbon dioxide | turns limewater milky |
| Ammonia | turns damp red litmus blue; dense white fumes with HCl |
| Hydrogen chloride | turns damp blue litmus red; dense white fumes with NH₃ |
| Sulfur dioxide | turns orange acidified dichromate green |
| Chlorine | turns damp blue litmus red, then bleaches it white |

:::example Identifying a salt
A white solid gives a white precipitate with NaOH(aq) that dissolves in excess, and a white precipitate with NH₃(aq) that also dissolves in excess. Its solution gives a white precipitate with acidified barium chloride. What is it?
@step Only $\ce{Zn^2+}$ gives a white precipitate that dissolves in excess of both reagents.
@step A white precipitate with acidified barium chloride shows sulfate.
@answer Zinc sulfate
:::

:::summary
- With NaOH: Zn²⁺, Al³⁺ and Pb²⁺ precipitates dissolve in excess; Ca²⁺ does not.
- With NH₃: only Zn²⁺ and Cu²⁺ precipitates dissolve in excess; Cu²⁺ gives a deep blue solution.
- Colours: Cu²⁺ blue, Fe²⁺ green, Fe³⁺ reddish-brown.
- Carbonate and sulfite give gases with acid; sulfate gives white BaSO₄; chloride gives white AgCl; nitrate gives a brown ring.
:::
