Chemistry is built on measurement. A chemist who says a solution is "about a quarter of a mole per litre" has said very little, while one who writes 0.248 mol dm⁻³ has told you both the value and how carefully it was measured. Learning to write measurements properly, and to judge how good they are, is the first skill of a working chemist.

In this lesson you will use SI units and significant figures, tell accuracy from precision, and describe the steps of the scientific method.

## SI units

:::definition SI units
The **SI units** (Système International) are the agreed units of measurement used by scientists everywhere. Each physical quantity has one **base unit**, and other units are built from them.
:::

| Quantity | SI base unit | Symbol |
|---|---|---|
| Mass | kilogram | kg |
| Length | metre | m |
| Time | second | s |
| Temperature | kelvin | K |
| Amount of substance | mole | mol |
| Electric current | ampere | A |

Chemists also use units built from these, such as the cubic decimetre (dm³, the same as a litre) for volume and mol dm⁻³ for concentration. A temperature in kelvin is the Celsius temperature plus 273.

## Significant figures

The **significant figures** in a measurement are the digits that carry meaning about its size. Count from the first non-zero digit:

- 25.40 has 4 significant figures (the final zero after the decimal point shows the reading was made to 0.01).
- 0.0036 has 2 (the leading zeros only place the decimal point).
- 1.005 has 4 (zeros between digits count).

When you multiply or divide measurements, give the answer to the **fewest** significant figures of any measurement you used.

:::example Writing a density to the right precision
A liquid has a mass of 12.46 g and a volume of 9.8 cm³. What is its density?
@step Density = mass ÷ volume = 12.46 ÷ 9.8 = 1.27142… g cm⁻³.
@step The volume has only 2 significant figures, so the answer has 2.
@answer 1.3 g cm⁻³
:::

## Accuracy and precision

**Accuracy** is how close a result is to the true value. **Precision** is how close repeated results are to each other. A set of titres of 24.10, 24.15 and 24.10 cm³ is precise; it is also accurate only if the true titre is near 24.1 cm³.

A **random error**, such as misreading a meniscus, scatters results either way and is reduced by repeating and averaging. A **systematic error**, such as a balance that always reads 0.20 g high, shifts every result the same way: the results can still be precise, but they are not accurate.

:::mistake Precise is not the same as correct
Three readings that agree closely can all be wrong in the same way if the apparatus has a systematic error.
:::

## The scientific method

Science moves from **observation** to a **hypothesis**, a testable explanation, which is checked by **experiment**. When many experiments agree, a pattern may be stated as a **law** (such as the conservation of mass), and a wide-ranging explanation that has survived many tests becomes a **theory** (such as the kinetic theory). Choosing the right apparatus matters too: a pipette measures 25.0 cm³ far more accurately than a measuring cylinder or a beaker.

:::summary
- Each quantity has one SI base unit; chemists also use dm³ and mol dm⁻³.
- Significant figures start at the first non-zero digit; give a calculated answer to the fewest significant figures used.
- Accuracy is closeness to the true value; precision is closeness of repeats to each other.
- Observation, hypothesis, experiment, then a law or a theory.
:::
