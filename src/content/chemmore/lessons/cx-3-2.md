A factory can run a reaction perfectly, losing not one gram, and still turn half of what it buys into waste. The reason is in the equation itself: the atoms that end up in the other products were paid for, carried in and heated, but they are not in the thing the factory sells.

In this lesson you will calculate the atom economy of a reaction from its balanced equation, use it to find the greatest mass of product, and compare two routes to the same product.

## What atom economy measures

:::definition Atom economy
The **atom economy** of a reaction is the percentage of the mass of the reactants that ends up in the desired product. It is worked out from the balanced equation alone.
:::

The formula uses relative formula masses, $M_r$:

$\text{atom economy} = \dfrac{\text{total } M_r \text{ of the desired product}}{\text{total } M_r \text{ of all the reactants}} \times 100\%$

"Total" matters: if the equation has a 2 in front of a formula, its $M_r$ is counted twice. Mass is conserved, so the total for the reactants equals the total for all the products, and either can go on the bottom.

A reaction with only **one product** has an atom economy of 100%, because every atom of the reactants ends up in that product. Making ammonia is an example: $\ce{N2 + 3H2 -> 2NH3}$.

:::example Using the totals
Lead is extracted by heating lead(II) oxide with carbon: $\ce{2PbO + C -> 2Pb + CO2}$. The reactants have a total relative formula mass of 458, and the lead has a total of 414. What is the atom economy for making lead?
@step Atom economy $= \dfrac{414}{458} \times 100$.
@answer 90.4%
:::

## Calculating it from the equation

:::example Making hydrogen from methane
Hydrogen is made from methane and steam: $\ce{CH4 + H2O -> CO + 3H2}$. What is the atom economy for making hydrogen? (Relative atomic masses: C = 12, H = 1, O = 16.)
@step Relative formula masses: $\ce{CH4}$ $= 16$, $\ce{H2O}$ $= 18$, $\ce{H2}$ $= 2$.
@step Total for the reactants $= 16 + 18 = 34$.
@step The equation makes $\ce{3H2}$, so the desired product totals $3 \times 2 = 6$.
@step Atom economy $= \dfrac{6}{34} \times 100$.
@answer 17.6%
:::

Only 17.6% of the mass that goes in comes out as hydrogen. The rest is carbon monoxide, which is wasted unless a use is found for it.

:::mistake Count the big numbers
The commonest slip is to use the $M_r$ of one formula unit and forget the number in front. In $\ce{2PbO + C -> 2Pb + CO2}$, the lead counts as $2 \times 207 = 414$, and the lead(II) oxide as $2 \times 223 = 446$.
:::

## The greatest mass of product

Atom economy is a fraction of the mass, so it gives the greatest mass of product directly, as long as the reactants are mixed in exactly the amounts the equation needs.

:::example Mass of hydrogen
$500\ \text{kg}$ of methane and steam, in exactly the amounts the equation needs, react completely. What mass of hydrogen is made?
@step The fraction of the mass that becomes hydrogen is $\dfrac{6}{34}$.
@step Mass of hydrogen $= 500 \times \dfrac{6}{34}$.
@answer $88.2\ \text{kg}$
:::

## Atom economy and yield

:::definition Percentage yield
The **percentage yield** compares the mass of product actually collected with the greatest mass that could have been made: $\text{yield} = \dfrac{\text{mass collected}}{\text{greatest possible mass}} \times 100\%$.
:::

The two measures answer different questions. Atom economy is fixed by the equation: it says how much of the mass **could** end up in the product. Yield depends on how the reaction is carried out: product is lost when a reaction does not finish, when other reactions happen as well, and when the product is separated and collected.

| | Atom economy | Percentage yield |
|---|---|---|
| Found from | The balanced equation | Measurements in an experiment |
| Raised by | Choosing a different reaction, or finding a use for the other products | Carrying out the reaction more carefully |

:::example Both together
A reaction has an atom economy of 60% for its product, and a percentage yield of 80%. A factory starts with $200\ \text{tonnes}$ of reactants in the right amounts. What mass of product does it collect?
@step Greatest possible mass $= 200 \times 60\% = 120\ \text{tonnes}$.
@step Mass collected $= 120 \times 80\% = 96\ \text{tonnes}$.
@answer $96\ \text{tonnes}$
:::

## Comparing two routes

When a product can be made in more than one way, a higher atom economy means less waste to deal with and less raw material to buy. It is not the only thing a company weighs: the yield, the speed of the reaction, the energy needed, the cost of the reactants and whether the other products can be sold all matter too.

:::example Two ways to make copper
Route 1: $\ce{2CuO + C -> 2Cu + CO2}$. Route 2: $\ce{CuO + H2 -> Cu + H2O}$. Which has the higher atom economy for copper? (Relative atomic masses: Cu = 63.5, O = 16, C = 12, H = 1.)
@step Route 1: reactants $= (2 \times 79.5) + 12 = 171$; copper $= 2 \times 63.5 = 127$. Atom economy $= \dfrac{127}{171} \times 100 = 74.3\%$.
@step Route 2: reactants $= 79.5 + 2 = 81.5$; copper $= 63.5$. Atom economy $= \dfrac{63.5}{81.5} \times 100 = 77.9\%$.
@answer Route 2, with 77.9% against 74.3%.
:::

:::summary
- **Atom economy** = (total $M_r$ of the desired product ÷ total $M_r$ of all the reactants) × 100%. Count the numbers in front of the formulae.
- A reaction with one product has an atom economy of 100%.
- Greatest mass of product = mass of reactants × atom economy, when the reactants are in the amounts the equation needs.
- Atom economy comes from the equation; **percentage yield** comes from what is actually collected. A process can have a high yield and still waste most of its atoms.
- A higher atom economy means less waste. Finding a use for the other products also cuts the waste.
:::
