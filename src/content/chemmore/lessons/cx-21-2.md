A darker cup of tea absorbs more light than a pale one. A spectrophotometer measures exactly how much light a solution absorbs, and from that number chemists can find the concentration of a coloured substance without weighing a single crystal.

In this lesson you will use the Beer–Lambert law, and find unknown concentrations from a calibration graph.

## The Beer–Lambert law

:::definition The Beer–Lambert law
$$A = \varepsilon b c$$
where $A$ is the absorbance (no unit), $\varepsilon$ is the molar absorptivity in L mol⁻¹ cm⁻¹, $b$ is the path length of the cuvette in cm, and $c$ is the concentration in mol/L.
:::

The molar absorptivity depends on the substance and on the wavelength, so measurements are made at the wavelength the substance absorbs most strongly, called $\lambda_\text{max}$. That gives the largest absorbance for each concentration, so small differences are easier to measure.

:::example Finding a concentration
A solution in a 1.00 cm cuvette has an absorbance of 0.540. The molar absorptivity is $1.80 \times 10^{4}$ L mol⁻¹ cm⁻¹. What is the concentration?
@step Rearrange: $c = \dfrac{A}{\varepsilon b}$.
@step $c = \dfrac{0.540}{1.80 \times 10^{4} \times 1.00}$.
@answer $c = 3.00 \times 10^{-5}$ mol/L.
:::

## Calibration graphs

In practice chemists rarely know $\varepsilon$ in advance. Instead they measure the absorbance of several **standard solutions** of known concentration and plot a calibration graph.

Here we see that the absorbance rises by 0.20 for each $1 \times 10^{-4}$ mol/L. So an unknown solution with an absorbance of 0.70 has a concentration of $\dfrac{0.70}{0.20} = 3.5 \times 10^{-4}$ mol/L. If the sample was diluted before it was measured, multiply by the dilution factor to get the concentration of the original.

## Errors in the measurement

Anything that changes the measured absorbance changes the concentration worked out from it. Fingerprints or scratches on the cuvette absorb and scatter light, so the absorbance and the concentration come out too high. A cuvette that is still wet with water dilutes the sample, so both come out too low. A reading at a wavelength other than $\lambda_\text{max}$ gives a smaller absorbance and a less sensitive measurement.

:::summary
- $A = \varepsilon b c$: absorbance is proportional to concentration and to path length.
- Measure at $\lambda_\text{max}$, where the substance absorbs most strongly.
- A calibration graph of standards gives a straight line through the origin, from which you read an unknown's concentration.
- Errors that raise the absorbance make the concentration too high, and those that lower it make the concentration too low.
:::
