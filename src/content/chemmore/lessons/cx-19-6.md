Why is chloroethanoic acid about eighty times stronger than ethanoic acid, when the chlorine is two atoms away from the acidic hydrogen? Why is phenol acidic while ethanol is not? The answers come from following electrons: how bonds break, and how atoms nearby push or pull electron density through the molecule.

In this lesson you will tell homolytic from heterolytic fission, classify nucleophiles, electrophiles and free radicals, and use the inductive and mesomeric effects to compare the strengths of organic acids and bases.

## Bond fission

:::definition Bond fission
**Bond fission** is the breaking of a covalent bond. In **homolytic fission** each atom keeps one electron of the shared pair, forming two **free radicals**. In **heterolytic fission** one atom takes both electrons, forming a positive and a negative ion.
:::

| Fission | Example | Favoured by |
|---|---|---|
| Homolytic | Cl–Cl → 2Cl• | ultraviolet light, high temperature, gas phase |
| Heterolytic | (CH₃)₃C–Br → (CH₃)₃C⁺ + Br⁻ | polar bonds and polar solvents |

A polar bond breaks so that the more electronegative atom takes both electrons, so a C–Br bond gives a bromide ion.

## Kinds of reagent

- A **nucleophile** has a lone pair and attacks an electron-poor carbon: $\ce{OH-}$, $\ce{CN-}$, $\ce{NH3}$, $\ce{H2O}$.
- An **electrophile** is electron-poor and accepts a pair from an electron-rich site, such as a double bond or a benzene ring: $\ce{H+}$, $\ce{NO2+}$, $\ce{Br+}$, carbocations.
- A **free radical** has an unpaired electron: Cl•, CH₃•.

When bromine approaches ethene, the π electrons polarise the Br–Br molecule, and it adds as an electrophile.

## The inductive effect

The **inductive effect** is the shift of electrons along a chain of σ bonds. Electronegative atoms such as halogens **withdraw** electrons (−I); alkyl groups **push** electrons (+I). The effect weakens quickly with distance.

An acid is stronger when its anion is more stable. A −I group spreads the negative charge of the carboxylate ion, so

$$\ce{CH3COOH} < \ce{ClCH2COOH} < \ce{Cl2CHCOOH} < \ce{Cl3CCOOH} \quad \text{(increasing acid strength)}$$

and fluoroethanoic acid is stronger than chloroethanoic acid, because fluorine is more electronegative. Alkyl groups (+I) make the anion less stable, so methanoic acid is stronger than ethanoic acid, which is stronger than propanoic acid.

## The mesomeric effect

The **mesomeric (resonance) effect** is the spreading of π electrons or lone pairs over several atoms through overlapping p orbitals. In the ethanoate ion the negative charge is shared by both oxygens; in the phenoxide ion it spreads into the benzene ring. So phenol is a stronger acid than ethanol, though much weaker than ethanoic acid.

:::example Comparing base strengths
Put ammonia, methylamine and phenylamine in order of increasing base strength.
@step The methyl group pushes electrons onto nitrogen (+I), so methylamine's lone pair is more available than ammonia's.
@step In phenylamine the lone pair is partly delocalised into the ring (mesomeric effect), so it is less available.
@answer Phenylamine < ammonia < methylamine
:::

:::summary
- Homolytic fission gives free radicals; heterolytic fission gives ions.
- Nucleophiles donate lone pairs; electrophiles accept pairs; free radicals have an unpaired electron.
- −I groups (halogens) strengthen acids; +I groups (alkyl) weaken acids and strengthen bases.
- Delocalisation stabilises phenoxide and carboxylate ions and weakens phenylamine as a base.
:::
