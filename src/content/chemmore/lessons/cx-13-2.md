Long before anyone could see an atom, chemists weighed the substances that went into and came out of reactions, and found patterns that never failed. Those patterns, the laws of chemical combination, were the evidence that led John Dalton to propose that matter is made of atoms.

In this lesson you will use the laws of conservation of mass, constant composition and multiple proportions, and Gay-Lussac's law of combining volumes.

## Conservation of mass and constant composition

:::definition Law of conservation of mass
The **law of conservation of mass** says that matter is neither created nor destroyed in a chemical reaction, so the total mass of the products equals the total mass of the reactants.
:::

When calcium carbonate is heated in an open crucible, it seems to lose mass, but only because carbon dioxide escapes: the mass lost is the mass of gas given off. When magnesium burns, it gains mass because oxygen from the air joins it.

The **law of constant composition** (definite proportions) says that a pure compound always contains the same elements in the same proportions by mass, however it is made. Copper(II) oxide made by heating copper in air and copper(II) oxide made from copper(II) carbonate both contain 79.9% copper.

:::example Using constant composition
A sample of water contains 2.00 g of hydrogen combined with 16.0 g of oxygen. What mass of oxygen is combined with 5.00 g of hydrogen in another sample?
@step The ratio of oxygen to hydrogen is always 16.0 : 2.00 = 8 : 1.
@step Mass of oxygen = 5.00 × 8 = 40.0 g.
@answer 40.0 g
:::

## Multiple proportions

The **law of multiple proportions** says that when two elements form more than one compound, the masses of one element that combine with a fixed mass of the other are in a simple whole-number ratio. In carbon monoxide, 12 g of carbon combines with 16 g of oxygen; in carbon dioxide, 12 g of carbon combines with 32 g of oxygen. The ratio 16 : 32 is 1 : 2.

To test the law, scale both compounds to the same mass of the first element, then compare the masses of the second.

## Gay-Lussac's law of combining volumes

**Gay-Lussac's law** says that when gases react, the volumes of the gases that react and of any gaseous products are in a simple whole-number ratio, if all are measured at the same temperature and pressure. The ratio is the ratio of the coefficients in the equation:

$$\ce{N2(g) + 3H2(g) -> 2NH3(g)}$$

So 20 cm³ of nitrogen reacts with 60 cm³ of hydrogen and gives 40 cm³ of ammonia. Avogadro explained this: equal volumes of gases at the same temperature and pressure contain equal numbers of molecules.

:::mistake Liquids do not count
Volume ratios only apply to gases. If water is formed as a liquid at room temperature, its volume is too small to count.
:::

:::summary
- Mass is conserved in every reaction; apparent gains or losses come from gases joining or leaving.
- A pure compound always has the same composition by mass.
- Masses of one element combining with a fixed mass of another are in a simple ratio (multiple proportions).
- Reacting gas volumes are in the ratio of the coefficients, at the same temperature and pressure.
:::
