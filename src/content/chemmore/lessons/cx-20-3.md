Two hydrogen atoms far apart feel almost nothing. Bring them closer and each nucleus starts to attract the other atom's electron, so the energy falls. Push them too close and the two nuclei repel. The bond sits at the distance in between, where the energy is lowest.

In this lesson you will read potential energy curves, and explain the properties of ionic solids, metals and alloys.

## The potential energy curve of a bond

:::definition Bond length and bond energy
On a graph of potential energy against the distance between two nuclei, the **bond length** is the distance at the lowest point, and the **bond energy** is the depth of the well below zero (the energy needed to pull the atoms fully apart).
:::

## Ionic solids

In an ionic solid, each ion is surrounded by ions of the opposite charge in a repeating lattice. By Coulomb's law, the attraction is strongest when the ions have **large charges** and are **small**, so their centres are close. That is why $\ce{MgO}$ (ions of charge 2+ and 2−) melts at about 2800 °C while $\ce{NaCl}$ (1+ and 1−) melts at about 800 °C.

:::example Comparing two ionic solids
Which has the higher melting point, potassium chloride, $\ce{KCl}$, or calcium oxide, $\ce{CaO}$? The ions are of similar sizes.
@step $\ce{KCl}$ is made of $\ce{K+}$ and $\ce{Cl-}$ ions, with charges of 1+ and 1−.
@step $\ce{CaO}$ is made of $\ce{Ca^2+}$ and $\ce{O^2-}$ ions, with charges of 2+ and 2−, so by Coulomb's law the attraction between each pair is about four times as strong.
@answer Calcium oxide has the higher melting point (about 2600 °C, against about 770 °C for potassium chloride).
:::

Ionic solids are hard, but they are **brittle**: a blow shifts one layer of ions, so ions of the same charge end up side by side and the repulsion splits the crystal. They do not conduct as solids, because the ions cannot move, but they do when molten or dissolved.

## Metals and alloys

A metal is a lattice of positive ions in a sea of delocalised electrons. The electrons move freely, so metals conduct, and the layers of ions can slide without breaking the bonding, so metals are malleable and ductile.

:::definition Interstitial and substitutional alloys
- In a **substitutional alloy**, atoms of a similar size replace some of the host metal's atoms, as zinc does in brass.
- In an **interstitial alloy**, much smaller atoms fit into the gaps between the host atoms, as carbon does in steel. The small atoms make it harder for the layers to slide, so the alloy is stronger and less malleable.
:::

:::summary
- The lowest point of a potential energy curve gives the bond length, and the depth of the well gives the bond energy.
- Ionic solids with small, highly charged ions have the strongest attractions and the highest melting points; they are brittle.
- Metals conduct and are malleable because of their sea of delocalised electrons.
- Substitutional alloys swap in atoms of a similar size; interstitial alloys fit small atoms into the gaps.
:::
