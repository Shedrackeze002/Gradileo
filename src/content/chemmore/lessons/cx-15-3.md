Sugar dissolves in tea, but only up to a point: keep adding it and some stays at the bottom of the cup. Every solute has a limit like this, which depends on the temperature. Chemists measure that limit, plot it against temperature, and use the curve to work out how much solid crystallises when a hot solution cools.

In this lesson you will calculate solubility in g dm⁻³ and mol dm⁻³, read solubility curves, and work out the mass of crystals formed on cooling.

## Solubility

:::definition Solubility
The **solubility** of a substance is the greatest amount of it that dissolves in a given amount of solvent at a stated temperature, giving a **saturated solution** in equilibrium with undissolved solid.
:::

Solubility can be given in g per 100 g of water, in g dm⁻³ of solution, or in mol dm⁻³:

$$\text{solubility (g dm}^{-3}) = \frac{\text{mass of solute} \times 1000}{\text{volume of saturated solution in cm}^3} \qquad \text{solubility (mol dm}^{-3}) = \frac{\text{g dm}^{-3}}{M}$$

:::example Solubility from an evaporation
25.0 cm³ of a saturated solution of potassium chloride (M = 74.5) leaves 8.94 g of solid on evaporation. Find its solubility in mol dm⁻³.
@step Solubility = 8.94 × 1000 ÷ 25.0 = 357.6 g dm⁻³.
@step In mol dm⁻³: 357.6 ÷ 74.5 = 4.80.
@answer 4.80 mol dm⁻³
:::

In the laboratory, a dish is weighed empty, with saturated solution, and again after evaporating to dryness. The mass of salt is the last reading minus the empty dish, and the mass of water is the solution minus the salt; dividing gives the solubility per 100 g of water.

## Solubility curves

| Temperature (°C) | 0 | 20 | 40 | 60 | 80 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Potassium nitrate (g per 100 g water) | 13 | 32 | 64 | 110 | 169 |
| Sodium chloride (g per 100 g water) | 36 | 36 | 37 | 37 | 38 |

The solubility of most solids rises with temperature, potassium nitrate steeply and sodium chloride hardly at all. When a saturated solution cools, the extra solute crystallises out:

$$\text{mass of crystals} = (\text{solubility at higher } T - \text{solubility at lower } T) \times \frac{\text{mass of water}}{100}$$

For example, cooling a solution of potassium nitrate in 50 g of water, saturated at 60 °C, to 20 °C gives $(110 - 32) \times \dfrac{50}{100} = 39$ g of crystals.

Sodium chloride is obtained from sea water by evaporation, because cooling gives almost no crystals. Gases behave the other way: they become **less** soluble as the water warms.

:::summary
- Solubility in g dm⁻³ = mass × 1000 ÷ volume in cm³; divide by molar mass for mol dm⁻³.
- A solubility curve shows how solubility changes with temperature.
- Crystals on cooling = drop in solubility × mass of water ÷ 100.
- Most solids get more soluble when heated; gases get less soluble.
:::
