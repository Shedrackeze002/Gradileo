How do chemists know that chlorine is a mixture of two isotopes, or the mass of a new drug molecule to four decimal places? They use a mass spectrometer, which sorts ions by their mass and counts how many of each there are.

In this lesson you will describe how a time of flight mass spectrometer works, and use a mass spectrum to calculate a relative atomic mass.

## The four stages

:::definition Time of flight mass spectrometer
An instrument that turns a sample into positive ions, gives them all the same kinetic energy, and times how long each takes to fly along a tube. Lighter ions arrive first. The whole instrument is kept under a **vacuum**, so the ions do not collide with molecules of air.
:::

| Stage | What happens |
|---|---|
| **Ionisation** | The sample is turned into positive ions (see below). |
| **Acceleration** | An electric field gives every ion of the same charge the **same kinetic energy**. |
| **Ion drift** | The ions fly along the flight tube. Lighter ions move faster, so they separate. |
| **Detection** | Each ion gains an electron at the detector, making a current. The bigger the current, the more ions of that mass. |

Because every ion has the same kinetic energy, $\tfrac{1}{2}mv^2$, an ion of small mass $m$ must have a large speed $v$. So the time of flight is proportional to $\sqrt{m}$.

## Two ways to make ions

- **Electrospray ionisation.** The sample is dissolved in a volatile solvent and sprayed from a fine needle at a high voltage. Each molecule gains a proton: $\ce{X(g) + H+ -> XH+(g)}$. This is gentle, so large molecules such as proteins stay whole. The ion’s mass is one more than the molecule’s.
- **Electron impact.** The vaporised sample is hit by high-energy electrons from an electron gun, which knock out an electron: $\ce{X(g) -> X+(g) + e-}$. This often breaks molecules into fragments.

:::check cx-tof-stages 2
:::

:::example Time of flight and mass
An ion of mass 20 takes $15.0\ \mu\text{s}$ to cross the flight tube. How long does an ion of mass 22 take?
@step $t \propto \sqrt{m}$, so $t_2 = t_1\sqrt{\dfrac{m_2}{m_1}}$.
@step $t_2 = 15.0 \times \sqrt{\dfrac{22}{20}} = 15.73$
@answer $15.73\ \mu\text{s}$
:::

## Relative atomic mass from a spectrum

A mass spectrum of an element shows one peak for each isotope. The position of a peak is its mass-to-charge ratio, $m/z$; for ions of charge +1 this is just the mass. The height of the peak shows the **abundance** of that isotope.

:::definition Relative atomic mass
The mean mass of an atom of an element, taking account of its isotopes and their abundances, compared with one-twelfth of the mass of an atom of carbon-12.
:::

$$A_r = \dfrac{\sum (\text{mass} \times \text{abundance})}{\sum \text{abundance}}$$

:::example Relative atomic mass of copper
A sample of copper is 69.2% copper-63 and 30.8% copper-65. Find its relative atomic mass.
@step $A_r = \dfrac{(63 \times 69.2) + (65 \times 30.8)}{100}$
@step $= \dfrac{4359.6 + 2002.0}{100} = 63.62$
@answer $63.62$
:::

:::tip Check your answer
The relative atomic mass must lie between the lightest and heaviest isotopes, and closer to the more abundant one. Copper’s 63.62 is close to 63, as most of the atoms are copper-63.
:::

## Working backwards

If you know the relative atomic mass, you can find the abundances. Call the percentage of one isotope $x$; the other is then $100 - x$.

:::example Finding an abundance
Boron is boron-10 and boron-11 only, and its relative atomic mass is 10.80. What percentage of the atoms are boron-10?
@step $\dfrac{10x + 11(100 - x)}{100} = 10.80$
@step $1100 - x = 1080$, so $x = 20$
@answer 20% boron-10
:::

:::mistake Dividing by the wrong total
When the abundances are relative peak heights, such as 3 and 1, divide by their sum, 4, not by 100.
:::

:::summary
- A time of flight mass spectrometer has four stages: **ionisation, acceleration, ion drift, detection**, all under a vacuum.
- Electrospray ionisation adds a proton ($\ce{XH+}$); electron impact knocks out an electron ($\ce{X+}$).
- All the ions get the same kinetic energy, so lighter ions are faster: time of flight $\propto \sqrt{m}$.
- The detector current shows the abundance of each ion.
- $A_r = \sum(\text{mass} \times \text{abundance}) \div \sum \text{abundance}$.
:::
