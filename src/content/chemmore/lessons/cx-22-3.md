Put a pan of ice on the stove and watch a thermometer in it. The temperature climbs, then stops at 0 °C while the ice melts, climbs again, and stops for a long time at 100 °C while the water boils. All the while the stove is giving out heat at the same rate.

In this lesson you will calculate the energy of phase changes and read heating curves.

## The enthalpy of a phase change

Melting and boiling separate particles that attract each other, so they take in energy. Freezing and condensing let the particles come back together, so they give out the same amount of energy.

:::definition Enthalpies of fusion and vaporisation
- The **molar enthalpy of fusion**, $\Delta H_\text{fus}$, is the energy needed to melt one mole of a solid at its melting point.
- The **molar enthalpy of vaporisation**, $\Delta H_\text{vap}$, is the energy needed to boil one mole of a liquid at its boiling point.

For $n$ moles, $q = n\,\Delta H$.
:::

$\Delta H_\text{vap}$ is much larger than $\Delta H_\text{fus}$, because boiling separates the particles completely while melting only loosens them. For water, $\Delta H_\text{fus} = 6.01$ kJ/mol and $\Delta H_\text{vap} = 40.7$ kJ/mol.

On the sloped parts (A, C and E) one state warms up, and the energy goes into making the particles move faster, with $q = mc\Delta T$. On the flat parts (B and D) the state is changing, and all the energy goes into separating the particles, so the temperature stays still.

:::example From ice to steam
How much energy is needed to turn 10.0 g of water at 50 °C into steam at 100 °C? Use $c = 4.18$ J/(g·°C) for water, $\Delta H_\text{vap} = 40.7$ kJ/mol and a molar mass of 18.0 g/mol.
@step Warm the water: $10.0 \times 4.18 \times 50 = 2090$ J $= 2.09$ kJ.
@step Boil it: $\dfrac{10.0}{18.0} \times 40.7 = 22.6$ kJ.
@answer Total $= 2.09 + 22.6 = 24.7$ kJ, most of it for boiling.
:::

:::summary
- Melting and boiling take in energy; freezing and condensing give out the same amount.
- $q = n\,\Delta H_\text{fus}$ or $q = n\,\Delta H_\text{vap}$ for a phase change, and $q = mc\Delta T$ for warming one state.
- On a heating curve the flat parts are phase changes and the sloped parts are one state warming.
- $\Delta H_\text{vap}$ is much larger than $\Delta H_\text{fus}$, so boiling takes far longer than melting.
:::
