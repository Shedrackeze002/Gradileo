Silver chloride is called insoluble, yet a tiny amount of it does dissolve, and that tiny amount follows strict rules. The solubility product describes the equilibrium between a sparingly soluble solid and its ions. With it, chemists can predict whether mixing two solutions will give a precipitate, and why adding a common ion or changing the pH changes how much dissolves.

In this lesson you will write solubility product expressions, convert between $K_{sp}$ and molar solubility, compare the ionic product with $K_{sp}$ to predict precipitation, and explain the common ion effect and the effect of pH.

## The solubility product

:::definition Solubility product
The **solubility product**, $K_{sp}$, is the equilibrium constant for a sparingly soluble salt dissolving in water. For $\ce{A_xB_y(s) <=> xA^{y+}(aq) + yB^{x-}(aq)}$, $K_{sp} = [\ce{A^{y+}}]^x[\ce{B^{x-}}]^y$, at a stated temperature.
:::

The solid does not appear in the expression, because its concentration does not change. So

$$K_{sp}(\ce{AgCl}) = [\ce{Ag+}][\ce{Cl-}] \qquad K_{sp}(\ce{PbI2}) = [\ce{Pb^2+}][\ce{I-}]^2$$

## Solubility and $K_{sp}$

If the **molar solubility** is $s$ mol dm⁻³, each formula unit that dissolves gives $x$ of A and $y$ of B, so $[\text{A}] = xs$ and $[\text{B}] = ys$.

- For a 1 : 1 salt such as AgCl, $K_{sp} = s \times s = s^2$, so $s = \sqrt{K_{sp}}$.
- For a 1 : 2 salt such as $\ce{PbI2}$, $K_{sp} = s \times (2s)^2 = 4s^3$, so $s = \sqrt[3]{K_{sp}/4}$.

:::example Molar solubility of lead(II) iodide
$K_{sp}$ for $\ce{PbI2}$ is $7.1 \times 10^{-9}$ at 25 °C. Find its molar solubility.
@step $K_{sp} = s(2s)^2 = 4s^3$
@step $s^3 = \dfrac{7.1 \times 10^{-9}}{4} = 1.775 \times 10^{-9}$
@answer $s = 1.21 \times 10^{-3}$ mol dm⁻³
:::

Only salts of the same formula type can be compared by $K_{sp}$ alone: AgCl ($1.8 \times 10^{-10}$) is more soluble than AgBr ($5.0 \times 10^{-13}$).

## Will a precipitate form?

Work out the **ionic product**, $Q$, from the concentrations in the mixture, in the same form as $K_{sp}$. Remember that mixing equal volumes halves each concentration.

- If $Q > K_{sp}$, the solution is more than saturated, so a **precipitate forms**.
- If $Q < K_{sp}$, the solution is unsaturated, so **no precipitate** forms.

## The common ion effect

A sparingly soluble salt is **less soluble** in a solution that already contains one of its ions. In 0.10 mol dm⁻³ sodium chloride, the chloride concentration is fixed at about 0.10, so the solubility of silver chloride is $s = \dfrac{K_{sp}}{0.10}$, far smaller than in pure water. Passing hydrogen chloride into saturated brine makes sodium chloride crystals appear for the same reason.

## The effect of pH

A salt whose anion is a **base**, such as a hydroxide, carbonate or fluoride, is **more soluble in acid**. The hydrogen ions remove the anion (as water, carbon dioxide or HF), so more solid dissolves to replace it: magnesium hydroxide and calcium carbonate both dissolve in dilute acid. A salt whose anion comes from a strong acid, such as the chloride in AgCl, is hardly affected by pH.

:::mistake Compare like with like
A smaller $K_{sp}$ means a less soluble salt only when the salts have the same formula type, such as two 1 : 1 salts.
:::

:::

:::summary
- $K_{sp}$ is the product of the ion concentrations, each raised to its coefficient; the solid is left out.
- 1 : 1 salt: $K_{sp} = s^2$. 1 : 2 salt: $K_{sp} = 4s^3$.
- $Q > K_{sp}$: a precipitate forms. $Q < K_{sp}$: no precipitate.
- A common ion lowers solubility; acid raises the solubility of salts with basic anions.
:::
