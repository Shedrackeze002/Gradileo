Nitrogen makes up nearly four-fifths of the air, yet plants cannot use it directly, and it hardly reacts with anything. Turning it into ammonia, and ammonia into nitric acid, took some of the most important chemistry of the last century, and it feeds billions of people through fertilisers.

In this lesson you will describe nitrogen and ammonia, their preparation, tests and uses, calculate the percentage of nitrogen in fertilisers, and describe how nitric acid is made, how it reacts, and the effect of heat on nitrates.

## Nitrogen

:::definition Nitrogen fixation
**Nitrogen fixation** is the change of unreactive nitrogen gas into compounds such as ammonia and nitrates that plants can use.
:::

Nitrogen is unreactive because of the very strong triple bond in $\ce{N#N}$. It is obtained industrially from liquid air, and in the laboratory by warming ammonium chloride with sodium nitrite, which gives ammonium nitrite that decomposes: $\ce{NH4NO2 -> N2 + 2H2O}$. It is used to fill food packets, as liquid nitrogen for freezing, and to make ammonia.

## Ammonia

Ammonia is made in the laboratory by heating an ammonium salt with an alkali, and industrially by the **Haber process**:

$$\ce{N2 + 3H2 <=> 2NH3}$$

at about 450 °C and 200 atmospheres over an iron catalyst. Ammonia is a colourless gas with a sharp smell, lighter than air and extremely soluble: in the fountain experiment, water rushes into a flask of ammonia and the litmus in it turns blue. It gives dense white fumes with hydrogen chloride, and it reduces hot copper(II) oxide:

$$\ce{3CuO + 2NH3 -> 3Cu + N2 + 3H2O}$$

Its main use is making fertilisers such as ammonium nitrate and urea; it is also used to make nitric acid and nylon, and as a refrigerant.

@step There are 2 nitrogen atoms: 2 × 14 = 28.
@answer 28 ÷ 80 × 100 = 35.0%

## Nitric acid

In the laboratory, nitric acid is made by heating potassium nitrate with concentrated sulfuric acid in an all-glass apparatus, because the acid attacks rubber and cork: $\ce{KNO3 + H2SO4 -> KHSO4 + HNO3}$. Industrially, the **Ostwald process** oxidises ammonia over a platinum–rhodium gauze at about 900 °C:

$$\ce{4NH3 + 5O2 -> 4NO + 6H2O}$$

The nitrogen monoxide is oxidised to nitrogen dioxide, which is absorbed in water with more air to give nitric acid. Concentrated nitric acid turns yellow in light because it decomposes and the brown nitrogen dioxide dissolves in it. It is a strong oxidising agent: with copper it gives brown fumes of nitrogen dioxide, not hydrogen.

$$\ce{Cu + 4HNO3 -> Cu(NO3)2 + 2NO2 + 2H2O}$$

## The effect of heat on nitrates

| Nitrate | Products on heating |
|---|---|
| Sodium, potassium | the nitrite and oxygen: $\ce{2KNO3 -> 2KNO2 + O2}$ |
| Most other metals (Ca, Mg, Zn, Pb, Cu) | the oxide, nitrogen dioxide and oxygen: $\ce{2Pb(NO3)2 -> 2PbO + 4NO2 + O2}$ |
| Silver (and mercury) | the metal, nitrogen dioxide and oxygen |
| Ammonium | dinitrogen oxide and water: $\ce{NH4NO3 -> N2O + 2H2O}$ |

:::summary
- Nitrogen is unreactive because of its triple bond; it comes from liquid air.
- Ammonia is made by the Haber process; it is very soluble, alkaline, lighter than air, and reduces CuO.
- % nitrogen = (number of N atoms × 14) ÷ molar mass × 100.
- Nitric acid is made by the Ostwald process and is a strong oxidising agent.
- Na and K nitrates give the nitrite and oxygen; most others give the oxide, NO₂ and O₂.
:::
