Pass an electric current through molten lead(II) bromide and it comes apart: silvery lead collects at one electrode and brown bromine vapour rises from the other. Do the same with salty water and you get neither sodium nor, at first sight, anything you expected: hydrogen bubbles from one electrode and chlorine from the other. The water has joined in.

In this lesson you will predict what forms at each electrode for a molten compound and for a solution in water, and write the half equations that show the electrons moving.

## Electrolysis of a molten compound

:::definition Electrolysis
**Electrolysis** is the breaking down of an ionic compound by passing an electric current through it. The compound must be molten or dissolved in water, so that its ions are free to move. The liquid that conducts is called the **electrolyte**.
:::

:::definition Cathode and anode
The **cathode** is the negative electrode, and the **anode** is the positive electrode. Opposite charges attract, so positive ions move to the cathode and negative ions move to the anode.
:::

A solid ionic compound does not conduct, because its ions are held in fixed places. Melting frees them. In a molten compound of one metal and one non-metal there are only two kinds of ion, so the products are easy to predict:

- at the **cathode**, the positive metal ions gain electrons and become the **metal**;
- at the **anode**, the negative non-metal ions lose electrons and become the **non-metal**.

:::example Molten zinc chloride
What forms at each electrode when molten zinc chloride is electrolysed?
@step The ions are $\ce{Zn^2+}$ and $\ce{Cl-}$.
@step $\ce{Zn^2+}$ ions move to the cathode and become zinc metal.
@step $\ce{Cl-}$ ions move to the anode and become chlorine gas.
@answer Zinc at the cathode and chlorine at the anode.
:::

## Electrolysis of a solution

Water itself breaks up very slightly into ions: $\ce{H2O <=> H+ + OH-}$. So a solution of an ionic compound holds four kinds of ion, and at each electrode two of them compete. Two rules settle which one is **discharged**, that is, which gains or loses electrons there.

| Electrode | What forms |
|---|---|
| Cathode (negative) | **Hydrogen**, unless the metal is less reactive than hydrogen (copper, silver). Then the **metal** forms. |
| Anode (positive) | **Oxygen**, unless the solution contains chloride, bromide or iodide ions. Then the **halogen** forms. |

:::example Sodium chloride solution
What forms at each electrode when sodium chloride solution is electrolysed, and what is left in the solution?
@step The ions are $\ce{Na+}$ and $\ce{Cl-}$ from the salt, and $\ce{H+}$ and $\ce{OH-}$ from the water.
@step Cathode: sodium is more reactive than hydrogen, so hydrogen forms.
@step Anode: chloride ions are present, so chlorine forms.
@step The $\ce{Na+}$ and $\ce{OH-}$ ions stay behind.
@answer Hydrogen at the cathode, chlorine at the anode, and sodium hydroxide solution is left.
:::

:::example Copper(II) sulfate solution
What forms at each electrode when copper(II) sulfate solution is electrolysed with inert electrodes?
@step Cathode: copper is less reactive than hydrogen, so copper metal coats the electrode.
@step Anode: there are no halide ions, so oxygen forms.
@answer Copper at the cathode and oxygen at the anode.
:::

:::mistake Molten or dissolved?
Molten sodium chloride gives sodium at the cathode. Sodium chloride **solution** gives hydrogen. Before you answer, check whether the compound is molten or dissolved in water.
:::

## Half equations

:::definition Half equation
A **half equation** shows what happens at one electrode, with the electrons, $\ce{e-}$, written in. Electrons on the left are being gained (reduction). Electrons on the right are being lost (oxidation).
:::

At the cathode, positive ions gain electrons, so **reduction happens at the cathode**:

$\ce{Pb^2+ + 2e- -> Pb}$

At the anode, negative ions lose electrons, so **oxidation happens at the anode**:

$\ce{2Br- -> Br2 + 2e-}$

A half equation must balance twice: the same number of each kind of atom on both sides, and the same total charge on both sides.

:::example How many electrons?
Balance the half equation for aluminium ions at the cathode: $\ce{Al^3+ + n e- -> Al}$.
@step The right-hand side has no charge.
@step The left-hand side has a charge of 3+ from the ion, so 3 electrons (3−) are needed to bring the total to zero.
@answer $\ce{Al^3+ + 3e- -> Al}$, so $n = 3$.
:::

:::example Oxide ions at the anode
Write the half equation for oxide ions, $\ce{O^2-}$, forming oxygen gas.
@step Oxygen gas is $\ce{O2}$, so two oxide ions are needed: $\ce{2O^2- -> O2}$.
@step The left-hand side has a charge of 4−. The right-hand side is neutral, so 4 electrons are given out.
@answer $\ce{2O^2- -> O2 + 4e-}$
:::

In solutions, the two half equations for the ions from water are worth knowing:

- hydrogen at the cathode: $\ce{2H+ + 2e- -> H2}$
- oxygen at the anode: $\ce{4OH- -> O2 + 2H2O + 4e-}$

:::summary
- **Electrolysis** breaks down a molten or dissolved ionic compound with an electric current. The ions must be free to move.
- Positive ions go to the **cathode** (negative) and are reduced. Negative ions go to the **anode** (positive) and are oxidised.
- Molten compound: the **metal** forms at the cathode and the **non-metal** at the anode.
- Solution: **hydrogen** forms at the cathode unless the metal is less reactive than hydrogen; **oxygen** forms at the anode unless halide ions are present, when the halogen forms.
- A **half equation** shows the electrons: $\ce{Cu^2+ + 2e- -> Cu}$ (gain, reduction) and $\ce{2Cl- -> Cl2 + 2e-}$ (loss, oxidation). Atoms and charge must both balance.
:::
