Ethanol is made in two very different ways: by yeast fermenting sugar in a vat, and by reacting ethene with steam in a chemical plant. Once made, alcohols can be oxidised to aldehydes, ketones and acids, or have water removed to give alkenes, so they are one of the busiest junctions in organic chemistry.

In this lesson you will classify alcohols, compare the two ways of making ethanol, and predict what alcohols become when they are oxidised or dehydrated.

## Primary, secondary and tertiary alcohols

:::definition Primary, secondary and tertiary alcohols
An alcohol is **primary**, **secondary** or **tertiary** when the carbon carrying the –OH group is bonded to one, two or three other carbon atoms.
:::

Ethanol and propan-1-ol are primary, propan-2-ol and butan-2-ol are secondary, and 2-methylpropan-2-ol is tertiary.

## Making ethanol

| | Fermentation | Hydration of ethene |
|---|---|---|
| Equation | $\ce{C6H12O6 -> 2C2H5OH + 2CO2}$ | $\ce{C2H4 + H2O -> C2H5OH}$ |
| Conditions | yeast, about 35 °C, no air | steam, phosphoric acid catalyst, about 300 °C and 60–70 atm |
| Raw material | sugar from crops: **renewable** | ethene from crude oil: **finite** |
| Rate and process | slow, batch | fast, continuous |
| Product | dilute, needs distilling | almost pure |

Air is kept out of fermentation so the ethanol is not oxidised to ethanoic acid, and the temperature is kept moderate so the yeast enzymes are not denatured.

Ethanol from fermentation is sometimes called **carbon neutral**: the carbon dioxide released when it is made and burned was taken in by the crops as they grew. In practice, farming, transport and distillation use energy, so it is not completely carbon neutral.

:::example Yield from fermentation
What is the greatest mass of ethanol that 90 g of glucose can give? ($M_r$: glucose 180, ethanol 46)
@step Moles of glucose $= 90 \div 180 = 0.500$
@step Moles of ethanol $= 2 \times 0.500 = 1.00$
@step Mass $= 1.00 \times 46 = 46.0$ g
@answer 46.0 g
:::

## Oxidation with acidified dichromate(VI)

Warming an alcohol with **acidified potassium dichromate(VI)** oxidises it. The orange $\ce{Cr2O7^2-}$ is reduced to green $\ce{Cr^3+}$.

| Alcohol | Product | How |
|---|---|---|
| Primary | **aldehyde** | distil it off as it forms, so it is not oxidised further |
| Primary | **carboxylic acid** | heat under **reflux** with excess oxidising agent |
| Secondary | **ketone** | warm; ketones are not oxidised further |
| Tertiary | **no reaction** | the solution stays orange |

:::example Oxidising propan-1-ol
What forms when propan-1-ol is (a) warmed with acidified dichromate and distilled, and (b) heated under reflux with excess?
@step (a) The aldehyde is distilled off before it can react further: propanal, $\ce{CH3CH2CHO}$.
@step (b) Under reflux, propanal is oxidised further: propanoic acid, $\ce{CH3CH2COOH}$.
@answer (a) Propanal; (b) propanoic acid
:::

## Elimination: alcohols to alkenes

Heating an alcohol with a **concentrated acid catalyst**, such as phosphoric acid, removes a molecule of water. This **dehydration** is an elimination reaction: propan-2-ol gives propene. It means alkenes, and so polymers, can be made from ethanol from crops instead of from crude oil.

:::summary
- Alcohols are primary, secondary or tertiary by the number of carbons on the C–OH carbon.
- Fermentation: renewable but slow and impure. Hydration of ethene: fast and pure, but from finite crude oil.
- Acidified dichromate(VI), orange → green: primary → aldehyde (distil) → carboxylic acid (reflux); secondary → ketone; tertiary → no reaction.
- An acid catalyst dehydrates an alcohol to an alkene.
:::
