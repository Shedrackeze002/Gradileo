Water left in an open dish slowly disappears, even on a cool day, while water in a pot on a fire bubbles away in minutes. Both are liquid turning to vapour, but they happen in different ways. The idea of vapour pressure explains both, and it also explains why water boils at a lower temperature on a mountain top.

In this lesson you will use saturated vapour pressure to explain evaporation and boiling, describe the evidence for moving particles, and classify solids by the particles and forces in them.

## Saturated vapour pressure

:::definition Saturated vapour pressure
The **saturated vapour pressure** of a liquid is the pressure of its vapour when the vapour is in equilibrium with the liquid in a closed container. It rises as the temperature rises.
:::

**Evaporation** happens at the surface of a liquid at any temperature, as the fastest molecules escape. **Boiling** happens throughout the liquid at one temperature: a liquid boils when its saturated vapour pressure equals the external pressure. At sea level that pressure is about 101 kPa, which water reaches at 100 °C.

| Temperature (°C) | 60 | 70 | 80 | 90 | 100 | 110 | 120 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Vapour pressure of water (kPa) | 19.9 | 31.2 | 47.4 | 70.1 | 101.3 | 143.3 | 198.5 |

:::example Boiling at lower pressure
Using the table, at what temperature does water boil when the pressure above it is 70.1 kPa?
@step Water boils when its vapour pressure equals the external pressure.
@step The table gives 70.1 kPa at 90 °C.
@answer 90 °C
:::

On a high mountain the air pressure is lower, so water boils below 100 °C and food cooks more slowly. A pressure cooker does the opposite: the higher pressure raises the boiling point. A volatile liquid such as ether has a high vapour pressure at room temperature, because the forces between its molecules are weak.

## Evidence that particles move

The **kinetic theory** says that the particles of all matter are always moving, faster when hotter. Under a microscope, pollen grains in water and smoke specks in air jiggle about in short, random jerks. This **Brownian motion** happens because the invisible molecules around them strike them unevenly from different sides. Diffusion is further evidence: ink spreads through water, and spreads faster in hot water.

As a substance changes from gas to liquid to solid, its particles become more ordered. Some solids, such as iodine, turn straight into a vapour when warmed, which is **sublimation**.

## Kinds of solid

| Kind of solid | Particles and forces | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Ionic | ions held by strong electrostatic attraction | sodium chloride, magnesium oxide |
| Metallic | positive ions in a sea of delocalised electrons | copper, iron |
| Covalent network | atoms joined in one giant covalent network | diamond, graphite, sand |
| Molecular | small molecules held by weak intermolecular forces | iodine, ice, sugar |

:::summary
- Saturated vapour pressure rises with temperature; a liquid boils when it equals the external pressure.
- Evaporation is at the surface at any temperature; boiling is throughout the liquid at one temperature.
- Lower pressure lowers the boiling point; higher pressure raises it.
- Brownian motion and diffusion are evidence that particles are always moving.
- Solids are ionic, metallic, covalent network or molecular.
:::
