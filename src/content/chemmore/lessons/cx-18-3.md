Every country needs sulfuric acid, fertilisers, soap, cement and medicines, and the chemical industry makes them. Some of its products are made by the million tonnes, others by the kilogram. Long before modern factories, people in West Africa were already using living things to process food, in bread, gari and palm wine.

In this lesson you will tell heavy chemicals from fine chemicals, give the factors that decide where a chemical plant is built and its effects, and describe fermentation in food processing.

## Heavy and fine chemicals

:::definition Heavy chemical
A **heavy chemical** is made in very large amounts at a low cost per tonne, such as sulfuric acid, ammonia, sodium hydroxide and cement. A **fine chemical** is made in small amounts at high purity and high value, such as a medicine, a dye or a perfume.
:::

## Siting a chemical plant

A plant is built where these come together at the lowest cost:

- **raw materials**, especially bulky ones such as limestone for cement;
- **energy**, such as electricity for aluminium smelting;
- **water** for cooling and for the reactions;
- **transport** by road, rail or sea;
- **labour** and a **market** for the products;
- **safety and the environment**, away from crowded homes, with ways to treat waste.

Plants that use bulky raw materials are built near them; plants whose products are bulky or fragile are built near their markets. A **by-product**, such as slag or chlorine from brine, can be sold to cut costs, and unreacted materials are often recycled, as in the Haber process.

A chemical industry brings jobs, products and less spending on imports, but untreated waste can poison rivers and air, so it must be controlled.

## Fermentation and biotechnology

**Biotechnology** is the use of living things such as yeast and bacteria to make useful products. **Fermentation** is the breakdown of sugars by their enzymes without air:

$$\ce{C6H12O6 -> 2C2H5OH + 2CO2}$$

- **Bread**: yeast ferments sugar in the dough, and the carbon dioxide makes it rise; baking kills the yeast and drives off the ethanol.
- **Palm wine and beer**: yeasts turn sugars into ethanol and carbon dioxide; left open to air, microbes turn the ethanol into ethanoic acid, so the wine turns sour.
- **Gari**: grated cassava is fermented, which sours it and helps release its poisonous cyanide compounds, before it is pressed and roasted.

Fermentation works best at about 30 to 40 °C; hotter, the enzymes are destroyed.

:::example Ethanol from glucose
What mass of ethanol (M = 46) can be made from 90 g of glucose (M = 180)?
@step Glucose = 90 ÷ 180 = 0.50 mol, which gives 2 × 0.50 = 1.0 mol of ethanol.
@answer 1.0 × 46 = 46.0 g
:::

:::summary
- Heavy chemicals are made in huge amounts cheaply; fine chemicals in small amounts at high purity.
- A plant is sited by raw materials, energy, water, transport, labour, market and safety.
- Fermentation: C₆H₁₂O₆ → 2C₂H₅OH + 2CO₂, by yeast enzymes, without air, at about 30 to 40 °C.
- It makes bread rise, gives palm wine and helps make cassava safe as gari.
