The simple picture of electrons in shells of 2, 8 and 8 works well for the first twenty elements, but it cannot explain why iron forms Fe²⁺ and Fe³⁺, or why chromium and copper behave oddly. To go further, chemists describe each electron by the orbital it occupies and fill those orbitals by three rules.

In this lesson you will write electron configurations using s, p and d orbitals, apply the rules for filling them, and use the quantum numbers that label each shell and sub-level.

## Shells, sub-levels and orbitals

:::definition Orbital
An **orbital** is a region around the nucleus where there is a high chance of finding an electron. Each orbital holds at most two electrons.
:::

Each shell has a **principal quantum number** $n$ = 1, 2, 3 and so on. A shell is split into **sub-levels**, labelled by the **azimuthal (subsidiary) quantum number** $l$: $l = 0$ is s, $l = 1$ is p, $l = 2$ is d. The shell with number $n$ has $n$ sub-levels and can hold $2n^2$ electrons.

| Sub-level | $l$ | Orbitals | Electrons it holds | Shape |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| s | 0 | 1 | 2 | sphere |
| p | 1 | 3 | 6 | dumb-bell (two lobes) |
| d | 2 | 5 | 10 | mostly four lobes |

## The rules for filling orbitals

- **Aufbau principle:** electrons go into the lowest-energy orbitals first, in the order 1s, 2s, 2p, 3s, 3p, 4s, 3d.
- **Pauli exclusion principle:** an orbital holds no more than two electrons, and they spin in opposite directions.
- **Hund's rule:** in a sub-level, electrons occupy separate orbitals singly, with parallel spins, before any pair up.

So nitrogen's three 2p electrons sit one in each p orbital, [↑][↑][↑], and oxygen's fourth 2p electron pairs up, [↑↓][↑][↑].

## Writing electron configurations

:::example The configuration of iron
Write the electron configuration of iron (atomic number 26), and of the Fe³⁺ ion.
@step Fill in order: 1s² 2s² 2p⁶ 3s² 3p⁶ uses 18 electrons; 4s² takes 2 more; the remaining 6 go into 3d.
@step Fe: 1s² 2s² 2p⁶ 3s² 3p⁶ 3d⁶ 4s².
@step Ions lose their 4s electrons first, so Fe³⁺ loses both 4s electrons and one 3d electron.
@answer Fe is 1s² 2s² 2p⁶ 3s² 3p⁶ 3d⁶ 4s²; Fe³⁺ is 1s² 2s² 2p⁶ 3s² 3p⁶ 3d⁵.
:::

Two elements break the simple order: chromium is 3d⁵ 4s¹ and copper is 3d¹⁰ 4s¹, because a half-full or full d sub-level is especially stable. Count unpaired electrons by drawing the last sub-level as boxes and using Hund's rule.

:::mistake The 4s electrons leave first
4s fills before 3d, but when a transition metal forms an ion, the 4s electrons are lost first.

:::summary
- Shell $n$ has $n$ sub-levels and holds $2n^2$ electrons; s, p and d sub-levels have 1, 3 and 5 orbitals.
- Aufbau: lowest energy first. Pauli: two electrons per orbital, opposite spins. Hund: singly, with parallel spins, before pairing.
- Chromium is 3d⁵ 4s¹ and copper is 3d¹⁰ 4s¹.
- Transition metal ions lose their 4s electrons before their 3d electrons.
:::
