A phone battery, a torch battery and the fuel cell in a hydrogen bus all make electricity from chemical reactions. The torch battery is thrown away when it goes flat, the phone battery is plugged in to charge, and the bus is simply refilled with hydrogen.

In this lesson you will describe the reaction in a hydrogen fuel cell and write its half equations, compare cells, batteries and fuel cells, and work out the voltage of a battery.

## Cells and batteries

:::definition Cell
A **cell** contains chemicals that react to produce electricity. Two different metals (or other electrodes) in an electrolyte give a voltage. A **battery** is two or more cells connected in series, so that their voltages add up.
:::

The voltage of a cell depends on the electrodes and the electrolyte: the further apart the two metals are in the reactivity series, the larger the voltage.

:::example The voltage of a battery
A battery is made of 4 identical cells in series. Each cell gives 1.5 V. What is the voltage of the battery?
@step Cells in series add their voltages.
@step $4 \times 1.5 = 6$
@answer 6 V
:::

- In a **non-rechargeable** cell, such as an alkaline battery, the reaction stops when one of the reactants is used up.
- In a **rechargeable** cell, such as a lithium-ion battery, the reaction can be reversed by an external electric current.

## Hydrogen fuel cells

:::definition Fuel cell
A **fuel cell** is supplied continuously with a fuel and oxygen (or air), and the fuel is oxidised electrochemically to produce a voltage. It keeps working as long as the fuel is supplied.
:::

In a **hydrogen fuel cell**, hydrogen and oxygen react to make water. Water is the only chemical product.

$$\ce{2H2 + O2 -> 2H2O}$$

## The half equations

With an acidic electrolyte, the reaction happens in two halves.

| Electrode | What happens | Half equation |
|---|---|---|
| Negative electrode | Hydrogen loses electrons: **oxidation** | $\ce{2H2 -> 4H+ + 4e-}$ |
| Positive electrode | Oxygen gains electrons: **reduction** | $\ce{O2 + 4H+ + 4e- -> 2H2O}$ |

The electrons flow through the external circuit from the negative to the positive electrode, and the hydrogen ions move through the electrolyte. Adding the two half equations, the $\ce{4H+}$ and $\ce{4e-}$ cancel, leaving $\ce{2H2 + O2 -> 2H2O}$.

:::mistake Oxidation is loss
Hydrogen is the fuel, so it is the one that is **oxidised**: it loses electrons. Oxygen gains them and is **reduced**. Remember OIL RIG: oxidation is loss, reduction is gain.
:::

## Comparing fuel cells with rechargeable batteries

| | Hydrogen fuel cell | Rechargeable battery |
|---|---|---|
| Products | Only water | No products in use, but toxic metals must be disposed of at the end of its life |
| Topping up | Refilled with hydrogen in minutes | Recharging takes much longer |
| Lifetime | Long, with few parts to wear out | Wears out after many charges |
| Drawbacks | Hydrogen is flammable and hard to store, and is often made from fossil fuels | Heavy, and the energy to charge it may come from fossil fuels |

:::check cx-cells-batteries 3
:::

:::summary
- A **cell** produces a voltage from a chemical reaction. A **battery** is cells in series, whose voltages add.
- Non-rechargeable cells stop when a reactant is used up; rechargeable cells are reversed by an external current.
- A **hydrogen fuel cell** makes water: $\ce{2H2 + O2 -> 2H2O}$, and works while hydrogen is supplied.
- At the negative electrode $\ce{2H2 -> 4H+ + 4e-}$ (oxidation); at the positive electrode $\ce{O2 + 4H+ + 4e- -> 2H2O}$ (reduction).
- Fuel cells refuel fast and produce only water, but hydrogen is hard to store and is often made from fossil fuels.
:::
