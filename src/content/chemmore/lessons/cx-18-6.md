The Brønsted–Lowry idea of acids as proton donors explains most acids you meet, but some reactions look acid–base-like with no proton in sight. Boron trifluoride grabs ammonia; a copper ion collects four ammonia molecules into a deep blue complex. Gilbert Lewis widened the definition to cover them, by following the electron pairs.

In this lesson you will identify Lewis acids and bases, and name complex ions and coordination compounds, with their coordination numbers, shapes and oxidation states.

## Lewis acids and bases

:::definition Lewis acid
A **Lewis acid** is an electron-pair acceptor. A **Lewis base** is an electron-pair donor. When they react, they form a dative (coordinate) covalent bond, in which both electrons come from the base.
:::

$$\ce{BF3 + NH3 -> F3B-NH3}$$

Here ammonia donates its lone pair to boron, which has only six outer electrons in $\ce{BF3}$, so $\ce{BF3}$ is the Lewis acid and $\ce{NH3}$ the Lewis base. A Lewis acid need not contain hydrogen: $\ce{H+}$, $\ce{BF3}$, $\ce{AlCl3}$ and metal ions such as $\ce{Cu^2+}$ all accept pairs. A Lewis base must have a lone pair, so $\ce{NH3}$, $\ce{H2O}$, $\ce{OH-}$ and $\ce{Cl-}$ can act as bases, but methane cannot. Every Brønsted–Lowry base is also a Lewis base, since it uses a lone pair to accept a proton.

## Complex ions

A **complex ion** has a central metal ion bonded to **ligands** by dative bonds. The ligands are Lewis bases and the metal ion is the Lewis acid. The **coordination number** is the number of dative bonds to the central ion.

| Coordination number | Shape | Example |
|---|---|---|
| 2 | linear | $\ce{[Ag(NH3)2]+}$ |
| 4 | tetrahedral | $\ce{[CoCl4]^2-}$, $\ce{[Zn(NH3)4]^2+}$ |
| 4 | square planar | $\ce{[Cu(NH3)4]^2+}$, $\ce{[Ni(CN)4]^2-}$ |
| 6 | octahedral | $\ce{[Cu(H2O)6]^2+}$, $\ce{[Fe(CN)6]^3-}$ |

## Naming complex ions

1. Name the ligands with a prefix for their number: di (2), tetra (4), hexa (6). Neutral ligands: ammine ($\ce{NH3}$), aqua ($\ce{H2O}$). Negative ligands: chloro ($\ce{Cl-}$), cyano ($\ce{CN-}$), hydroxo ($\ce{OH-}$).
2. Name the metal, with its oxidation state in Roman numerals. If the complex ion is negative, the metal name ends in **-ate**, often from its Latin name: ferrate (iron), cuprate (copper), argentate (silver).

To find the oxidation state: oxidation state of the metal + total charge of the ligands = charge of the complex ion.

:::example Naming a cyanide complex
Name $\ce{[Fe(CN)6]^3-}$ and find the oxidation state of iron.
@step Six cyano ligands, each −1: total −6. Iron + (−6) = −3, so iron is +3.
@step The complex ion is negative, so iron becomes ferrate.
@answer Hexacyanoferrate(III) ion
:::

:::summary
- Lewis acids accept electron pairs; Lewis bases donate them, forming dative bonds.
- In a complex ion, the ligands are Lewis bases and the metal ion is the Lewis acid.
- Coordination number 2 is linear, 4 tetrahedral or square planar, 6 octahedral.
- Name ligands (with di, tetra, hexa), then the metal with its oxidation state; a negative complex ends in -ate.
:::
