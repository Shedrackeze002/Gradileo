Every trend in the periodic table, from the size of atoms to how easily they lose electrons, comes from one law of attraction between charges. A photoelectron spectrum lets you see that attraction at work, one subshell at a time.

In this lesson you will use Coulomb's law to explain ionisation energies, and read photoelectron spectra to find an atom's electron configuration.

## Coulomb's law

:::definition Coulomb's law
The force between two charges $q_1$ and $q_2$ a distance $r$ apart is proportional to the product of the charges and inversely proportional to the square of the distance:
$$F \propto \frac{q_1 q_2}{r^2}$$
Opposite charges attract and like charges repel.
:::

So doubling one charge doubles the force, while doubling the distance cuts it to a quarter. In an atom, the positive nucleus attracts each negative electron, so an electron is held more strongly when the nucleus has more protons (a larger effective charge) and when the electron is closer to it.

## Ionisation energy and the periodic table

The **first ionisation energy** is the energy needed to remove the outermost electron from each atom in a mole of gaseous atoms. Coulomb's law explains both main trends.

- **Down a group** the outer electron is in a shell farther from the nucleus, and the inner shells shield it, so it is held less strongly and the ionisation energy falls.
- **Across a period** the nuclear charge rises while the outer electrons stay in the same shell, so they are held more strongly and the ionisation energy rises.

## Photoelectron spectroscopy

In **photoelectron spectroscopy** (PES), high-energy photons knock electrons out of atoms, and the instrument measures how much energy each electron was bound with. Electrons in the same subshell have the same binding energy, so each occupied subshell gives one peak.

:::definition Reading a photoelectron spectrum
- Each **peak** is one occupied subshell.
- A peak's **height** is proportional to the number of electrons in that subshell.
- A peak's **position** gives the binding energy. Peaks farther to the left (higher binding energy) belong to electrons closer to the nucleus.
:::

Here we see the spectrum of sodium. The 1s peak is farthest to the left because those two electrons are closest to the nucleus and feel almost all of its eleven protons. The small 3s peak on the right is sodium's single outer electron, which is why sodium loses it so easily.

:::example Identifying an element from its spectrum
A spectrum has peaks holding 2, 2, 6, 2 and 3 electrons. Which element is it?
@step The peaks are 1s, 2s, 2p, 3s and 3p: $\mathrm{1s^2\,2s^2\,2p^6\,3s^2\,3p^3}$.
@step Total electrons: $2 + 2 + 6 + 2 + 3 = 15$.
@answer A neutral atom with 15 electrons is phosphorus.

:::summary
- Coulomb's law: $F \propto \dfrac{q_1 q_2}{r^2}$, so the attraction grows with the charges and falls quickly with distance.
- Ionisation energy falls down a group (outer electrons farther out) and rises across a period (more nuclear charge).
- In a photoelectron spectrum, each peak is a subshell, its height counts the electrons, and peaks farther left are bound more strongly.
:::
