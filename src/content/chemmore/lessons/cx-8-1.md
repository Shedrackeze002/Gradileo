Walk along Period 3 from sodium to argon and the elements change completely: a soft metal that melts in hot water, a hard grey solid that melts at over 1400 °C, a yellow powder, a green gas. These changes repeat in every period, which is why the pattern is called **periodicity**.

In this lesson you will explain the trends in atomic radius, ionisation energy and melting point across Period 3.

## Atomic radius

:::definition Periodicity
The repeating pattern in the properties of the elements across each period of the periodic table.
:::

Across Period 3, the atoms get **smaller**. Each element has one more proton than the last, but its outer electrons are still in the third shell, shielded by the same inner electrons. The larger nuclear charge pulls them closer.

| Element | Na | Mg | Al | Si | P | S | Cl |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Atomic radius / nm | 0.191 | 0.160 | 0.130 | 0.118 | 0.110 | 0.102 | 0.099 |

Argon is left out: it forms no bonds, so its radius is measured in a different way and cannot be compared.

## First ionisation energy

For the same reason, the first ionisation energy generally **rises** across the period. There are two small dips:

- **Aluminium** is lower than magnesium, because its outer electron is in a 3p sub-shell, higher in energy than 3s.
- **Sulfur** is lower than phosphorus, because two of its 3p electrons share an orbital and repel each other.

## Structure and melting point

The melting points follow the **structure** of each element, not its position alone.

| Element | Structure | Melting point / °C |
|---|---|---|
| Na, Mg, Al | metallic | 98, 650, 660 |
| Si | giant covalent (macromolecular) | 1414 |
| P ($\ce{P4}$), S ($\ce{S8}$), Cl ($\ce{Cl2}$) | simple molecular | 44, 115, −101 |
| Ar | monatomic | −189 |

- **Sodium to aluminium:** the metal ions get smaller and more highly charged (1+, 2+, 3+), with more delocalised electrons each, so the metallic bonding gets stronger.
- **Silicon:** each atom is covalently bonded to four others in a giant lattice. Melting it breaks many strong covalent bonds, so it has the highest melting point.
- **Phosphorus, sulfur and chlorine:** only weak van der Waals forces between molecules are overcome. $\ce{S8}$ molecules are the largest, with the most electrons, so sulfur melts highest of the three; $\ce{Cl2}$ is the smallest.
- **Argon:** single atoms with very weak forces between them.

:::

:::mistake Melting a simple molecular solid does not break covalent bonds
When sulfur melts, the S–S bonds inside each $\ce{S8}$ ring stay intact. Only the weak forces **between** the molecules are overcome. That is why sulfur melts at only 115 °C.
:::

:::

:::example Ordering melting points
Put aluminium, phosphorus and silicon in order of melting point, highest first.
@step Silicon is giant covalent: many strong covalent bonds to break.
@step Aluminium is metallic, with strong bonding between 3+ ions and delocalised electrons.
@step Phosphorus is simple molecular: only weak van der Waals forces between $\ce{P4}$ molecules.
@answer Silicon, aluminium, phosphorus
:::

:::

:::summary
- Across Period 3 the nuclear charge rises while the shielding stays the same, so the atomic radius **falls** and the first ionisation energy generally **rises**.
- The ionisation energy dips at aluminium (3p electron) and sulfur (paired 3p electrons).
- Na, Mg and Al are metallic, with melting points rising as the bonding strengthens.
- Silicon is giant covalent and has the highest melting point.
- P, S and Cl are simple molecular, with low melting points set by van der Waals forces; argon is monatomic.
:::
