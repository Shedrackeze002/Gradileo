The Group 2 metals and their compounds turn up in surprising places: in the indigestion tablet you chew, the lime a farmer spreads on a field, the white drink a patient swallows before an X-ray, and the fireworks that burn red and green.

In this lesson you will explain the trends down Group 2, write equations for the metals reacting with water, and link the properties of Group 2 compounds to their uses.

## Trends down the group

The Group 2 metals, magnesium to barium, each have **two outer electrons**, which they lose to form $\ce{M^2+}$ ions. In all their compounds they have an oxidation state of **+2**.

Going down the group, each atom has one more shell of electrons. So:

| Property | Trend down the group | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Atomic radius | increases | an extra shell each time |
| First ionisation energy | decreases | the outer electrons are further away and more shielded |
| Reactivity | increases | the outer electrons are lost more easily |

## Reactions with water

The metals react with water to give a hydroxide and hydrogen, more and more vigorously down the group:

$$\ce{M + 2H2O -> M(OH)2 + H2}$$

**Magnesium** reacts only very slowly with cold water. With **steam** it burns with a bright white light, giving magnesium **oxide**:

$$\ce{Mg + H2O -> MgO + H2}$$

In every case the metal is **oxidised**, from 0 to +2, and hydrogen is reduced, from +1 to 0.

Magnesium is also used to extract titanium, because it is more reactive and displaces titanium from its chloride: $\ce{TiCl4 + 2Mg -> Ti + 2MgCl2}$.

What mass of hydrogen forms when 2.0 g of calcium reacts completely with water? ($A_r$: Ca = 40, H = 1)
@step Moles of Ca $= 2.0 \div 40 = 0.0500$ mol
@step One mole of Ca gives one mole of $\ce{H2}$, so 0.0500 mol of $\ce{H2}$.

## Solubility of hydroxides and sulfates

The two families of compounds go in **opposite** directions down the group.

| Compound | Trend down the group | Extremes |
|---|---|---|
| Hydroxides, $\ce{M(OH)2}$ | **more** soluble | $\ce{Mg(OH)2}$ is sparingly soluble; $\ce{Ba(OH)2}$ is the most soluble |
| Sulfates, $\ce{MSO4}$ | **less** soluble | $\ce{MgSO4}$ is soluble; $\ce{BaSO4}$ is insoluble |

## Uses of Group 2 compounds

- **Magnesium hydroxide** is an antacid (milk of magnesia): a weak base that neutralises excess stomach acid.
- **Calcium hydroxide** (slaked lime) neutralises acidic soil.
- **Calcium oxide** and **calcium carbonate** remove sulfur dioxide from the waste gases of power stations.
- **Barium sulfate** is the “barium meal” for X-rays of the gut. Barium ions are toxic, but barium sulfate is **insoluble**, so it is not absorbed into the blood, and it blocks X-rays so the gut shows up.

### The test for sulfate ions

Add **acidified barium chloride** solution. A **white precipitate** of barium sulfate shows sulfate ions:

$$\ce{Ba^2+(aq) + SO4^2-(aq) -> BaSO4(s)}$$

The hydrochloric acid is added first to remove carbonate ions, which would also give a white precipitate.

:::mistake Barium compounds are not all safe
Only barium **sulfate** can be swallowed, because it is insoluble. A soluble barium compound, such as barium chloride, is poisonous.
:::

:::summary
- Down Group 2, atomic radius increases, first ionisation energy decreases, and reactivity increases.
- $\ce{M + 2H2O -> M(OH)2 + H2}$; magnesium with steam gives $\ce{MgO + H2}$. The metal is oxidised from 0 to +2.
- Hydroxides become **more** soluble down the group; sulfates become **less** soluble.
- Uses: $\ce{Mg(OH)2}$ antacid, $\ce{Ca(OH)2}$ for acid soil, $\ce{CaO}$/$\ce{CaCO3}$ to remove $\ce{SO2}$, $\ce{BaSO4}$ barium meal, Mg to extract Ti.
- Acidified barium chloride gives a white precipitate with sulfate ions.
:::
