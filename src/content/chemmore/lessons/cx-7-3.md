In an ordinary covalent bond, each atom puts one electron into the shared pair. But a hydrogen ion has no electrons at all, and yet it bonds to ammonia to make the ammonium ion. The answer is a bond in which one atom supplies **both** electrons.

In this lesson you will explain how dative covalent bonds form, and work out the shapes of the species that contain them.

## What a dative bond is

:::definition Dative covalent bond
A shared pair of electrons in which **both** electrons come from the same atom. It is also called a **coordinate bond**.
:::

For a dative bond to form:

- the **donor** atom needs a **lone pair** of electrons in its outer shell;
- the **acceptor** atom needs an **empty orbital** in its outer shell.

In a displayed formula, a dative bond is drawn as an **arrow** from the donor to the acceptor.

:::example The ammonium ion
How does ammonia, $\ce{NH3}$, form a bond with a hydrogen ion, $\ce{H+}$?
@step Nitrogen in $\ce{NH3}$ has a lone pair. $\ce{H+}$ has no electrons and an empty orbital.
@step Nitrogen donates its lone pair to the hydrogen ion.
@answer $\ce{NH3 + H+ -> NH4+}$, with one dative bond, N → H
:::

Once it has formed, a dative bond is **the same** as any other covalent bond. All four N–H bonds in $\ce{NH4+}$ are identical, and the positive charge belongs to the whole ion. In the same way, water accepts a hydrogen ion to form the oxonium ion, $\ce{H3O+}$.

## Electron-deficient molecules

Some molecules have a central atom with fewer than eight outer electrons, so they can accept a lone pair.

- **Boron trifluoride**, $\ce{BF3}$: boron has only six outer electrons. It accepts the lone pair of ammonia to form $\ce{H3NBF3}$, with one dative bond from N to B.
- **Aluminium chloride** forms molecules of $\ce{Al2Cl6}$. Each aluminium has only six outer electrons, so a chlorine on each $\ce{AlCl3}$ unit donates a lone pair to the other aluminium: **two** dative bonds.
- **Carbon monoxide**, $\ce{CO}$: of its triple bond, two are ordinary covalent bonds and the third is a dative bond from oxygen to carbon.

:::mistake The arrow points to the acceptor
The arrow starts at the atom with the lone pair and points to the atom with the empty orbital. In $\ce{NH4+}$ it points from N to H, never from H to N.
:::

## Shapes of species with dative bonds

The shape round a central atom depends on the number of electron pairs around it, bonding pairs and lone pairs alike. A dative bond counts as a bonding pair. Pairs repel each other to be as far apart as possible, and lone pairs repel more strongly than bonding pairs, closing the bond angles by about $2.5^\circ$ each.

| Species | Pairs round the central atom | Shape | Bond angle |
|---|---|---|---|
| $\ce{NH3}$ | 3 bonding, 1 lone | trigonal pyramidal | $107^\circ$ |
| $\ce{NH4+}$ | 4 bonding | tetrahedral | $109.5^\circ$ |
| $\ce{H3O+}$ | 3 bonding, 1 lone | trigonal pyramidal | $107^\circ$ |
| $\ce{BF3}$ | 3 bonding | trigonal planar | $120^\circ$ |
| $\ce{H3NBF3}$ (round B) | 4 bonding | tetrahedral | $109.5^\circ$ |

:::example From ammonia to ammonium
How does the bond angle change when ammonia becomes the ammonium ion?
@step Ammonia has three bonding pairs and one lone pair: $107^\circ$.
@step The lone pair becomes a bonding pair, so there are four bonding pairs.
@answer The angle opens to $109.5^\circ$, a tetrahedral shape.
:::

:::summary
- In a **dative covalent bond**, both shared electrons come from one atom, shown by an arrow from donor to acceptor.
- The donor needs a lone pair; the acceptor needs an empty orbital.
- Examples: $\ce{NH4+}$ and $\ce{H3O+}$ (one each), $\ce{Al2Cl6}$ (two), $\ce{H3NBF3}$ and $\ce{CO}$ (one each).
- Once formed, a dative bond is identical to any other covalent bond.
- A dative bond counts as a bonding pair when working out shapes: $\ce{NH4+}$ is tetrahedral, $109.5^\circ$.
:::
