A white powder is found in an unlabelled jar. It could be table salt, washing soda, or something far less friendly, and looking at it tells you nothing. A chemist works out what it is with a handful of quick tests, each of which picks out one kind of ion by a colour.

In this lesson you will name the metal ion in a compound from a flame colour or a precipitate, name the negative ion from three test-tube tests, and put the two together to identify the compound.

## Flame tests

:::definition Flame test
A **flame test** identifies some metal ions by the colour they give to a flame. A clean wire loop is dipped into the compound and held in a blue Bunsen flame.
:::

| Metal ion | Flame colour |
|---|---|
| Lithium, $\ce{Li+}$ | Crimson |
| Sodium, $\ce{Na+}$ | Yellow |
| Potassium, $\ce{K+}$ | Lilac |
| Calcium, $\ce{Ca^2+}$ | Orange-red |
| Copper, $\ce{Cu^2+}$ | Green |

The colour comes from the metal ion, so sodium chloride, sodium sulfate and sodium carbonate all give the same yellow flame. If a sample holds two of these ions, the colours mix and one can hide the other: the strong yellow of sodium easily masks the pale lilac of potassium.

## Precipitates with sodium hydroxide

:::definition Precipitate
A **precipitate** is a solid that forms when two solutions are mixed, because one of the products does not dissolve in water. It makes the liquid cloudy.
:::

Most metal hydroxides do not dissolve. So when sodium hydroxide solution is added to a solution of a metal compound, the metal hydroxide appears as a precipitate, and its colour identifies the metal ion.

| Metal ion | Precipitate with sodium hydroxide solution |
|---|---|
| Copper(II), $\ce{Cu^2+}$ | Blue |
| Iron(II), $\ce{Fe^2+}$ | Green |
| Iron(III), $\ce{Fe^3+}$ | Brown |
| Calcium, $\ce{Ca^2+}$ | White |
| Magnesium, $\ce{Mg^2+}$ | White |
| Aluminium, $\ce{Al^3+}$ | White, and it **dissolves** when more sodium hydroxide is added |

Three ions give a white precipitate. Adding more sodium hydroxide picks out aluminium, because only its precipitate dissolves. A flame test then separates the other two: calcium gives an orange-red flame, and magnesium gives the flame no colour.

The ionic equation shows the metal ion meeting the hydroxide ions. The hydroxide ion is $\ce{OH-}$, so the number of hydroxide ions needed equals the charge on the metal ion:

$\ce{Cu^2+ + 2OH- -> Cu(OH)2}$

:::example An equation for a precipitate
Write the ionic equation for the brown precipitate that forms when sodium hydroxide solution is added to a solution containing $\ce{Fe^3+}$ ions.
@step The precipitate is iron(III) hydroxide. It has no overall charge.
@step One $\ce{Fe^3+}$ ion needs three $\ce{OH-}$ ions, so the formula is $\ce{Fe(OH)3}$.
@answer $\ce{Fe^3+ + 3OH- -> Fe(OH)3}$
:::

## Tests for negative ions

Three tests cover the common negative ions.

| Ion | What is added | Positive result |
|---|---|---|
| Carbonate, $\ce{CO3^2-}$ | Dilute acid | Fizzing. The gas is carbon dioxide, which turns limewater milky |
| Chloride, $\ce{Cl-}$ | Dilute nitric acid, then silver nitrate solution | **White** precipitate |
| Bromide, $\ce{Br-}$ | Dilute nitric acid, then silver nitrate solution | **Cream** precipitate |
| Iodide, $\ce{I-}$ | Dilute nitric acid, then silver nitrate solution | **Yellow** precipitate |
| Sulfate, $\ce{SO4^2-}$ | Dilute hydrochloric acid, then barium chloride solution | **White** precipitate |

The halide precipitates are the silver halides: for example $\ce{Ag+ + Cl- -> AgCl}$. The sulfate precipitate is barium sulfate: $\ce{Ba^2+ + SO4^2- -> BaSO4}$.

:::mistake Why the acid goes in first
Carbonate ions also give a white precipitate with silver nitrate and with barium chloride, which would look like a positive result. The acid added first reacts with any carbonate ions and removes them. The acid must not contain the ion being tested for, so nitric acid is used before silver nitrate: hydrochloric acid would add chloride ions of its own.
:::

## Identifying a compound

An ionic compound has a positive ion and a negative ion, so it takes two tests to name it: one for each ion.

:::example Naming an unknown compound
A white solid gives a yellow flame. Its solution, with dilute hydrochloric acid and then barium chloride solution, gives a white precipitate. What is the solid?
@step A yellow flame shows sodium ions.
@step A white precipitate with barium chloride shows sulfate ions.
@answer Sodium sulfate.
:::

:::summary
- **Flame tests:** lithium crimson, sodium yellow, potassium lilac, calcium orange-red, copper green.
- **Sodium hydroxide solution** gives coloured precipitates: copper(II) blue, iron(II) green, iron(III) brown. Calcium, magnesium and aluminium give white, and only the aluminium precipitate dissolves in an excess.
- **Carbonates** fizz with dilute acid, giving carbon dioxide.
- **Halides,** with dilute nitric acid and silver nitrate solution: chloride white, bromide cream, iodide yellow.
- **Sulfates,** with dilute hydrochloric acid and barium chloride solution, give a white precipitate.
- To identify a compound, test for the positive ion and for the negative ion.
:::
