Twenty amino acids, joined in different orders, make every protein in your body. Four bases, paired up and read in order, store the instructions for making them. Both depend on the same organic chemistry you have already met: amines, acids and hydrogen bonds.

In this lesson you will describe amino acids and proteins, explain the structure of DNA, and explain how the anticancer drug cisplatin works.

## Amino acids

An α-amino acid has an $\ce{NH2}$ group and a COOH group on the same carbon, $\ce{H2NCH(R)COOH}$. All except glycine have a chiral centre.

Because it is both an acid and a base, an amino acid can exist in different forms:

| Conditions | Form |
|---|---|
| acid (low pH) | $\ce{H3N+CH(R)COOH}$, positive |
| at the isoelectric point | $\ce{H3N+CH(R)COO-}$, a **zwitterion** |
| alkali (high pH) | $\ce{H2NCH(R)COO-}$, negative |

:::definition Zwitterion
An ion with both a positive and a negative charge, but no overall charge.
:::

## Proteins

Amino acids join by **condensation** reactions, forming **peptide links**, –CONH–, and losing water. Heating a protein with 6 mol dm⁻³ hydrochloric acid hydrolyses the peptide links back to amino acids, which can be identified by chromatography.

- **Primary structure**: the sequence of amino acids.
- **Secondary structure**: an α-helix or β-pleated sheet, held by hydrogen bonds between the C=O and N–H of peptide links.
- **Tertiary structure**: the overall folded shape, held by hydrogen bonds, ionic bonds and **disulfide bridges** between side chains.

**Enzymes** are proteins whose active site has a precise shape. It is **stereospecific**, so it often works on only one enantiomer of a substrate. Drugs can be designed as inhibitors that block the active site.

## DNA

Each **nucleotide** has a phosphate group, the sugar **2-deoxyribose**, and one of four bases: adenine (A), thymine (T), cytosine (C) or guanine (G). Phosphate and sugar groups alternate to form the **backbone** of each strand.

Two strands twist into a **double helix**, held together by hydrogen bonds between bases:

- **A pairs with T**, with **two** hydrogen bonds;
- **C pairs with G**, with **three** hydrogen bonds.

:::example Counting hydrogen bonds
One strand reads ATGC. How many hydrogen bonds hold it to its partner strand?
@step The partner is TACG: pairs A–T, T–A, G–C, C–G.
@step A–T pairs: $2 \times 2 = 4$. C–G pairs: $2 \times 3 = 6$.
@answer 10 hydrogen bonds
:::

## Cisplatin

Cisplatin, $\ce{Pt(NH3)2Cl2}$, is a square planar complex with the two chloride ligands next to each other. In a cell, the chloride ligands are replaced by nitrogen atoms on two **guanine** bases of DNA. This bends the DNA and stops it being copied, so cancer cells cannot divide. It also harms healthy cells (causing side effects such as hair loss), so doses must be kept low.

:::mistake Two hydrogen bonds or three?
A–T has two hydrogen bonds and C–G has three. So DNA rich in C and G is held together more strongly.
:::

:::summary
- Amino acids are positive in acid, zwitterions at the isoelectric point, and negative in alkali.
- Peptide links (–CONH–) form by condensation; acid hydrolysis breaks them.
- Protein structure: primary (sequence), secondary (H bonds), tertiary (H bonds, ionic bonds, disulfide bridges).
- DNA: phosphate–deoxyribose backbone; A–T (2 H bonds), C–G (3 H bonds).
- Cisplatin binds to guanine and stops DNA from being copied.
:::
