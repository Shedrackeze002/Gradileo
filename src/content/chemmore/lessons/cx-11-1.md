Across Period 3, from sodium to sulfur, the elements change from a soft metal that fizzes on water to a yellow non-metal that burns with a blue flame. Their oxides change too, from strongly basic to strongly acidic, and the reason is the bonding in each one.

In this lesson you will describe how the Period 3 elements react with water and oxygen, and link the properties of their oxides to their structure and bonding.

## Reactions with water

**Sodium** reacts vigorously with cold water, fizzing and melting into a ball, and gives a strongly alkaline solution (pH 13–14):

$$\ce{2Na + 2H2O -> 2NaOH + H2}$$

**Magnesium** reacts only very slowly with cold water, giving a weakly alkaline solution (about pH 9–10) because magnesium hydroxide is only slightly soluble. With **steam** it burns with a bright white light:

$$\ce{Mg + H2O(g) -> MgO + H2}$$

## Reactions with oxygen

| Element | Observation | Oxide |
|---|---|---|
| Na | yellow-orange flame | $\ce{Na2O}$ |
| Mg | bright white flame | $\ce{MgO}$ |
| Al | white flame (as a powder) | $\ce{Al2O3}$ |
| Si | burns when strongly heated | $\ce{SiO2}$ |
| P | white flame, white smoke | $\ce{P4O10}$ |
| S | blue flame | $\ce{SO2}$ |

## Structure and bonding of the oxides

| Oxide | Bonding and structure | Melting point |
|---|---|---|
| $\ce{Na2O}$, $\ce{MgO}$, $\ce{Al2O3}$ | ionic lattice | high |
| $\ce{SiO2}$ | giant covalent (macromolecular) | very high |
| $\ce{P4O10}$, $\ce{SO2}$, $\ce{SO3}$ | simple covalent molecules | low |

$\ce{MgO}$ melts higher than $\ce{Na2O}$ because $\ce{Mg^2+}$ is smaller and more highly charged than $\ce{Na+}$. $\ce{SiO2}$ has to have strong covalent bonds broken to melt, while the molecular oxides only need weak intermolecular forces overcome.

## Oxides with water: acids and bases

The **ionic** oxides contain $\ce{O^2-}$ ions, which react with water to make hydroxide ions, so they are **basic**. The **simple covalent** oxides react with water to make acids.

| Oxide | Reaction with water | pH |
|---|---|---|
| $\ce{Na2O}$ | $\ce{Na2O + H2O -> 2NaOH}$ | 14 |
| $\ce{MgO}$ | $\ce{MgO + H2O -> Mg(OH)2}$ | about 9 |
| $\ce{Al2O3}$, $\ce{SiO2}$ | insoluble | 7 |
| $\ce{P4O10}$ | $\ce{P4O10 + 6H2O -> 4H3PO4}$ | about 0 |
| $\ce{SO2}$ | $\ce{SO2 + H2O -> H2SO3}$ | about 2–3 |
| $\ce{SO3}$ | $\ce{SO3 + H2O -> H2SO4}$ | about 0 |

## Neutralisation, and amphoteric aluminium oxide

Basic oxides neutralise acids, and acidic oxides neutralise bases. **Aluminium oxide** is **amphoteric**: it reacts with both.

:::example Aluminium oxide as an acid and as a base
Write equations for aluminium oxide reacting with hydrochloric acid and with sodium hydroxide solution.
@step As a base, with acid: $\ce{Al2O3 + 6HCl -> 2AlCl3 + 3H2O}$
@step As an acid, with alkali, forming the aluminate ion: $\ce{Al2O3 + 2NaOH + 3H2O -> 2NaAl(OH)4}$
@answer It reacts with both, so it is amphoteric.
:::

Silicon dioxide is insoluble in water, but it is still acidic: it reacts with hot, concentrated sodium hydroxide, $\ce{SiO2 + 2NaOH -> Na2SiO3 + H2O}$.

:::mistake Insoluble is not neutral
$\ce{SiO2}$ and $\ce{Al2O3}$ give pH 7 only because they do not dissolve. Their reactions with bases (and, for $\ce{Al2O3}$, with acids) show their real character.
:::

:::summary
- Sodium reacts vigorously with cold water; magnesium reacts slowly with cold water but burns in steam.
- Oxides of Na, Mg and Al are ionic; $\ce{SiO2}$ is giant covalent; P and S oxides are simple molecules.
- Ionic oxides are basic; simple covalent oxides form acids; $\ce{SiO2}$ is acidic.
- $\ce{Al2O3}$ is amphoteric: it reacts with acids and with bases.
:::
