Add a few drops of sodium hydroxide to a test tube of an unknown metal ion solution. A green, blue, brown or white precipitate appears, and its colour (and what happens with more alkali) tells you which metal ion is there.

In this lesson you will explain the acidity of metal aqua ions, identify ions from their test-tube reactions, and explain why multidentate ligands form such stable complexes.

## The acidity of metal aqua ions

In water, metal ions exist as **aqua ions** such as $\ce{[Fe(H2O)6]^3+}$. The metal ion pulls electron density from the O–H bonds of its water ligands, so a water ligand can lose an $\ce{H+}$:

$$\ce{[Fe(H2O)6]^3+ + H2O <=> [Fe(H2O)5(OH)]^2+ + H3O+}$$

A 3+ ion has a higher **charge density** than a 2+ ion, so it polarises the water ligands more. So solutions of 3+ aqua ions are **more acidic** than those of 2+ ions.

## Test-tube reactions

Hydroxide ions and ammonia both remove $\ce{H+}$ from the water ligands, giving neutral hydroxide precipitates.

| Aqua ion | NaOH(aq) or a little $\ce{NH3}$(aq) | Excess NaOH | Excess $\ce{NH3}$ | $\ce{Na2CO3}$(aq) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| $\ce{[Fe(H2O)6]^2+}$, green | green precipitate $\ce{Fe(OH)2(H2O)4}$ | no change | no change | green $\ce{FeCO3}$ precipitate |
| $\ce{[Cu(H2O)6]^2+}$, blue | blue precipitate $\ce{Cu(OH)2(H2O)4}$ | no change | deep blue solution $\ce{[Cu(NH3)4(H2O)2]^2+}$ | blue-green $\ce{CuCO3}$ precipitate |
| $\ce{[Fe(H2O)6]^3+}$, yellow-brown | brown precipitate $\ce{Fe(OH)3(H2O)3}$ | no change | no change | brown precipitate and bubbles of $\ce{CO2}$ |
| $\ce{[Al(H2O)6]^3+}$, colourless | white precipitate $\ce{Al(OH)3(H2O)3}$ | dissolves: $\ce{[Al(OH)4]-}$ | no change | white precipitate and bubbles of $\ce{CO2}$ |

The 2+ ions form **metal carbonates** with sodium carbonate. The 3+ ions are acidic enough to react with carbonate ions, so they give the **hydroxide** and fizz with carbon dioxide.

:::example Identifying an unknown solution
A colourless solution gives a white precipitate with sodium hydroxide, which dissolves in excess. With sodium carbonate it fizzes. What is the ion?
@step Colourless and a white precipitate: not iron or copper.
@step The precipitate dissolves in excess NaOH, so the hydroxide is amphoteric.
@step Fizzing with carbonate shows a 3+ ion.
@answer $\ce{[Al(H2O)6]^3+}$
:::

## Ligand substitution

Ligands can swap. $\ce{H2O}$ and $\ce{NH3}$ are similar in size, so the coordination number stays at 6. Chloride ions are larger, so only four fit and the complex becomes tetrahedral:

$$\ce{[Cu(H2O)6]^2+ + 4Cl- -> [CuCl4]^2- + 6H2O}$$
$$\ce{[Co(H2O)6]^2+ + 4Cl- -> [CoCl4]^2- + 6H2O}$$

The copper complex turns yellow-green and the cobalt complex turns from pink to blue.

## The chelate effect

When a multidentate ligand replaces monodentate ones, the number of particles goes **up**:

$$\ce{[Cu(H2O)6]^2+ + 3H2NCH2CH2NH2 -> [Cu(H2NCH2CH2NH2)3]^2+ + 6H2O}$$

Four particles become seven. The bonds broken and made are similar (Cu–O and Cu–N), so $\Delta H$ is close to zero, but $\Delta S$ is large and positive. So $\Delta G = \Delta H - T\Delta S$ is negative and the reaction is feasible. This extra stability is the **chelate effect**, and it is why EDTA grabs metal ions so strongly.

:::mistake The chelate effect is about entropy
The new bonds are not much stronger. The driving force is the increase in the number of particles, which makes $\Delta S$ positive.
:::

:::summary
- Metal aqua ions are acidic; 3+ ions are more acidic than 2+ ions because of their higher charge density.
- NaOH and $\ce{NH3}$ give hydroxide precipitates; $\ce{Al(OH)3}$ dissolves in excess NaOH, and copper hydroxide dissolves in excess $\ce{NH3}$.
- 2+ ions give metal carbonates; 3+ ions give hydroxides and $\ce{CO2}$.
- Chloride ligands give tetrahedral complexes with coordination number 4.
- The chelate effect: multidentate ligands increase the number of particles, so $\Delta S$ is positive.
:::
