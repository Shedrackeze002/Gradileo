Not every salt solution is neutral. Ammonium chloride solution turns blue litmus red, sodium carbonate solution feels soapy and turns red litmus blue, and common salt does neither. The difference comes from how the ions of each salt react with water. Salts also differ in what they contain: some still hold replaceable hydrogen, some hold hydroxide, and some hold two metals at once.

In this lesson you will classify salts as normal, acid, basic, double and complex salts, find water of crystallisation, predict whether a salt solution is acidic, neutral or alkaline, and describe deliquescent, efflorescent and hygroscopic substances.

## Types of salts

:::definition Salt
A **salt** is the compound formed when the hydrogen of an acid is replaced, wholly or partly, by a metal ion or the ammonium ion.
:::

| Type | What it contains | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Normal salt | no replaceable hydrogen | $\ce{NaCl}$, $\ce{K2SO4}$, $\ce{Na2CO3}$ |
| Acid salt | some replaceable hydrogen left | $\ce{NaHSO4}$, $\ce{NaHCO3}$ |
| Basic salt | hydroxide ions as well as the acid anion | $\ce{Zn(OH)Cl}$ |
| Double salt | two cations; ionises fully into all its simple ions | potash alum, $\ce{KAl(SO4)2.12H2O}$ |
| Complex salt | a complex ion that stays together in solution | $\ce{[Cu(NH3)4]SO4}$ |

Only an acid with more than one replaceable hydrogen can form an acid salt, so hydrochloric acid cannot.

2.86 g of washing soda, $\ce{Na2CO3.xH2O}$, is heated until all its water is driven off, leaving 1.06 g of anhydrous sodium carbonate (M = 106). Find x.
@step Water lost = 2.86 − 1.06 = 1.80 g, which is 1.80 ÷ 18 = 0.100 mol.
@step Sodium carbonate = 1.06 ÷ 106 = 0.0100 mol.
@answer x = 0.100 ÷ 0.0100 = 10

## Salt hydrolysis

**Salt hydrolysis** is the reaction of the ions of a salt with water, which can make the solution acidic or alkaline.

- A salt of a **strong acid and a strong base** (NaCl, KNO₃) gives a **neutral** solution.
- A salt of a **strong acid and a weak base** gives an **acidic** solution: $\ce{NH4+ + H2O <=> NH3 + H3O+}$. Small, highly charged metal ions such as $\ce{Al^3+}$ and $\ce{Fe^3+}$ do the same.
- A salt of a **weak acid and a strong base** gives an **alkaline** solution: $\ce{CO3^2- + H2O <=> HCO3- + OH-}$.

## Salts and water from the air

| Term | Meaning | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Deliquescent | absorbs water from the air until it dissolves | calcium chloride, sodium hydroxide |
| Efflorescent | loses water of crystallisation to the air | washing soda, Glauber's salt |
| Hygroscopic | absorbs water from the air without dissolving | concentrated sulfuric acid, quicklime, silica gel |

Hygroscopic and deliquescent substances are used as drying agents.

:::summary
- Salts are normal, acid, basic, double or complex.
- Water of crystallisation: compare the moles of water lost with the moles of anhydrous salt.
- Strong acid + strong base gives a neutral solution; strong acid + weak base, acidic; weak acid + strong base, alkaline.
- Deliquescent substances dissolve in the water they absorb; efflorescent ones lose water; hygroscopic ones absorb it but stay solid.
:::
