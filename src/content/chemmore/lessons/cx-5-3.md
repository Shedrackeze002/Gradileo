A diamond can cut glass, and the graphite in a pencil is soft enough to leave a trail on paper. Both are pure carbon. The difference lies entirely in how the carbon atoms are joined together.

In this lesson you will explain the properties of diamond, graphite, graphene and the fullerenes from their structures, recognise the size of nanoparticles, and calculate surface area to volume ratios.

## Diamond and graphite

:::definition Giant covalent structure
A **giant covalent structure** is a huge network of atoms joined by strong covalent bonds. Melting it means breaking a great many bonds, so it has a very high melting point.
:::

| | Diamond | Graphite |
|---|---|---|
| Bonds to each carbon atom | 4 | 3 |
| Structure | A rigid three-dimensional network | Layers of hexagons, with weak forces between the layers |
| Hardness | Very hard | Soft and slippery: the layers slide over each other |
| Conducts electricity? | No: every outer electron is in a bond | Yes: one electron from each atom is delocalised |
| Uses | Cutting tools and drill tips | Electrodes, lubricants, pencils |

:::definition Delocalised electron
A **delocalised electron** is not held between two particular atoms, and it can move through the structure, carrying charge.
:::

## Graphene and the fullerenes

**Graphene** is a single layer of graphite: carbon atoms in hexagons, one atom thick. It is very strong, light and an excellent conductor, so it is being developed for electronics and composites.

**Fullerenes** are molecules of carbon atoms with hollow shapes, built from rings of five, six or seven atoms. The first discovered was buckminsterfullerene, $\ce{C60}$, a hollow sphere. **Carbon nanotubes** are cylindrical fullerenes with very high length to diameter ratios. They are very strong when pulled (high tensile strength) and light, so they reinforce materials such as tennis rackets, and they conduct electricity.

:::example Choosing a form of carbon
An electrode for electrolysis must conduct electricity and survive high temperatures. Which form of carbon suits it?
@step Diamond does not conduct, so it is ruled out.
@step Graphite conducts, through its delocalised electrons, and has a very high melting point.
@answer Graphite
:::

## Nanoparticles

:::definition Nanoparticle
A **nanoparticle** is a particle between 1 nm and 100 nm across, where 1 nm (one nanometre) is $1 \times 10^{-9}$ m, one billionth of a metre. A nanoparticle contains only a few hundred atoms.
:::

:::check cx-nanoparticles 1
:::

The smaller a particle, the larger its surface for its size. For a cube:

$$\text{surface area} = 6 \times \text{side}^2 \qquad \text{volume} = \text{side}^3$$

:::example Surface area to volume ratio
A cube-shaped particle has sides of 2 nm. What is its surface area to volume ratio?
@step Surface area $= 6 \times 2^2 = 24\ \text{nm}^2$.
@step Volume $= 2^3 = 8\ \text{nm}^3$.
@step Ratio $= 24 \div 8$.
@answer 3
:::

When the side of a cube gets 10 times smaller, its surface area to volume ratio gets 10 times larger.

:::check cx-nanoparticles 2
:::

Because so much of a nanoparticle is surface, a smaller mass does the same job. Nanoparticles are used as catalysts, in sun creams (which block ultraviolet light without leaving a white film) and in wound dressings that kill bacteria. Their small size is also a risk: they may pass into the body and its cells, and their long-term effects are not yet fully known.

:::summary
- **Diamond**: each carbon forms 4 bonds in a rigid network, so it is very hard and does not conduct.
- **Graphite**: each carbon forms 3 bonds in layers that slide, and the fourth electron is delocalised, so it is soft and conducts.
- **Graphene** is one layer of graphite. **Fullerenes** are hollow carbon molecules, and **nanotubes** are strong, light cylinders.
- **Nanoparticles** are 1 to 100 nm across. Their huge surface area to volume ratio makes them useful, but their effects on health are still being studied.
:::
