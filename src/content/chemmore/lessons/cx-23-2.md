Hydrochloric acid gives up its proton completely in water, while hydrogen sulfide barely does. Ethanoic acid gives up its proton far more readily than ethanol, though both have an O–H group. The difference lies in the structure of each acid and, above all, in how stable its conjugate base is.

In this lesson you will explain acid strength from molecular structure, and use pKa to decide which form of an acid is present at a given pH.

## Acid strength and structure

An acid HA is stronger when it loses its proton more easily, which happens when the conjugate base A⁻ is more **stable**. Three features make the conjugate base more stable.

- **Electronegativity.** Across a period, a more electronegative atom holds the negative charge better, so HCl is stronger than $\ce{H2S}$.
- **Extra oxygen atoms.** In oxoacids, each extra oxygen pulls electron density away from the O–H bond and spreads out the charge, so $\ce{HClO4}$ is stronger than $\ce{HClO3}$, which is stronger than $\ce{HClO}$.
- **Resonance.** The ethanoate ion spreads its charge over two oxygen atoms, while the ethoxide ion keeps it on one, so ethanoic acid is far stronger than ethanol.

## Which form is present?

:::definition pKa
$$\text{p}K_a = -\log K_a$$
A smaller pKa means a stronger acid.
:::

Comparing the pH of a solution with the pKa of an acid tells you which form dominates.

- **pH < pKa**: the protonated form, HA, is present in the larger amount.
- **pH = pKa**: HA and A⁻ are present in equal amounts.
- **pH > pKa**: the deprotonated form, A⁻, is present in the larger amount.

:::example An amino acid's carboxyl group
A carboxyl group has a pKa of 2.3. In blood at pH 7.4, which form is present?
@step pH 7.4 is far above the pKa of 2.3.
@answer The deprotonated form, $\ce{-COO-}$, dominates.
:::

## Indicators

An acid–base indicator is a weak acid whose two forms have different colours. It changes colour over the pH range around its own pKa, so the best indicator for a titration has a pKa close to the pH at the equivalence point.

:::summary
- An acid is stronger when its conjugate base is more stable: more electronegative atoms, extra oxygens and resonance all help.
- $\text{p}K_a = -\log K_a$, and a smaller pKa means a stronger acid.
- Below the pKa the protonated form dominates, at the pKa the two are equal, and above it the deprotonated form dominates.
- Choose an indicator whose pKa is close to the pH at the equivalence point.
:::
