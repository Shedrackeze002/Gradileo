A steel bike left out in the rain grows orange flakes, while an aluminium ladder left beside it looks almost the same for years. Rusting costs a great deal every year in repairs and replacements, so preventing it, and choosing the right material in the first place, matters.

In this lesson you will say what iron needs in order to rust, explain barrier methods and sacrificial protection, explain why alloys are harder than pure metals, and compare ceramics, polymers and composites.

## Corrosion and rusting

:::definition Corrosion
**Corrosion** is the destruction of a material by chemical reactions with substances in its surroundings. **Rusting** is the corrosion of iron.
:::

Iron rusts only when **both oxygen and water** are present. A nail in dry air, or in boiled water sealed under oil, does not rust. Salt water makes rusting faster.

$$\text{iron} + \text{oxygen} + \text{water} \rightarrow \text{hydrated iron(III) oxide (rust)}$$

## Preventing rust

**Barrier methods** keep oxygen and water away from the iron: painting, coating with oil or grease, and electroplating with another metal.

**Sacrificial protection** uses a metal that is **more reactive** than iron. It reacts with the oxygen and water in place of the iron, so it corrodes and the iron is left alone. Blocks of zinc or magnesium are bolted to ships' hulls and buried pipelines for this reason.

**Galvanising** coats iron with zinc and does both: the zinc is a barrier, and if it is scratched the zinc still corrodes first.

:::example Choosing a sacrificial metal
Order of reactivity, most reactive first: magnesium, zinc, iron, tin, copper. Will tin give sacrificial protection to an iron pipe?
@step A sacrificial metal must be more reactive than iron, so that it corrodes first.
@step Tin is below iron in the order.
@answer No. Tin is less reactive than iron, so the iron would corrode instead.
:::

## Alloys

:::definition Alloy
An **alloy** is a mixture of a metal with one or more other elements, usually other metals or carbon.
:::

In a pure metal the atoms are all the same size and sit in regular layers, so the layers slide over each other easily and the metal is soft. In an alloy the different-sized atoms distort the layers, so they cannot slide as easily, and the alloy is **harder**.

| Alloy | Made of | Useful because |
|---|---|---|
| Bronze | Copper and tin | Hard and resists corrosion: statues and bells |
| Brass | Copper and zinc | Hard and does not tarnish easily: fittings and instruments |
| Steels | Iron with carbon and other metals | Strong; stainless steel (with chromium and nickel) does not rust |
| Gold alloys | Gold with silver, copper and zinc | Harder than pure gold, which is too soft for jewellery |
| Aluminium alloys | Aluminium with other metals | Light and strong: aircraft |

The purity of gold is given in **carats**. Pure gold is 24 carat, so 18 carat gold is $\frac{18}{24} = 75\%$ gold.

## Ceramics, polymers and composites

- **Glass**: most is soda-lime glass, made by heating sand, sodium carbonate and limestone. Borosilicate glass, with boron trioxide, melts at a higher temperature.
- **Clay ceramics** such as pottery and bricks are made by shaping wet clay and then heating it in a furnace.
- **Polymers**: their properties depend on the monomers and the reaction conditions. Low density and high density poly(ethene) come from the same monomer made under different conditions. **Thermosoftening** polymers melt when heated, because there are only weak forces between their chains, while **thermosetting** polymers have cross-links between the chains and do not melt.
- **Composites** are made of one material (the reinforcement) set in another (the matrix). Concrete, fibreglass and carbon fibre are examples.

:::summary
- Iron rusts only when both oxygen and water are present.
- **Barrier methods** (paint, grease, electroplating) keep them out; **sacrificial protection** uses a more reactive metal that corrodes first.
- **Alloys** are harder than pure metals because different-sized atoms stop the layers sliding. Pure gold is 24 carat.
- Glass and clay ceramics are made by heating; polymers depend on their monomers and conditions; composites set a reinforcement in a matrix.
:::
