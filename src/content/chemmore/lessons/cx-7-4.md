In a flask of gas, the molecules do not all move at the same speed. A few crawl, most move at a middling speed, and a few race. Only the fastest have enough energy to react when they collide, and that small group decides how fast the reaction goes.

In this lesson you will read a Maxwell–Boltzmann distribution, and use it to explain the effects of temperature and catalysts on rate.

## The distribution of energies

:::definition Maxwell–Boltzmann distribution
A graph of the **number of molecules** with each **energy**, for a gas at one temperature.
:::

Four features of the curve matter:

- It starts at the **origin**: no molecule has zero energy.
- The **peak** is the **most probable energy**. The mean energy is a little to its right, because of the long tail.
- It never meets the energy axis at high energy: there is **no maximum energy**.
- The **area under the curve** is the total number of molecules.

:::

## Activation energy

:::definition Activation energy, $E_a$
The minimum energy that colliding particles need for a reaction to happen.
:::

Only the molecules to the **right** of $E_a$ on the curve can react when they collide. Most collisions fail, because most molecules have less than $E_a$.

:::check cx-maxwell-boltzmann 2
:::

:::example Comparing rates
At one temperature, 1.5% of molecules have at least the activation energy. At a temperature 10 K higher, 3.0% do. If the rate is proportional to this percentage, how many times faster is the reaction?
@step Factor $= \dfrac{3.0}{1.5}$
@answer 2.0 times as fast
:::

:::check cx-maxwell-boltzmann 3
:::

## Raising the temperature

At a higher temperature, more molecules have high energies, so:

- the peak moves to the **right** and becomes **lower**, and the curve flattens;
- the **area stays the same**, because the number of molecules has not changed;
- the area beyond $E_a$ is **much larger**.

The molecules also collide a little more often, but the main reason a small temperature rise speeds up a reaction so much is the large increase in the number of molecules with at least $E_a$.

:::mistake The curve does not get bigger
When the temperature rises, the curve spreads out but its area is unchanged. Drawing a hotter curve that is higher everywhere would mean more molecules.
:::

:::check cx-mb-changes 1
:::

## Catalysts and concentration

- A **catalyst** gives an alternative route with a **lower activation energy**. The curve itself does not change; the $E_a$ line moves to the left, so more of the area lies beyond it.
- A higher **concentration** (or pressure) at the same temperature puts more molecules in the same volume. The curve keeps its shape but is **taller**. $E_a$ does not change, but there are more molecules beyond it in each unit of volume, so successful collisions happen more often.

:::

:::check cx-mb-changes 3
:::

:::summary
- The Maxwell–Boltzmann curve starts at the origin, peaks at the most probable energy, and never meets the energy axis.
- The area under it is the number of molecules; the area beyond $E_a$ is the number that can react.
- Higher temperature: lower peak, moved right, same area, many more molecules beyond $E_a$.
- A catalyst lowers $E_a$ without changing the curve.
- Higher concentration: a taller curve of the same shape.
:::
