Ice melts on a warm day even though melting takes in energy. Enthalpy alone cannot explain this. Nature also favours spreading out, and chemists measure that spreading with **entropy**.

In this lesson you will predict and calculate entropy changes, and combine them with enthalpy to decide whether a reaction can happen.

## Entropy

:::definition Entropy, S
A measure of the number of ways that particles and their energy can be arranged. The more ways, the higher the entropy. Its units are J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹.
:::

Gases have far more entropy than liquids, and liquids more than solids. A perfect crystal at 0 K has zero entropy. So entropy **increases** when:

- a solid melts or a liquid boils;
- a solid dissolves;
- a reaction makes **more moles of gas** than it uses.

## Calculating an entropy change

$$\Delta S = \Sigma S(\text{products}) - \Sigma S(\text{reactants})$$

Multiply each standard entropy by the number of moles in the equation. Elements have non-zero entropies, so you cannot leave them out.

:::example The entropy change for the Haber process
$\ce{N2(g) + 3H2(g) -> 2NH3(g)}$. Standard entropies in J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹: $\ce{N2}$ 192, $\ce{H2}$ 131, $\ce{NH3}$ 193.
@step Products: $2 \times 193 = 386$.
@step Reactants: $192 + 3 \times 131 = 585$.
@answer $\Delta S = 386 - 585 = -199$ J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹, negative because 4 moles of gas become 2.
:::

## Gibbs free energy

Whether a reaction is **feasible** (can happen without outside help) depends on both $\Delta H$ and $\Delta S$, combined in the Gibbs free-energy change:

$$\Delta G = \Delta H - T\Delta S$$

A reaction is feasible when $\Delta G \le 0$. Here $T$ is in kelvin, and $\Delta S$ must be converted to **kJ** K⁻¹ mol⁻¹ (divide by 1000) to match $\Delta H$.

:::mistake Convert the entropy units
$\Delta S$ is usually given in J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹, but $\Delta H$ is in kJ mol⁻¹. Forgetting to divide $\Delta S$ by 1000 gives an answer a thousand times too big.
:::

## The temperature at which a reaction becomes feasible

When $\Delta H$ and $\Delta S$ are both positive, a reaction becomes feasible above the temperature where $\Delta G = 0$:

$$T = \frac{\Delta H}{\Delta S}$$

:::example Decomposing calcium carbonate
For $\ce{CaCO3(s) -> CaO(s) + CO2(g)}$, $\Delta H = +178$ kJ mol⁻¹ and $\Delta S = +161$ J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹. Above what temperature is it feasible?
@step Convert: $\Delta S = 0.161$ kJ K⁻¹ mol⁻¹.
@step $T = \dfrac{178}{0.161} = 1105.6$ K.
@answer Above about 1106 K
:::

A feasible reaction may still be very slow if its activation energy is high, so $\Delta G$ tells you whether a reaction *can* go, not how fast.

:::summary
- Entropy measures the number of ways of arranging particles and energy; gases have the most.
- $\Delta S = \Sigma S(\text{products}) - \Sigma S(\text{reactants})$.
- $\Delta G = \Delta H - T\Delta S$, with $\Delta S$ in kJ K⁻¹ mol⁻¹; feasible when $\Delta G \le 0$.
- $\Delta G = 0$ at $T = \Delta H / \Delta S$.
- Feasible does not mean fast.
:::
