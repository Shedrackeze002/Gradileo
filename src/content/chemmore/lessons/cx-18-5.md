Carbon and silicon sit one above the other in Group 4, so you might expect their oxides to be alike. Yet carbon dioxide is a gas you breathe out and silicon dioxide is sand. Structure explains the difference, just as it explains why some carbonates fall apart on gentle heating while others survive a Bunsen flame.

In this lesson you will compare silicon dioxide with carbon dioxide, describe the uses of silicon and its compounds, and explain the trends in the thermal stability of carbonates and nitrates.

## Silicon and silicon dioxide

:::definition Giant covalent lattice
A **giant covalent lattice** is a structure in which every atom is joined to its neighbours by covalent bonds, throughout the whole solid.
:::

| | Carbon dioxide | Silicon dioxide |
|---|---|---|
| Structure | small $\ce{O=C=O}$ molecules | giant lattice; each Si bonded to four O |
| State at room temperature | gas | hard solid |
| Melting | only weak forces between molecules to overcome | many strong Si–O bonds to break |

Carbon forms strong double bonds with oxygen. Silicon is too large to form them well, so it makes four single Si–O bonds instead, which link every atom into one lattice. Silicon itself has a diamond-like structure.

Silicon dioxide is an **acidic oxide**: it reacts with hot sodium hydroxide to give sodium silicate, $\ce{SiO2 + 2NaOH -> Na2SiO3 + H2O}$. Silicon is obtained by heating silica with carbon in an electric furnace, $\ce{SiO2 + 2C -> Si + 2CO}$.

| Substance | Use |
|---|---|
| Silicon | semiconductor for computer chips and solar cells |
| Silica (sand) | glass (with sodium carbonate and limestone), cement |
| Silica gel | drying agent in packets |
| Silicones | sealants, lubricants, heat-resistant rubbers |

## Thermal stability of carbonates and nitrates

:::definition Polarising power
The **polarising power** of a cation is its ability to distort the electron cloud of a nearby anion. Small, highly charged cations have a high **charge density** and so a high polarising power.
:::

A cation with high polarising power pulls electron density from the carbonate or nitrate ion, weakening its bonds so it breaks up more easily. Down Group 2 the cations get larger, so their polarising power falls and the salts need **higher** temperatures to decompose:

$$\ce{MgCO3} < \ce{CaCO3} < \ce{SrCO3} < \ce{BaCO3} \quad \text{(increasing thermal stability)}$$

Group 1 ions carry only a +1 charge and are larger, so Group 1 carbonates are more stable than Group 2 ones: sodium and potassium carbonates do not decompose in a Bunsen flame. Lithium is the exception: its small ion behaves like $\ce{Mg^2+}$, so lithium nitrate gives the oxide, nitrogen dioxide and oxygen.

:::summary
- CO₂ is a gas of small molecules; SiO₂ is a hard giant covalent lattice.
- SiO₂ is acidic; silicon is a semiconductor; silica makes glass; silica gel dries; silicones seal.
- Small, highly charged cations polarise anions and make their salts less stable to heat.
- Thermal stability rises down Group 2, and Group 1 salts are more stable than Group 2 salts.
:::
