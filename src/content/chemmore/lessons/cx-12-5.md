Amines are what you get when the hydrogens of ammonia are swapped for carbon groups. They keep ammonia’s lone pair, so they are bases, and they are the starting point for dyes, drugs and the fabric softener in a washing machine.

In this lesson you will classify amines, explain why some are stronger bases than others, and describe how amines are made.

## Classifying amines

| Type | Carbons on the N | Example |
|---|---|---|
| primary | 1 | ethylamine, $\ce{CH3CH2NH2}$ |
| secondary | 2 | dimethylamine, $\ce{(CH3)2NH}$ |
| tertiary | 3 | trimethylamine, $\ce{(CH3)3N}$ |
| quaternary ammonium ion | 4 | $\ce{(CH3)4N+}$ |

Quaternary ammonium salts with a long carbon chain are **cationic surfactants**. Their positive heads stick to negatively charged fibres and hair, so they are used in fabric softeners and hair conditioners.

## Amines as bases

An amine is a base because the lone pair on its nitrogen accepts a proton:

$$\ce{CH3CH2NH2 + HCl -> CH3CH2NH3+Cl-}$$

The more available the lone pair, the stronger the base:

- **Alkyl amines** are stronger bases than ammonia, because alkyl groups push electron density onto the nitrogen.
- **Phenylamine** is a weaker base than ammonia, because the lone pair is partly delocalised into the benzene ring.

:::example Ordering base strength
Put ammonia, phenylamine and methylamine in order of base strength, strongest first.
@step Methylamine: the methyl group pushes electrons onto the N, so its lone pair is the most available.
@step Phenylamine: its lone pair is drawn into the ring, so it is the least available.
@answer Methylamine > ammonia > phenylamine
:::

## Making amines

**From halogenoalkanes.** Heat with **excess ammonia** in ethanol, in a sealed tube. The product is itself a nucleophile, so it can react again to give secondary and tertiary amines and quaternary salts. Excess ammonia makes this less likely, but you still get a mixture.

$$\ce{CH3CH2Br + 2NH3 -> CH3CH2NH2 + NH4Br}$$

**From nitriles.** Reduce with hydrogen and a nickel catalyst, or with $\ce{LiAlH4}$. This gives **only** the primary amine, so it is a better way to make a pure one:

$$\ce{CH3CH2CN + 4[H] -> CH3CH2CH2NH2}$$

**Aromatic amines.** Reduce nitrobenzene with **tin and concentrated hydrochloric acid**, then add sodium hydroxide to free the amine:

$$\ce{C6H5NO2 + 6[H] -> C6H5NH2 + 2H2O}$$

:::mistake Counting the carbons after the nitrile route
Making a nitrile with KCN adds a carbon, so bromoethane (2 carbons) gives propanenitrile, which reduces to **propylamine** (3 carbons), not ethylamine.
:::

:::summary
- Amines are primary, secondary or tertiary by the number of carbons on the N; quaternary ions have four.
- Amines are bases because the N lone pair accepts $\ce{H+}$.
- Base strength: alkyl amines > ammonia > phenylamine.
- Halogenoalkane + excess $\ce{NH3}$ gives a mixture; reducing a nitrile gives only the primary amine.
- Nitrobenzene + Sn/conc. HCl, then NaOH, gives phenylamine.
:::
