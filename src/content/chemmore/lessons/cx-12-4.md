Benzene, $\ce{C6H6}$, looks as if it should have three C=C double bonds, yet it does not decolourise bromine water the way alkenes do. Its electrons are shared in a ring, and that ring is so stable that benzene would rather swap a hydrogen than lose it.

In this lesson you will use evidence to explain the structure of benzene, and describe its electrophilic substitution reactions.

## The structure of benzene

In benzene, each carbon uses three electrons in σ bonds and has one p electron left. These six p electrons overlap above and below the ring to form a **delocalised** ring of electrons. The molecule is a flat hexagon with bond angles of 120°.

The evidence:

- **Bond lengths**: all six C–C bonds are 0.139 nm, between a C–C single bond (0.154 nm) and a C=C double bond (0.134 nm).
- **Enthalpy of hydrogenation**: cyclohexene, with one C=C, has $-120$ kJ mol⁻¹. A ring with three separate C=C bonds would be expected to have $3 \times (-120) = -360$ kJ mol⁻¹, but benzene’s is only $-208$ kJ mol⁻¹.

:::example How much more stable is benzene?
Use the hydrogenation data above.
@step Expected for three separate C=C bonds: $-360$ kJ mol⁻¹.
@step Actual for benzene: $-208$ kJ mol⁻¹.
@answer Benzene is $360 - 208 = 152$ kJ mol⁻¹ more stable, because of delocalisation.
:::

## Electrophilic substitution

The ring of electrons attracts **electrophiles**. Addition would destroy the delocalised ring, so benzene reacts by **substitution**: an H is replaced and the ring is restored.

### Nitration

Benzene is warmed to about 50 °C with **concentrated nitric acid and concentrated sulfuric acid**. The acids make the electrophile, the **nitronium ion**:

$$\ce{HNO3 + 2H2SO4 -> NO2+ + H3O+ + 2HSO4-}$$

$$\ce{C6H6 + NO2+ -> C6H5NO2 + H+}$$

Above about 55 °C, more than one nitro group is substituted. Nitrobenzene is used to make explosives such as TNT and, by reduction, phenylamine for dyes.

### Friedel–Crafts acylation

Benzene is heated with an **acyl chloride** and an **aluminium chloride** catalyst, which makes the electrophile $\ce{RCO+}$:

$$\ce{CH3COCl + AlCl3 -> CH3CO+ + AlCl4-}$$

$$\ce{C6H6 + CH3CO+ -> C6H5COCH3 + H+}$$

The product is the aromatic ketone **phenylethanone**. The $\ce{H+}$ reacts with $\ce{AlCl4-}$ to give back $\ce{AlCl3}$ and HCl.

:::mistake Substitution, not addition
Benzene and an alkene both react with electrophiles, but an alkene **adds** them while benzene **substitutes**, keeping its ring of delocalised electrons.
:::

:::summary
- Benzene has a ring of six delocalised p electrons; all C–C bonds are the same length.
- Its enthalpy of hydrogenation is less exothermic than expected, so it is more stable.
- It reacts by electrophilic substitution, keeping the ring.
- Nitration: conc. $\ce{HNO3}$ + conc. $\ce{H2SO4}$, 50 °C, electrophile $\ce{NO2+}$.
- Friedel–Crafts acylation: acyl chloride + $\ce{AlCl3}$, electrophile $\ce{RCO+}$, gives an aromatic ketone.
:::
