Glucose is $\ce{C6H12O6}$, and the acid in vinegar is $\ce{C2H4O2}$. They taste nothing alike, yet an analysis of what each is made of gives exactly the same answer: one carbon atom and one oxygen atom for every two hydrogen atoms. The simplest ratio of the atoms is one formula, and the real molecule is another.

In this lesson you will work out an empirical formula from masses or percentages, and turn it into a molecular formula using the relative formula mass.

## The empirical formula

:::definition Empirical formula
The **empirical formula** of a compound shows the simplest whole-number ratio of the atoms of each element in it.
:::

:::definition Molecular formula
The **molecular formula** shows the actual number of atoms of each element in one molecule. It is the empirical formula, or a whole-number multiple of it.
:::

To go from a molecular formula to an empirical formula, divide all the numbers by the largest whole number that goes into every one of them. Glucose, $\ce{C6H12O6}$, has 6, 12 and 6 atoms. All divide by 6, so its empirical formula is $\ce{CH2O}$. Water, $\ce{H2O}$, cannot be simplified, so its empirical formula is the same as its molecular formula.

## From masses to a formula

An experiment gives the **mass** of each element in a compound, but a formula counts **atoms**. Atoms of different elements have different masses, so the masses must be converted first. Dividing each mass by the relative atomic mass, $A_r$, gives the number of moles of atoms of that element, and the ratio of the moles is the ratio of the atoms.

1. Write down the mass of each element.
2. Divide each mass by the relative atomic mass of that element.
3. Divide every answer by the smallest of them.
4. If the numbers are not whole, multiply them all by 2 (or 3) to make them whole.

:::example Magnesium oxide
A compound contains $4.8\ \text{g}$ of magnesium and $3.2\ \text{g}$ of oxygen. What is its empirical formula? (Relative atomic masses: Mg = 24, O = 16.)
@step Divide by $A_r$. Mg: $4.8 \div 24 = 0.2$. O: $3.2 \div 16 = 0.2$.
@step Divide by the smallest, $0.2$. Mg: $1$. O: $1$.
@answer $\ce{MgO}$
:::

:::example When the ratio is not whole
A compound contains $5.4\ \text{g}$ of aluminium and $4.8\ \text{g}$ of oxygen. What is its empirical formula? (Relative atomic masses: Al = 27, O = 16.)
@step Divide by $A_r$. Al: $5.4 \div 27 = 0.2$. O: $4.8 \div 16 = 0.3$.
@step Divide by the smallest, $0.2$. Al: $1$. O: $1.5$.
@step $1.5$ is not a whole number, so multiply both by 2. Al: $2$. O: $3$.
@answer $\ce{Al2O3}$
:::

:::mistake Do not round 1.5 to 2
A ratio of $1 : 1.5$ is not $1 : 2$ and it is not $1 : 1$. A value ending in $.5$ means multiply everything by 2; a value ending in $.33$ or $.67$ means multiply everything by 3. Round only when a number is very close to a whole number, such as $1.99$ or $3.02$.
:::

## From percentages, and from an experiment

Percentages by mass are handled in exactly the same way. Imagine $100\ \text{g}$ of the compound: a compound that is 40% calcium then holds $40\ \text{g}$ of calcium, so each percentage can be used as a mass in grams.

:::example Calcium carbonate
By mass, a compound is 40% calcium, 12% carbon and 48% oxygen. What is its empirical formula? (Relative atomic masses: Ca = 40, C = 12, O = 16.)
@step Divide by $A_r$. Ca: $40 \div 40 = 1$. C: $12 \div 12 = 1$. O: $48 \div 16 = 3$.
@step The smallest is $1$, so the ratio is already $1 : 1 : 3$.
@answer $\ce{CaCO3}$
:::

In an experiment, a metal is often heated in air until it has all turned into its oxide. The mass of oxygen is not measured directly: it is the **gain in mass**.

:::example Heating copper
$12.7\ \text{g}$ of copper is heated in oxygen until it has all reacted. The oxide formed has a mass of $15.9\ \text{g}$. What is its empirical formula? (Relative atomic masses: Cu = 63.5, O = 16.)
@step Mass of oxygen $= 15.9 - 12.7 = 3.2\ \text{g}$.
@step Divide by $A_r$. Cu: $12.7 \div 63.5 = 0.2$. O: $3.2 \div 16 = 0.2$.
@step The ratio is $1 : 1$.
@answer $\ce{CuO}$
:::

## The molecular formula

The empirical formula alone cannot tell glucose from the acid in vinegar. One more fact settles it: the relative formula mass, $M_r$, of the compound.

1. Work out the relative formula mass of the empirical formula.
2. Divide the $M_r$ of the compound by it. The answer is a whole number.
3. Multiply every number in the empirical formula by that whole number.

:::example Butane
A compound has the empirical formula $\ce{C2H5}$ and a relative formula mass of 58. What is its molecular formula? (Relative atomic masses: C = 12, H = 1.)
@step $M_r$ of $\ce{C2H5}$: $(2 \times 12) + (5 \times 1) = 29$.
@step $58 \div 29 = 2$.
@step Multiply each number by 2.
@answer $\ce{C4H10}$
:::

:::tip A quick check
Add up the relative formula mass of your molecular formula. It must equal the $M_r$ given in the question. For $\ce{C4H10}$: $(4 \times 12) + (10 \times 1) = 58$.
:::

:::summary
- The **empirical formula** is the simplest whole-number ratio of the atoms in a compound. The **molecular formula** is the actual number of atoms in a molecule.
- To find an empirical formula: divide each mass (or percentage) by the relative atomic mass, divide by the smallest answer, then multiply up if needed to reach whole numbers.
- When a metal is heated to make its oxide, the mass of oxygen is the gain in mass.
- Molecular formula = empirical formula × ($M_r$ of the compound ÷ $M_r$ of the empirical formula).
:::
