A battery's voltage measures how hard its reaction pushes electrons around a circuit. That push is the same driving force that free energy describes, so the cell potential, the free energy change and the equilibrium constant all tell the same story in different units.

In this lesson you will link cell potential to free energy, and predict how a cell's potential changes away from standard conditions.

## Cell potential and free energy

:::definition Cell potential and free energy
$$\Delta G^\circ = -nFE^\circ$$
where $n$ is the number of moles of electrons transferred in the equation as written, $F = 96\,485$ C/mol is the Faraday constant, and $E^\circ$ is the standard cell potential in volts. The answer is in J/mol.
:::

So a positive $E^\circ$ gives a negative $\Delta G^\circ$, and the reaction is favoured, which is why a voltaic cell can produce a current. A negative $E^\circ$ means the reaction is not favoured, and it must be driven by an outside source in an electrolytic cell.

:::example A Daniell cell
For $\ce{Zn(s) + Cu^2+(aq) -> Zn^2+(aq) + Cu(s)}$, $E^\circ = +1.10$ V and $n = 2$.
@step $\Delta G^\circ = -2 \times 96\,485 \times 1.10 = -212\,267$ J/mol.
@answer $\Delta G^\circ = -212$ kJ/mol.
:::

## Nonstandard conditions

$E^\circ$ is the potential under standard conditions, with every solution at 1 mol/L, where $Q = 1$. Under other conditions the potential changes, and it acts as a measure of how far the cell is from equilibrium.

- If the conditions make $Q$ **smaller than 1**, the cell is farther from equilibrium and $E$ is **greater** than $E^\circ$.
- If they make $Q$ **larger than 1**, the cell is closer to equilibrium and $E$ is **smaller** than $E^\circ$.
- As the cell runs, $Q$ rises towards $K$ and $E$ falls, reaching **zero at equilibrium**, when the battery is flat.

A working cell is not at equilibrium, so Le Chatelier's principle does not apply to it. Think instead about whether a change moves the cell closer to equilibrium or farther from it.

## Concentration cells

A **concentration cell** has the same metal and the same ion on both sides, at different concentrations. Its $E^\circ$ is zero, but it still gives a voltage because the system moves towards equal concentrations. The dilute side makes more ions, so oxidation happens there and it is the anode, while the concentrated side uses ions up at the cathode.

:::

:::summary
- $\Delta G^\circ = -nFE^\circ$: a positive cell potential means a favoured reaction.
- $E^\circ$ applies when $Q = 1$. Smaller $Q$ gives a larger $E$, larger $Q$ a smaller $E$, and $E = 0$ at equilibrium.
- Le Chatelier's principle does not apply to a working cell, because it is not at equilibrium.
- In a concentration cell, the dilute side is the anode, and electrons flow until the concentrations are equal.
:::
