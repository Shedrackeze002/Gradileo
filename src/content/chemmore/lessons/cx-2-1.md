A carton of orange juice says "100% pure", and a chemist would disagree: the juice is water, sugars, acids and many other substances mixed together. In chemistry the word has a much stricter meaning, and one simple measurement, the temperature at which a solid melts, can show whether a sample deserves it.

In this lesson you will decide whether a substance is pure from the way it melts, say what a formulation is, and name a gas from the result of a simple test.

## Pure substances

:::definition Pure substance
A **pure substance** contains only one element or only one compound, with nothing else mixed in. In everyday speech "pure" means natural, or with nothing added; in chemistry it means a single substance.
:::

Distilled water is pure: it is the compound water and nothing else. Mineral water is not, because calcium, magnesium and sodium salts are dissolved in it, even though nobody added them. Air is a mixture of gases, so it is not pure either, however clean it is.

:::mistake More than one element does not mean impure
A compound such as sodium chloride contains two elements, but they are chemically joined into one substance. A crystal of sodium chloride is pure. A substance is impure only when two or more substances are **mixed**.
:::

## Melting points and purity

A pure substance melts at one fixed temperature, its **melting point**, and it melts sharply: the whole sample turns to liquid at that temperature. An impurity changes this in two ways. It **lowers** the melting point, and it makes the sample melt **over a range** of temperatures.

| What is seen | What it shows |
|---|---|
| Melts sharply at the known melting point | The pure substance |
| Melts sharply, but at a different temperature | A pure substance, but a different one |
| Starts to melt below the melting point, and melts over a range | The substance with an impurity |

:::example Is it pure aspirin?
Pure aspirin melts at $136^\circ\text{C}$. A sample starts to melt at $128^\circ\text{C}$ and finishes at $133^\circ\text{C}$. What does this show?
@step The sample melts below $136^\circ\text{C}$.
@step It melts over a range of 5 degrees, not sharply.
@answer The sample is aspirin with an impurity in it.
:::

The bigger the amount of impurity, the lower the sample starts to melt and the wider the range. Boiling points can be used in the same way: an impurity usually **raises** the boiling point of a liquid, which is why salty water boils a little above $100^\circ\text{C}$.

## Formulations

:::definition Formulation
A **formulation** is a mixture that has been designed as a useful product. Each substance in it is added in a carefully measured amount, and each has a job to do.
:::

A paint is a formulation: a pigment gives the colour, a binder holds the pigment to the wall, and a solvent keeps the paint runny until it dries. Medicines, fertilisers, fuels, alloys, cleaning products and foods are formulations too. A formulation is never a pure substance, however carefully it is made, because it is a mixture by design.

## Tests for four gases

Many reactions give off a colourless gas. Four simple tests tell the common ones apart.

| Gas | Test | Result |
|---|---|---|
| Hydrogen, $\ce{H2}$ | Hold a **burning** splint at the mouth of the tube | The gas burns with a squeaky pop |
| Oxygen, $\ce{O2}$ | Put a **glowing** splint into the tube | The splint relights |
| Carbon dioxide, $\ce{CO2}$ | Bubble the gas through limewater | The limewater turns milky |
| Chlorine, $\ce{Cl2}$ | Hold **damp** litmus paper in the gas | The paper is bleached white |

Each test follows from what the gas does. Hydrogen burns, and a small amount mixed with air burns with a pop. Oxygen does not burn, but it makes other things burn better, so a splint that is only glowing bursts back into flame. Carbon dioxide reacts with limewater, a solution of calcium hydroxide, to make tiny white particles of calcium carbonate. Chlorine reacts with the water on damp litmus paper to make substances that bleach.

:::example Which gas is it?
Magnesium ribbon is dropped into dilute hydrochloric acid and the mixture fizzes. Which test identifies the gas?
@step A metal and an acid give a salt and hydrogen.
@step The test for hydrogen is a burning splint.
@answer Hold a burning splint at the mouth of the tube. The gas burns with a squeaky pop.
:::

:::mistake Burning splint or glowing splint?
The two splint tests are easy to swap. A **burning** splint is for hydrogen, the gas that burns. A **glowing** splint is for oxygen, the gas that relights it. A glowing splint put into carbon dioxide simply goes out.

:::summary
- A **pure substance** is a single element or a single compound. Mineral water, air and milk are mixtures, so they are not pure in the chemist's sense.
- A pure substance melts **sharply at a fixed temperature**. An impurity **lowers** the melting point and makes the sample melt **over a range**.
- A **formulation** is a mixture designed as a useful product, with each part in a measured amount: paints, medicines, fertilisers, fuels and alloys.
- Hydrogen: a burning splint gives a squeaky pop. Oxygen: a glowing splint relights. Carbon dioxide: limewater turns milky. Chlorine: damp litmus paper is bleached white.
:::
