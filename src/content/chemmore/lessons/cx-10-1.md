You cannot measure the lattice enthalpy of sodium chloride directly, because you cannot collect a mole of gaseous sodium and chloride ions and let them crash together. Instead chemists build a cycle of steps they *can* measure, and Hess’s law fills in the missing one.

In this lesson you will build Born–Haber cycles, calculate lattice enthalpies, and use them to explain how ionic compounds dissolve.

## The steps in a Born–Haber cycle

:::definition Lattice formation enthalpy
The enthalpy change when one mole of an ionic solid forms from its gaseous ions, for example $\ce{Na+(g) + Cl-(g) -> NaCl(s)}$. It is always exothermic. The **lattice dissociation enthalpy** is the reverse, and is endothermic.
:::

To get from the elements to the gaseous ions you need:

| Step | Example for NaCl | Sign |
|---|---|---|
| Enthalpy of atomisation of the metal | $\ce{Na(s) -> Na(g)}$ | endothermic |
| First ionisation energy | $\ce{Na(g) -> Na+(g) + e-}$ | endothermic |
| Enthalpy of atomisation of the non-metal | $\ce{1/2Cl2(g) -> Cl(g)}$ | endothermic |
| First electron affinity | $\ce{Cl(g) + e- -> Cl-(g)}$ | exothermic |

The enthalpy of atomisation always makes **one mole of gaseous atoms**, so for chlorine it is half a mole of $\ce{Cl2}$. A **second electron affinity**, such as $\ce{O-(g) + e- -> O^2-(g)}$, is endothermic, because the electron is pushed onto an ion that is already negative.

## Calculating a lattice enthalpy

By Hess’s law, going from the elements straight to the solid gives the same enthalpy change as going round the cycle through the gaseous ions:

$$\Delta_f H = \text{(sum of the steps to the gaseous ions)} + \Delta_{LE} H$$

:::example The lattice enthalpy of sodium chloride
$\Delta_f H(\ce{NaCl}) = -411$, atomisation of Na $= +107$, first ionisation energy of Na $= +496$, atomisation of Cl $= +122$ and electron affinity of Cl $= -349$, all in kJ mol⁻¹. Find the lattice formation enthalpy.
@step Add the steps from the elements to the gaseous ions: $107 + 496 + 122 - 349 = +376$ kJ mol⁻¹.
@step Rearrange: $\Delta_{LE} H = \Delta_f H - 376 = -411 - 376$.
@answer $-787$ kJ mol⁻¹
:::

:::check cx-born-haber 2
:::

:::mistake Count every atom and every electron
For $\ce{MgCl2}$ you need **two** chlorine atomisations and **two** electron affinities, and magnesium needs its first **and** second ionisation energies to become $\ce{Mg^2+}$. Missing a doubled step is the most common error.
:::

:::

## What makes a lattice enthalpy large?

The lattice enthalpy is more exothermic when the ions are **smaller** and **more highly charged**, because they attract each other more strongly. So $\ce{MgO}$ (2+ and 2− ions) has a far larger lattice enthalpy than $\ce{NaCl}$, and $\ce{NaF}$ is larger than $\ce{NaI}$.

A **perfect ionic model** calculates the lattice enthalpy assuming the ions are perfect spheres. When the Born–Haber value is much more exothermic than the model, the bonding has some **covalent character**: a small, highly charged cation has distorted the anion’s electron cloud.

## Dissolving ionic compounds

When an ionic solid dissolves, the lattice is broken up and each ion is surrounded by water molecules. So:

$$\Delta_{sol} H = \Delta_{LD} H + \Delta_{hyd} H(\text{cation}) + \Delta_{hyd} H(\text{anion})$$

The **enthalpy of hydration** is the enthalpy change when one mole of gaseous ions dissolves to form aqueous ions. It is exothermic, and larger for smaller, more highly charged ions.

:::example The enthalpy of solution of sodium chloride
Lattice dissociation enthalpy $= +787$; hydration of $\ce{Na+} = -406$; hydration of $\ce{Cl-} = -378$ (kJ mol⁻¹). Find $\Delta_{sol} H$.
@step $\Delta_{sol} H = 787 + (-406) + (-378)$
@answer $+3$ kJ mol⁻¹, slightly endothermic
:::

:::check cx-lattice-enthalpy 3

:::summary
- Lattice formation enthalpy: gaseous ions → one mole of solid (exothermic).
- A Born–Haber cycle links it to atomisation, ionisation energies and electron affinities.
- Smaller, more highly charged ions give larger lattice and hydration enthalpies.
- A Born–Haber value larger than the perfect ionic model shows covalent character.
- $\Delta_{sol} H = \Delta_{LD} H + \text{hydration enthalpies of the ions}$.
:::
