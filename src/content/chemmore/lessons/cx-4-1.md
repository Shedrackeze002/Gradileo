Every breath you take is about four-fifths nitrogen, a gas your body does nothing with. The oxygen you need is only a fifth of the air, and for most of the Earth's history there was none of it at all. The air around you is the result of volcanoes, oceans and, above all, living things, working for more than four billion years.

In this lesson you will state what the air is made of and use the figures in calculations, and explain how the early atmosphere formed and how it changed into the one we breathe.

## The air today

:::definition Atmosphere
The **atmosphere** is the layer of gases around the Earth. For about the last 200 million years its composition has stayed much the same.
:::

| Gas | Percentage of dry air, by volume |
|---|---|
| Nitrogen, $\ce{N2}$ | about 78% (roughly four-fifths) |
| Oxygen, $\ce{O2}$ | about 21% (roughly one-fifth) |
| Argon, $\ce{Ar}$ | about 0.9% |
| Carbon dioxide, $\ce{CO2}$ | about 0.04% |

The air also holds water vapour, in an amount that changes with the weather, and tiny amounts of other noble gases.

:::example Oxygen in a room
A room holds $50\ \text{m}^3$ of dry air. If 21% of the air is oxygen, what volume of oxygen is in the room?
@step 21% means 21 out of every 100.
@step Volume of oxygen $= \dfrac{21}{100} \times 50$.
@answer $10.5\ \text{m}^3$
:::

## Measuring the oxygen in air

Hot copper reacts with oxygen and with nothing else in the air: copper + oxygen → copper(II) oxide. If a measured volume of air is pushed back and forth over hot copper, the oxygen is used up and the volume of gas falls. The fall in volume is the volume of oxygen.

:::example Finding the percentage of oxygen
$100\ \text{cm}^3$ of air is passed over hot copper until the volume stops changing. $79\ \text{cm}^3$ of gas is left, measured at room temperature. What percentage of the air was oxygen?
@step Volume of oxygen $= 100 - 79 = 21\ \text{cm}^3$.
@step Percentage $= \dfrac{21}{100} \times 100$.
@answer 21%
:::

:::tip Let the gas cool first
A hot gas takes up more room than a cool one. The final volume must be read at the same temperature as the first, or the answer will be too low.
:::

## The early atmosphere

The Earth is about 4.6 billion years old, and nobody was there to sample its first atmosphere. Scientists have several theories, and the evidence for any of them is limited. The most widely accepted one goes like this.

1. **Volcanoes** were intensely active for the first billion years. They released the gases that formed the early atmosphere: mostly **carbon dioxide**, with water vapour and nitrogen, and small amounts of methane and ammonia. There was little or no oxygen. The atmospheres of Mars and Venus are like this today.
2. As the Earth cooled, the **water vapour condensed** and fell as rain, which collected to form the oceans.
3. **Carbon dioxide dissolved** in the oceans. There it formed carbonates, which were precipitated as sediments.

## How oxygen arrived

About 2.7 billion years ago, algae evolved that could photosynthesise.

:::definition Photosynthesis
**Photosynthesis** is the process by which algae and plants use the energy of light to make glucose from carbon dioxide and water. Oxygen is given out.
:::

carbon dioxide + water → glucose + oxygen

$\ce{6CO2 + 6H2O -> C6H12O6 + 6O2}$

Over the next billion years plants evolved, and the oxygen they released built up until animals could evolve to breathe it.

## Where the carbon dioxide went

The carbon dioxide of the early atmosphere fell to a tiny fraction of what it had been. It was removed in three ways.

| Where the carbon went | How |
|---|---|
| Algae and plants | Photosynthesis took in carbon dioxide |
| Sedimentary rocks, such as limestone | Dissolved carbon dioxide formed carbonates; sea creatures built shells and skeletons from them, and these settled as sediments |
| Fossil fuels | The remains of living things were buried before they could decay: trees in swamps became **coal**, and plankton in the mud of the sea bed became **crude oil and natural gas** |

:::mistake Volcanoes did not make the oxygen
It is easy to credit volcanoes with the whole atmosphere. They supplied the carbon dioxide, the water vapour and the nitrogen. The oxygen came later, from living things.
:::

:::summary
- Dry air is about **78% nitrogen** and **21% oxygen**, with about 0.9% argon and 0.04% carbon dioxide.
- Heating copper in a measured volume of air removes the oxygen: the fall in volume is the volume of oxygen.
- The **early atmosphere** came from volcanoes and was mostly carbon dioxide, with water vapour and nitrogen and little or no oxygen.
- Water vapour condensed to form the **oceans**, and carbon dioxide dissolved in them.
- **Photosynthesis** by algae and plants released oxygen and took in carbon dioxide.
- Carbon dioxide was also locked up in **sedimentary rocks** and **fossil fuels**.
:::
