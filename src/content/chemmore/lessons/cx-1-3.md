The blue crystals of copper(II) sulfate, the Epsom salts in a bath, and the zinc compound in a sun cream are all salts. A chemist who wants one of them does not dig it up: they choose two substances that react to make exactly that salt, and then separate it, pure and dry, from everything else in the mixture.

In this lesson you will choose the acid and the other reactant that make a named salt, predict what else forms, write the balanced equation, and follow each step of the method that gives pure, dry crystals.

## Choosing the reactants

:::definition Salt
A **salt** is an ionic compound made when the hydrogen of an acid is replaced by a metal. Its name has two parts: the first comes from the metal, and the second comes from the acid.
:::

| Acid | Formula | Salts it makes |
|---|---|---|
| Hydrochloric acid | $\ce{HCl}$ | chlorides |
| Sulfuric acid | $\ce{H2SO4}$ | sulfates |
| Nitric acid | $\ce{HNO3}$ | nitrates |

Four kinds of substance react with an acid to make a salt:

- metal + acid → salt + **hydrogen**
- metal oxide + acid → salt + **water**
- metal hydroxide + acid → salt + **water**
- metal carbonate + acid → salt + **water + carbon dioxide**

So zinc sulfate can be made from sulfuric acid and zinc, zinc oxide, zinc hydroxide or zinc carbonate. The metal part of the name tells you which metal the other reactant must contain, and the second part tells you the acid.

:::example Reactants for magnesium nitrate
Which acid and which base could be used to make magnesium nitrate?
@step "Nitrate" means the acid is nitric acid.
@step "Magnesium" must come from the other reactant: magnesium oxide, magnesium hydroxide or magnesium carbonate.
@answer Nitric acid with magnesium oxide (or magnesium hydroxide, or magnesium carbonate).
:::

:::mistake Not every metal reacts with a dilute acid
Only a metal above hydrogen in the reactivity series reacts with a dilute acid. Copper is below hydrogen, so copper metal will not make copper(II) sulfate with dilute sulfuric acid. Use copper(II) oxide or copper(II) carbonate instead: they are bases, and bases always neutralise acids.
:::

## The balanced equation

Work out the salt's formula from the charges of its ions, then balance. Magnesium, zinc and copper(II) ions are all 2+. Chloride and nitrate ions are 1−, and sulfate ions are 2−.

:::example Zinc oxide and hydrochloric acid
Write the balanced symbol equation for zinc oxide reacting with hydrochloric acid.
@step The salt is zinc chloride. One $\ce{Zn^2+}$ needs two $\ce{Cl-}$: $\ce{ZnCl2}$.
@step Unbalanced: $\ce{ZnO + HCl -> ZnCl2 + H2O}$. There are 2 Cl and 2 H on the right but 1 of each on the left.
@step Put a 2 in front of $\ce{HCl}$.
@answer $\ce{ZnO + 2HCl -> ZnCl2 + H2O}$
:::

## Making pure, dry crystals

The method below works when the other reactant is a solid that does **not** dissolve in water, such as a metal oxide or carbonate. Here it is for copper(II) sulfate, from copper(II) oxide and dilute sulfuric acid.

1. **Warm the acid gently.** The reaction goes faster in a warm acid.
2. **Add the solid a little at a time, stirring, until some is left over.** The solid is now **in excess**: all of the acid has reacted. With a carbonate, you also see the fizzing stop.
3. **Filter.** The unreacted solid stays in the filter paper as the **residue**. The salt solution passes through as the **filtrate**.
4. **Heat the filtrate in an evaporating basin until crystals just start to form.** Some of the water evaporates, and the solution becomes saturated.
5. **Leave it to cool.** Crystals grow slowly as the solution cools. Pour off any liquid and pat the crystals dry between filter papers.

:::definition Excess
A reactant is **in excess** when there is more of it than the reaction can use. Adding the insoluble solid in excess makes sure that no acid is left, and the spare solid is easy to filter off.
:::

:::example What is in the beaker?
In step 2 a student stops adding copper(II) oxide while the liquid is still acidic. What will be wrong with the crystals?
@step With no excess of solid, some acid has not reacted.
@step The acid is dissolved, so it passes through the filter paper with the salt.
@answer The crystals will be contaminated with sulfuric acid, so they are not pure.
:::

:::note When the base dissolves
Sodium and potassium hydroxides dissolve in water, so an excess cannot be filtered off. For their salts, the alkali is added from a burette until the acid is exactly neutralised, a titration, and the solution is then evaporated in the same way.
:::

:::tip Why not boil the solution dry?
Heating until every drop has gone gives a powder, not crystals, and the hot solid may spit out of the basin or break down. Evaporating only part of the water and cooling slowly gives larger, well-shaped crystals.
:::

:::summary
- A salt's name comes from the **metal** and the **acid**: hydrochloric acid makes chlorides, sulfuric acid sulfates and nitric acid nitrates.
- An acid makes a salt with a metal (plus hydrogen), a metal oxide or hydroxide (plus water), or a metal carbonate (plus water and carbon dioxide).
- A metal below hydrogen, such as copper, does not react with dilute acids; use its oxide or carbonate.
- Method for a soluble salt from an insoluble solid: warm the acid, add the solid **in excess**, **filter** off the spare solid, **evaporate** some water, then **cool** to crystallise and dry the crystals.
- The excess makes sure all the acid is used up; filtering removes the excess; slow cooling gives good crystals.
:::
