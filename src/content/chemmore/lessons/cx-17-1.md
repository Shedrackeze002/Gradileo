Hydrogen is the lightest element and oxygen the most abundant in the Earth's crust, and together they make water. Oxygen also combines with almost every other element, and the oxides it forms fall into four families that behave in different ways with acids and alkalis.

In this lesson you will describe the preparation, properties and uses of hydrogen and oxygen, calculate the volume of hydrogen a metal gives with acid, and classify oxides as acidic, basic, amphoteric or neutral.

## Hydrogen

:::definition Reducing agent
A **reducing agent** removes oxygen from a substance (or gives it electrons). Hydrogen is a reducing agent: it takes oxygen from the oxides of metals low in the reactivity series.
:::

Hydrogen is made in the laboratory from zinc and dilute hydrochloric acid, and industrially from natural gas and steam or by electrolysis. It is the least dense gas, burns with a pop and forms water. A mixture of hydrogen and air explodes when lit, so hydrogen is always tested for purity before it is lit at a jet. Passed over hot copper(II) oxide, it turns the black oxide pink-brown as copper forms:

$$\ce{CuO + H2 -> Cu + H2O}$$

Hydrogen is used to make ammonia, to harden vegetable oils into margarine, and as a clean fuel.

:::example Hydrogen from magnesium
What volume of hydrogen, at room temperature and pressure, is given off when 2.4 g of magnesium (Ar = 24) reacts with excess dilute hydrochloric acid? One mole of gas occupies 24 dm³. $\ce{Mg + 2HCl -> MgCl2 + H2}$
@step Amount of magnesium = 2.4 ÷ 24 = 0.10 mol, which gives 0.10 mol of hydrogen.
@answer Volume = 0.10 × 24 = 2.4 dm³
:::

## Oxygen

Oxygen is made in the laboratory by decomposing hydrogen peroxide with manganese(IV) oxide as a catalyst, and industrially by the fractional distillation of liquid air. It relights a glowing splint and supports burning. It is used in hospitals, in steel making, and in oxy-acetylene flames for welding.

## Classes of oxides

| Class | Behaviour | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Acidic | react with alkalis; many dissolve to form acids | $\ce{CO2}$, $\ce{SO2}$, $\ce{SO3}$, $\ce{NO2}$, $\ce{P4O10}$ |
| Basic | react with acids to form a salt and water | $\ce{Na2O}$, $\ce{CaO}$, $\ce{MgO}$, $\ce{CuO}$ |
| Amphoteric | react with both acids and alkalis | $\ce{ZnO}$, $\ce{Al2O3}$, $\ce{PbO}$ |
| Neutral | react with neither | $\ce{CO}$, $\ce{NO}$, $\ce{N2O}$, $\ce{H2O}$ |

Most non-metal oxides are acidic and most metal oxides are basic. Zinc oxide shows both sides:

$$\ce{ZnO + 2HCl -> ZnCl2 + H2O} \qquad \ce{ZnO + 2NaOH -> Na2ZnO2 + H2O}$$

A **peroxide**, such as $\ce{Na2O2}$, contains the $\ce{O2^2-}$ ion and gives hydrogen peroxide with dilute acid.

:::summary
- Hydrogen: zinc and dilute acid in the laboratory; burns with a pop; reduces hot copper(II) oxide.
- Oxygen: hydrogen peroxide and MnO₂ in the laboratory, liquid air in industry; relights a glowing splint.
- Volume of gas = amount in mol × 24 dm³ at room temperature and pressure.
- Oxides are acidic, basic, amphoteric (ZnO, Al₂O₃, PbO) or neutral (CO, NO, N₂O).
:::
