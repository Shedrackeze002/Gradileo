Every titration result depends on one solution whose concentration is known exactly. If that solution is wrong, every answer worked out from it is wrong too. So chemists take great care over how they make a standard solution, and over how they dilute a concentrated stock solution to the strength they need.

In this lesson you will calculate the mass of solid needed for a standard solution, convert between mol dm⁻³ and g dm⁻³, and dilute stock solutions, including concentrated acids given by percentage and density.

## Standard solutions

:::definition Standard solution
A **standard solution** is a solution whose concentration is known accurately. A **primary standard** is a pure, stable solid that can be weighed exactly to make one, such as anhydrous sodium carbonate.
:::

The key relationships are:

$$n = c \times V \qquad m = n \times M \qquad \text{mass concentration (g dm}^{-3}) = c \times M$$

where $n$ is the amount in mol, $c$ the concentration in mol dm⁻³, $V$ the volume in dm³ and $M$ the molar mass.

:::example Making a standard solution of sodium carbonate
What mass of anhydrous sodium carbonate (M = 106 g mol⁻¹) is needed to make 250 cm³ of a 0.100 mol dm⁻³ solution?
@step $n = 0.100 \times \dfrac{250}{1000} = 0.0250$ mol
@step $m = 0.0250 \times 106 = 2.65$ g
@answer 2.65 g
:::

To make it, weigh the solid accurately, dissolve it in a little distilled water in a beaker, pour the solution into a 250 cm³ volumetric flask with all the rinsings of the beaker, make it up to the mark, and shake. Sodium hydroxide is not a primary standard, because it absorbs water and carbon dioxide from the air; its exact concentration is found by titration against a primary standard, which makes it a **secondary standard**.

## Diluting a stock solution

Diluting with water does not change the amount of solute, so

$$c_1V_1 = c_2V_2$$

To make 250 cm³ of 0.20 mol dm⁻³ acid from 2.0 mol dm⁻³ acid, $V_1 = \dfrac{0.20 \times 250}{2.0} = 25$ cm³ of the stock is made up to 250 cm³.

## Concentrated reagents

Bottles of concentrated acid are labelled with their percentage by mass and density. One cubic decimetre (1000 cm³) of the acid has a mass of 1000 × density grams, and the given percentage of that is the acid itself:

$$c = \frac{1000 \times \rho \times \dfrac{\%}{100}}{M}$$

For example, concentrated sulfuric acid that is 98% $\ce{H2SO4}$ with a density of 1.84 g cm⁻³ has $c = \dfrac{1000 \times 1.84 \times 0.98}{98} = 18.4$ mol dm⁻³.

:::mistake Add acid to water
When diluting concentrated sulfuric acid, always add the acid slowly to the water, never water to the acid.
:::

:::summary
- $n = cV$ (with $V$ in dm³) and $m = nM$; g dm⁻³ = mol dm⁻³ × molar mass.
- A primary standard is pure, stable and weighed exactly; a secondary standard is found by titration.
- Dilution keeps the amount of solute fixed: $c_1V_1 = c_2V_2$.
- A concentrated reagent's concentration is $1000\rho \times \%/100 \div M$ mol dm⁻³.
:::
