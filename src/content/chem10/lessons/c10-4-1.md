Strike a match and, in a moment, its head has flared into flame, smoke and a little ash. It looks as if the match head has been destroyed, but it has not: every atom that was in it is still there, now joined to new partners in new substances. Chemists keep track of what reacts and what forms with equations, and the simplest kind is written in words.

In this lesson you will name the reactants and products of a reaction and write its word equation, then use the law of conservation of mass to find a missing mass, including in experiments where a gas escapes or is taken in from the air.

## Reactants and products

:::definition Chemical reaction
In a **chemical reaction**, substances change into new substances with different properties. The atoms are rearranged into new combinations; no atoms are made and none are destroyed.
:::

You met the signs of a chemical change in lesson 1.4: a gas given off (fizzing), a change of colour, a solid appearing in a solution, or energy given out or taken in, so that the mixture gets hot or cold, or glows.

:::definition Reactants and products
The **reactants** are the substances you start with. They are used up as the reaction goes on. The **products** are the new substances that form.
:::

:::definition Word equation
A **word equation** names the reactants on the left of an arrow and the products on the right. A + sign separates two substances on the same side, and the arrow reads "react to form":

reactants → products
:::

When magnesium ribbon burns in air, it reacts with oxygen and leaves a white powder of magnesium oxide. Magnesium and oxygen are used up, and magnesium oxide is new:

magnesium + oxygen → magnesium oxide

To write a word equation from a description:

1. **Find the reactants:** the substances that are used up.
2. **Find the products:** the new substances that form.
3. **Leave out** anything that is neither used up nor formed, such as a catalyst, and leave out energy: heat and light are not substances.
4. **Write** reactants → products, with a + between substances on the same side.

:::example Zinc in acid
Zinc granules are dropped into dilute hydrochloric acid. They fizz as hydrogen gas bubbles off, and zinc chloride forms in the solution. Write the word equation.
@step Used up: zinc and hydrochloric acid. These are the reactants, on the left.
@step New: zinc chloride and hydrogen. These are the products, on the right.
@answer zinc + hydrochloric acid → zinc chloride + hydrogen
:::

:::check c10-word-equation 1
:::

:::example Heating copper(II) carbonate
Green copper(II) carbonate powder is heated in a test tube. It turns black as it breaks down into copper(II) oxide and carbon dioxide. Write the word equation.
@step There is only one reactant: copper(II) carbonate. Heating supplies energy, but heat is not a substance, so it is not written as a reactant.
@step The products are copper(II) oxide and carbon dioxide.
@answer copper(II) carbonate → copper(II) oxide + carbon dioxide
:::

:::mistake A catalyst is not a reactant
Hydrogen peroxide breaks down into water and oxygen much faster when a little manganese(IV) oxide is added. The manganese(IV) oxide is still there, unchanged, at the end: it is a **catalyst** (lesson 4.5), so it is neither a reactant nor a product. The word equation is hydrogen peroxide → water + oxygen. Some books write the catalyst's name above the arrow, but never with a + among the reactants.
:::

:::check c10-word-equation 2
:::

## Identifying the products

A description does not always name every product. Often a gas is identified by a simple laboratory test, and so is water.

| Substance | Test | Result if present |
|---|---|---|
| hydrogen | hold a lighted splint at the mouth of the test tube | the gas burns with a squeaky pop |
| oxygen | put a glowing splint into the gas | the splint relights |
| carbon dioxide | bubble the gas through limewater | the limewater turns milky |
| water | add the liquid to white anhydrous copper(II) sulfate | the solid turns blue |

:::example Marble chips in acid
Marble chips, which are calcium carbonate, fizz in dilute hydrochloric acid, making calcium chloride and water. The gas given off turns limewater milky. Write the word equation.
@step A gas that turns limewater milky is carbon dioxide.
@step Reactants: calcium carbonate and hydrochloric acid. Products: calcium chloride, water and carbon dioxide.
@answer calcium carbonate + hydrochloric acid → calcium chloride + water + carbon dioxide
:::

:::example A water plant in light
A water plant is placed in bright light. It turns carbon dioxide and water into glucose, and bubbles of a gas rise from its leaves. The gas relights a glowing splint. Write the word equation for this reaction, photosynthesis.
@step A gas that relights a glowing splint is oxygen.
@step Reactants: carbon dioxide and water. Products: glucose and oxygen. The light supplies energy but is not a substance.
@answer carbon dioxide + water → glucose + oxygen
:::

:::check c10-word-equation 3
:::

## The law of conservation of mass

In a reaction, the atoms of the reactants are only rearranged. When hydrogen burns, two hydrogen molecules and one oxygen molecule become two water molecules: there are 4 hydrogen atoms and 2 oxygen atoms before the reaction and exactly the same atoms after it. Since the atoms are the same, so is their total mass.

:::definition Law of conservation of mass
In a chemical reaction, mass is neither created nor destroyed. The total mass of the products equals the total mass of the reactants.
:::

$$\text{total mass of reactants} = \text{total mass of products}$$

:::example A missing product
In a sealed tube, $5.60\ \text{g}$ of iron and $3.21\ \text{g}$ of sulfur are heated and react completely, forming iron(II) sulfide. What mass of iron(II) sulfide forms?
@step Mass of reactants = mass of products: $5.60 + 3.21 = m$
@answer $m = 8.81\ \text{g}$ of iron(II) sulfide.
:::

:::example A missing reactant
Calcium carbonate is heated strongly in a closed container until it has all broken down into $2.80\ \text{g}$ of calcium oxide and $2.20\ \text{g}$ of carbon dioxide. What mass of calcium carbonate was heated?
@step Mass of reactants = mass of products: $m = 2.80 + 2.20$
@answer $m = 5.00\ \text{g}$ of calcium carbonate.
:::

:::check c10-conservation-mass 1
:::

The law counts only what actually reacts. If one reactant is in **excess**, some of it is left over at the end, and that leftover part is not in the product.

:::example One reactant left over
In a sealed flask, $2.40\ \text{g}$ of magnesium burns in $2.00\ \text{g}$ of oxygen until all of the magnesium is used up. $0.42\ \text{g}$ of oxygen is left over. What mass of magnesium oxide forms?
@step Oxygen that reacted: $2.00 - 0.42 = 1.58\ \text{g}$
@step Magnesium oxide: $2.40 + 1.58 = 3.98\ \text{g}$
@answer $3.98\ \text{g}$ of magnesium oxide, with $0.42\ \text{g}$ of oxygen still in the flask.
:::

:::

## Open and closed containers

:::definition Closed and open systems
In a **closed system**, such as a sealed flask, no substance can get in or out, so the total mass never changes. In an **open system**, such as an open beaker, a gas can escape into the air or be taken in from it.
:::

Mass is conserved in every reaction, but in an open container a balance does not always show it:

- **The mass falls** when a product is a gas that escapes. Marble chips in acid give off carbon dioxide; a carbonate heated in an open tube gives off carbon dioxide.
- **The mass rises** when a reactant is a gas from the air. A metal heated in air combines with oxygen, and the oxide that forms holds the metal's mass plus the oxygen's.

:::example Marble chips on a balance
A flask of dilute hydrochloric acid stands open on a balance. Marble chips are added, and the reading is $85.40\ \text{g}$. When the fizzing stops, it reads $84.52\ \text{g}$. What mass of carbon dioxide escaped, and why did the mass fall?
@step The mass fell by $85.40 - 84.52 = 0.88\ \text{g}$.
@step No atoms were destroyed: $0.88\ \text{g}$ of carbon dioxide left the flask as a gas, so it was no longer on the balance.
@answer $0.88\ \text{g}$ of carbon dioxide escaped. In a sealed flask the reading would have stayed at $85.40\ \text{g}$.
:::

:::example Heating magnesium in a crucible
A crucible, its lid and some magnesium ribbon have a mass of $25.62\ \text{g}$. The magnesium is heated strongly, with the lid lifted now and then to let air in, until it has all turned into magnesium oxide. The mass is then $25.86\ \text{g}$. What mass of oxygen combined with the magnesium?
@step The mass rose by $25.86 - 25.62 = 0.24\ \text{g}$.
@step The only thing that could have come in is oxygen from the air, which combined with the magnesium.
@answer $0.24\ \text{g}$ of oxygen. The magnesium oxide holds the mass of the magnesium plus the mass of that oxygen.
:::

:::mistake "The mass went down, so mass was destroyed"
When the reading on a balance falls, it is tempting to say the reaction destroyed some mass, or that gases have no mass. Neither is true. A gas does have mass; it has simply escaped from the container. Repeat the reaction in a sealed flask and the reading does not change at all.
:::

:::check c10-conservation-mass 3
:::

:::summary
- In a reaction, the **reactants** are used up and the **products** form. Atoms are rearranged; none are made or destroyed.
- A **word equation** puts the reactants on the left and the products on the right: reactants → products. Leave out catalysts and energy.
- Tests: hydrogen burns with a squeaky pop, oxygen relights a glowing splint, carbon dioxide turns limewater milky, and water turns white anhydrous copper(II) sulfate blue.
- **Conservation of mass:** total mass of reactants = total mass of products. A reactant left over in excess is not part of the product.
- In an **open** container, the mass falls when a gas escapes and rises when a gas from the air is taken in. In a **closed** container it never changes.
:::
