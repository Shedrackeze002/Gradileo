A single drop of water, with a mass of about $0.05\ \text{g}$, holds about $1.7 \times 10^{21}$ water molecules. If you counted them at one a second, you would need more than 50 trillion years: thousands of times longer than the universe has existed. Atoms and molecules are far too small and too many to count one by one, so chemists count them in huge, fixed bundles called **moles**.

In this lesson you will use the mole and Avogadro's number to convert between moles and numbers of particles, and work out the molar mass of an element or compound from the molar masses of its atoms.

## Counting with the mole

People already count in bundles. A dozen eggs is 12 eggs, and a ream of paper is 500 sheets. Chemists need a much bigger bundle, because particles are so small.

| Bundle | How many it holds |
|---|---|
| a dozen | $12$ |
| a ream | $500$ |
| a mole | $6.02 \times 10^{23}$ |

:::definition Mole
The **mole** (symbol mol) is the unit chemists use to measure the amount of a substance. One mole of anything holds $6.02 \times 10^{23}$ particles.
:::

:::definition Avogadro's number
**Avogadro's number** is $6.02 \times 10^{23}$, the number of particles in one mole. It is named after the Italian scientist Amedeo Avogadro.
:::

The particles depend on the substance. Iron and helium are made of **atoms**. Water and carbon dioxide are made of **molecules**. An ionic compound such as sodium chloride is counted in **formula units**, the smallest group of ions its formula shows (lesson 3.2). One mole of each holds $6.02 \times 10^{23}$ of its particles.

To change moles into particles, multiply by Avogadro's number. To change particles into moles, divide by it:

$$N = n \times 6.02 \times 10^{23} \qquad n = \dfrac{N}{6.02 \times 10^{23}}$$

Here $n$ is the amount in moles and $N$ is the number of particles. The answers are huge, so write them in scientific notation, with one digit before the decimal point. To type $1.51 \times 10^{24}$ as an answer, enter `1.51e24` or `1.51*10^24`.

:::example Moles to molecules
How many molecules are in $2.5\ \text{mol}$ of carbon dioxide, $\ce{CO2}$? Give the answer in scientific notation to three significant figures.
@step Multiply the moles by Avogadro's number: $N = 2.5 \times 6.02 \times 10^{23}$.
@step $2.5 \times 6.02 = 15.05$, so $N = 15.05 \times 10^{23}$.
@step Move the decimal point one place left and add one to the power of ten: $15.05 \times 10^{23} = 1.505 \times 10^{24}$.
@answer $1.51 \times 10^{24}$ molecules
:::

:::example Atoms to moles
A piece of iron contains $9.03 \times 10^{23}$ iron atoms. How many moles of iron is that, to the nearest hundredth?
@step Divide the number of atoms by Avogadro's number: $n = \dfrac{9.03 \times 10^{23}}{6.02 \times 10^{23}}$.
@step The powers of ten are the same, so they cancel: $n = \dfrac{9.03}{6.02} = 1.5$.
@answer $1.50\ \text{mol}$
:::

:::tip Powers of ten on a calculator
Enter $6.02 \times 10^{23}$ with the calculator's exponent key, often marked EXP, EE or $\times 10^x$: type `6.02`, the exponent key, then `23`. If you type `÷ 6.02 × 10^23`, the calculator divides by 6.02 and then multiplies by $10^{23}$, which is wrong. Use the exponent key, or put brackets around the whole number.
:::

## Atoms inside molecules

A molecule is made of atoms, so a mole of molecules holds even more atoms. One molecule of water, $\ce{H2O}$, has 2 hydrogen atoms and 1 oxygen atom. So one mole of water molecules holds 2 mol of hydrogen atoms and 1 mol of oxygen atoms: 3 mol of atoms in all.

:::example Hydrogen atoms in water
How many hydrogen atoms are in $2\ \text{mol}$ of water, $\ce{H2O}$? Give the answer in scientific notation to three significant figures.
@step Each molecule of $\ce{H2O}$ has 2 hydrogen atoms, so 2 mol of water holds $2 \times 2 = 4\ \text{mol}$ of hydrogen atoms.
@step $N = 4 \times 6.02 \times 10^{23} = 24.08 \times 10^{23} = 2.408 \times 10^{24}$
@answer $2.41 \times 10^{24}$ hydrogen atoms
:::

:::mistake Molecules or atoms?
One mole of oxygen gas, $\ce{O2}$, is $6.02 \times 10^{23}$ molecules, but each molecule has 2 atoms, so it is $2 \times 6.02 \times 10^{23} = 1.20 \times 10^{24}$ oxygen atoms. Read the question carefully: if it asks for atoms, multiply by the number of those atoms in one molecule.
:::

## Molar mass

A dozen eggs and a dozen watermelons hold the same number of things but have very different masses. Moles are the same: one mole of every substance holds $6.02 \times 10^{23}$ particles, but the mass of a mole depends on how heavy the particles are.

:::definition Molar mass
The **molar mass**, $M$, of a substance is the mass of one mole of it, in grams per mole ($\text{g/mol}$). The molar mass of an element is its relative atomic mass (lesson 2.2) in grams per mole. For a compound, add up the molar masses of all the atoms in its formula.
:::

The periodic table gives each element's molar mass. The questions in this course give the ones they need, to one decimal place, such as H $1.0\ \text{g/mol}$, C $12.0\ \text{g/mol}$ and O $16.0\ \text{g/mol}$. To find a compound's molar mass:

1. **Count** the atoms of each element in the formula. A small number after a symbol counts the atom before it.
2. **Multiply** each count by that element's molar mass.
3. **Add** the results.

:::example Water
What is the molar mass of water, $\ce{H2O}$, to the nearest tenth? Use these molar masses: H $1.0\ \text{g/mol}$ and O $16.0\ \text{g/mol}$.
@step One molecule has 2 H and 1 O.
@step $M(\ce{H2O}) = 2 \times 1.0 + 16.0 = 2.0 + 16.0$
@answer $18.0\ \text{g/mol}$
:::

:::example Carbon dioxide
What is the molar mass of carbon dioxide, $\ce{CO2}$, to the nearest tenth? Use these molar masses: C $12.0\ \text{g/mol}$ and O $16.0\ \text{g/mol}$.
@step One molecule has 1 C and 2 O.
@step $M(\ce{CO2}) = 12.0 + 2 \times 16.0 = 12.0 + 32.0$
@answer $44.0\ \text{g/mol}$
:::

:::check c10-molar-mass 1
:::

:::example Sulfuric acid
What is the molar mass of sulfuric acid, $\ce{H2SO4}$, to the nearest tenth? Use these molar masses: H $1.0\ \text{g/mol}$, S $32.1\ \text{g/mol}$ and O $16.0\ \text{g/mol}$.
@step One molecule has 2 H, 1 S and 4 O.
@step $M = 2 \times 1.0 + 32.1 + 4 \times 16.0 = 2.0 + 32.1 + 64.0$
@answer $98.1\ \text{g/mol}$
:::

## Brackets in formulas

Some formulas have a group of atoms in brackets, such as the hydroxide ion in calcium hydroxide, $\ce{Ca(OH)2}$. The number after the bracket multiplies **everything** inside it. Count the atoms first, then add up their masses as before.

:::example Calcium hydroxide
What is the molar mass of calcium hydroxide, $\ce{Ca(OH)2}$, to the nearest tenth? Use these molar masses: Ca $40.1\ \text{g/mol}$, O $16.0\ \text{g/mol}$ and H $1.0\ \text{g/mol}$.
@step The 2 after the bracket multiplies the O and the H: one formula unit has 1 Ca, $2 \times 1 = 2$ O and $2 \times 1 = 2$ H.
@step $M = 40.1 + 2 \times 16.0 + 2 \times 1.0 = 40.1 + 32.0 + 2.0$
@answer $74.1\ \text{g/mol}$
:::

:::example Ammonium sulfate
What is the molar mass of ammonium sulfate, $\ce{(NH4)2SO4}$, a fertilizer, to the nearest tenth? Use these molar masses: N $14.0\ \text{g/mol}$, H $1.0\ \text{g/mol}$, S $32.1\ \text{g/mol}$ and O $16.0\ \text{g/mol}$.
@step The 2 after the bracket multiplies the N and the H: $2 \times 1 = 2$ N and $2 \times 4 = 8$ H. Outside the bracket there are 1 S and 4 O.
@step $M = 2 \times 14.0 + 8 \times 1.0 + 32.1 + 4 \times 16.0 = 28.0 + 8.0 + 32.1 + 64.0$
@answer $132.1\ \text{g/mol}$
:::

:::mistake The bracket multiplies everything inside
In $\ce{Ca(OH)2}$ the 2 multiplies both the oxygen and the hydrogen. Reading it as if it were $\ce{CaOH2}$, with 1 O and 2 H, gives $40.1 + 16.0 + 2.0 = 58.1\ \text{g/mol}$, which is too small.
:::

:::check c10-molar-mass 3
:::

:::summary
- The **mole** (mol) is the chemist's counting unit: one mole of anything holds $6.02 \times 10^{23}$ particles, **Avogadro's number**.
- Moles to particles: $N = n \times 6.02 \times 10^{23}$. Particles to moles: $n = \dfrac{N}{6.02 \times 10^{23}}$. Write the answers in scientific notation.
- To count atoms, multiply by the number of those atoms in one molecule: 1 mol of $\ce{H2O}$ holds 2 mol of hydrogen atoms.
- The **molar mass**, $M$, is the mass of one mole, in $\text{g/mol}$. Count the atoms in the formula, multiply each count by the element's molar mass, and add.
- A number after a bracket multiplies every atom inside it: $\ce{Ca(OH)2}$ has 1 Ca, 2 O and 2 H, so $M = 74.1\ \text{g/mol}$.
:::
