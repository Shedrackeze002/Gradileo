Melt an ice cube and you get water; freeze the water and you get the ice cube back. Burn a piece of paper and you get ash, smoke and gases, and no amount of cooling or squeezing will ever turn them back into paper. Both are changes, but they are changes of a very different kind.

In this lesson you will tell **physical changes** from **chemical changes** by the evidence they leave, sort the properties of substances into **physical** and **chemical properties**, and use one of the most useful physical properties of all, **density**, to describe and identify substances.

## Physical and chemical changes

:::definition Physical change
In a **physical change**, no new substance is made. The substance may change its state, its shape or its size, or it may dissolve or be mixed with something else, but it is still the same substance. Physical changes are usually easy to reverse.
:::

Melting, freezing, boiling, condensing, dissolving salt in water, cutting wood and bending a wire are all physical changes. Melted ice is still water, $\ce{H2O}$; the salt in salt water is still salt, and evaporating the water gets it back.

:::definition Chemical change
In a **chemical change** (a chemical reaction), one or more **new substances** are made, with properties different from those of the starting substances. Chemical changes are usually hard to reverse.
:::

Burning, rusting, cooking an egg, baking bread and milk going sour are all chemical changes. Rust is a new substance, iron oxide, which is reddish brown, flaky and not magnetic like the iron it came from.

You cannot see particles, so how can you tell that a new substance has been made? Look for evidence:

| Evidence of a chemical change | Example |
|---|---|
| a gas is given off (bubbles or fizzing), when nothing is boiling | zinc fizzes in dilute hydrochloric acid, giving hydrogen |
| a colour change | green copper(II) carbonate turns black when heated |
| a precipitate: a solid forms when two solutions are mixed | two colourless solutions give a bright yellow solid |
| energy is given out or taken in: heat, light or sound, or the mixture cools | magnesium burns with a dazzling white light |
| the change is hard to reverse | a cooked egg cannot be uncooked |

:::example Using the evidence
Green copper(II) carbonate powder is heated strongly in a test tube. It turns black, and the gas given off turns limewater cloudy. Is this a physical or a chemical change?
@step The black powder has a different colour from the green starting powder, so it is a new substance (it is copper(II) oxide, $\ce{CuO}$).
@step A gas is given off, carbon dioxide, which is also a new substance.
@step The black powder does not turn green again when it cools.
@answer A chemical change: $\ce{CuCO3 -> CuO + CO2}$. New substances have formed.
:::

Evidence can mislead you, so always come back to the real question: **is a new substance made?**

- Bubbles form when water boils, but the bubbles are water vapour, the same substance in a different state. Boiling is physical.
- A light bulb filament glows and gives out heat and light, but it is still the same metal, and it stops glowing when the bulb is switched off. Physical.
- Melting ice takes in energy, but melted ice is still water. Physical.

:::mistake Bubbles are not always a new gas
Bubbles in boiling water are steam, not a new substance, and the fog around dry ice is made of tiny droplets of water condensed from the air, not a new substance. Look for bubbles produced when substances are **mixed**, as when zinc is added to acid or vinegar to baking soda.
:::

## Physical and chemical properties

A **property** is a characteristic that helps you describe or identify a substance.

:::definition Physical and chemical properties
A **physical property** can be observed or measured without changing the substance into a new one: its colour, state, smell, melting point, boiling point, density, hardness, solubility, and whether it conducts electricity or is magnetic. A **chemical property** describes how the substance reacts to make new substances: whether it burns (is **flammable**), whether it reacts with water, air or acids, whether it breaks down when heated, and also whether it does **not** react.
:::

| Substance | Physical properties | Chemical properties |
|---|---|---|
| magnesium | silvery grey; melts at 650 °C; conducts electricity | burns in air with a bright white flame; reacts with dilute acids, giving hydrogen |
| ethanol | colourless liquid; boils at 78 °C; mixes with water | flammable: burns with a blue flame, forming carbon dioxide and water |
| gold | yellow and shiny; can be hammered into very thin sheets | does not react with air or water, so it never tarnishes |

To find a physical property you only need to look or measure. To find a chemical property you have to try a reaction, and afterwards the substance you tested has become something else.

:::example Sorting properties
Which of these are chemical properties of iron? (a) It is attracted to a magnet. (b) It rusts in damp air. (c) It melts at 1538 °C. (d) It reacts with sulfur when heated.
@step (a) and (c) can be observed or measured without turning the iron into anything new: physical properties.
@step (b) and (d) describe reactions that make new substances, rust and iron sulfide: chemical properties.
@answer (b) and (d) are chemical properties.
:::

:::mistake Not reacting is a chemical property
"Gold does not react with air" sounds like the absence of a property, but it describes how gold behaves in reactions, so it is a chemical property. Likewise, "carbon dioxide does not burn" is a chemical property of carbon dioxide.
:::

## Density

A brick is heavier than a sponge of the same size: in the same volume, the brick has more mass. That property is its **density**.

:::definition Density
The **density** of a substance is its mass per unit volume:
$$\rho = \frac{m}{V}$$
where $\rho$ (the Greek letter rho) is the density, $m$ the mass and $V$ the volume. For solids, density is usually measured in grams per cubic centimetre, $\text{g/cm}^3$; for liquids, in grams per millilitre, $\text{g/mL}$. Since $1\ \text{mL} = 1\ \text{cm}^3$, the two units are the same.
:::

Rearranging the formula gives the mass or the volume:
$$m = \rho V \qquad\qquad V = \frac{m}{\rho}$$

Every pure substance has its own density (at a given temperature), whatever the size of the sample, so density helps identify a substance:

| Substance | Density ($\text{g/cm}^3$) |
|---|---|
| ethanol | 0.79 |
| ice | 0.92 |
| water | 1.00 |
| aluminium | 2.70 |
| iron | 7.87 |
| copper | 8.96 |
| lead | 11.34 |
| gold | 19.32 |

:::example Finding a density
A block of metal has a mass of $81.0\ \text{g}$ and a volume of $30.0\ \text{cm}^3$. Find its density, and use the table to identify the metal.
@step $\rho = \dfrac{m}{V} = \dfrac{81.0\ \text{g}}{30.0\ \text{cm}^3} = 2.70\ \text{g/cm}^3$
@step The metal in the table with a density of $2.70\ \text{g/cm}^3$ is aluminium.
@answer $2.70\ \text{g/cm}^3$: the metal is aluminium.
:::

:::example Finding a mass
What is the mass of $250\ \text{mL}$ of ethanol (density $0.79\ \text{g/mL}$)?
@step $m = \rho V = 0.79 \times 250$
@answer $m = 197.5\ \text{g}$, about $198\ \text{g}$.
:::

An object with an irregular shape, such as a stone or a key, has a volume that is hard to calculate. Instead, lower it into a measuring cylinder of water: the water level rises by the volume of the object. This is called **displacement**.

:::example Volume by displacement
A measuring cylinder holds $40\ \text{mL}$ of water. When a key with a mass of $86.1\ \text{g}$ is lowered in, the water rises to $51\ \text{mL}$. What is the density of the key?
@step Volume of the key $= 51 - 40 = 11\ \text{mL} = 11\ \text{cm}^3$.
@step $\rho = \dfrac{86.1}{11} = 7.83\ \text{g/cm}^3$
@answer About $7.83\ \text{g/cm}^3$, close to the density of iron.
:::

Density also tells you whether an object floats. Water has a density of $1.00\ \text{g/cm}^3$. An object **less dense** than water floats in it; an object **denser** than water sinks. Ice, at $0.92\ \text{g/cm}^3$, floats with most of its volume under the surface; a steel nail sinks. In the same way, a liquid that does not mix with water and is less dense than water, such as oil, floats on top of it.

:::mistake Heavy is not the same as dense
A huge log is much heavier than a small pebble, yet the log floats and the pebble sinks. Floating depends on density, mass per unit volume, not on mass alone. Cutting a substance into pieces does not change its density either: half a block of wood has half the mass and half the volume.
:::

:::summary
- A **physical change** makes no new substance (changes of state, dissolving, changes of shape) and is usually easy to reverse. A **chemical change** makes new substances and is usually hard to reverse.
- Evidence of a chemical change: a gas given off when substances are mixed, a colour change, a precipitate, or energy given out or taken in. The real test is whether a new substance forms.
- **Physical properties** can be observed or measured without making a new substance (colour, melting point, density, conductivity). **Chemical properties** describe how a substance reacts, or does not react (flammability, rusting, reacting with acids).
- **Density** is mass per unit volume: $\rho = \dfrac{m}{V}$, so $m = \rho V$ and $V = \dfrac{m}{\rho}$, in $\text{g/cm}^3$ or $\text{g/mL}$. The volume of an irregular object can be found by displacement.
- Objects less dense than water ($1.00\ \text{g/cm}^3$) float in it; denser objects sink. Matching a measured density with a table identifies a substance.
:::
