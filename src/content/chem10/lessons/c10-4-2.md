A word equation says which substances react, but not how many particles of each are involved. A **symbol equation** uses formulas instead of names, and numbers in front of the formulas show how many particles take part. Because atoms are never made or destroyed, a correct symbol equation has the same atoms on both sides: it is **balanced**.

In this lesson you will count the atoms in formulas with coefficients and brackets, balance symbol equations, and add state symbols that show whether each substance is a solid, a liquid, a gas or dissolved in water.

## Counting atoms in a formula

A formula, as you wrote them in Unit 3, shows the atoms of each element in one particle of a substance.

:::definition Subscript and coefficient
A **subscript** is the small number written after a symbol. It counts the atoms of the element just before it: $\ce{H2O}$ holds 2 hydrogen atoms and 1 oxygen atom. A **coefficient** is the full-size number written in front of a formula. It counts whole particles: $\ce{3H2O}$ means three water molecules.
:::

Four rules count every atom:

1. A subscript counts the atom just **before** it. No subscript means 1.
2. A subscript after a **bracket** multiplies every atom inside the bracket: $\ce{Ca(OH)2}$ holds 2 oxygen and 2 hydrogen atoms.
3. A **coefficient** multiplies every atom in the whole formula.
4. When an element appears in more than one place, **add** the counts: $\ce{C2H5OH}$ holds $5 + 1 = 6$ hydrogen atoms.

:::example Brackets and a coefficient
How many atoms of each element are shown by $\ce{3Ca(OH)2}$?
@step One $\ce{Ca(OH)2}$ holds 1 calcium atom, and the bracket holds 1 oxygen and 1 hydrogen atom, taken 2 times: 2 oxygen and 2 hydrogen atoms.
@step The coefficient 3 multiplies them all: calcium $3 \times 1 = 3$, oxygen $3 \times 2 = 6$, hydrogen $3 \times 2 = 6$.
@answer 3 calcium atoms, 6 oxygen atoms and 6 hydrogen atoms: 15 atoms in all.
:::

| Shown | Atoms |
|---|---|
| $\ce{H2SO4}$ | 2 H, 1 S, 4 O |
| $\ce{4NH3}$ | 4 N, 12 H |
| $\ce{Mg(NO3)2}$ | 1 Mg, 2 N, 6 O |
| $\ce{2Al2(SO4)3}$ | 4 Al, 6 S, 24 O |

## Balancing step by step

The unbalanced symbol equation for hydrogen burning is $\ce{H2 + O2 -> H2O}$. Count the atoms on each side:

| Element | Left | Right |
|---|---|---|
| H | 2 | 2 |
| O | 2 | 1 |

The oxygen does not match, so the equation breaks the law of conservation of mass. To fix it, change the **coefficients** only:

1. **Count** the atoms of each element on both sides.
2. **Pick** an element that does not match and change a coefficient so that it does.
3. **Count again**: the change may upset another element. Repeat until every element matches.
4. **Check** that the coefficients are the smallest whole numbers that work. A coefficient of 1 is not written.

:::example Hydrogen and oxygen
Balance $\ce{H2 + O2 -> H2O}$.
@step Oxygen: 2 on the left, 1 on the right. Write $\ce{2H2O}$, which holds 2 oxygen atoms.
@step Now hydrogen is 2 on the left and 4 on the right. Write $\ce{2H2}$.
@answer $\ce{2H2 + O2 -> 2H2O}$. Check: H 4 = 4, O 2 = 2.
:::

:::example Making ammonia
Balance $\ce{N2 + H2 -> NH3}$.
@step Nitrogen: 2 on the left, so write $\ce{2NH3}$.
@step $\ce{2NH3}$ holds $2 \times 3 = 6$ hydrogen atoms, which is three $\ce{H2}$ molecules: write $\ce{3H2}$.
@answer $\ce{N2 + 3H2 -> 2NH3}$. Check: N 2 = 2, H 6 = 6.
:::

:::mistake Change coefficients, never subscripts
To balance $\ce{H2 + O2 -> H2O}$ you might be tempted to write $\ce{H2O2}$ on the right, so that the oxygen matches. But $\ce{H2O2}$ is hydrogen peroxide, a different substance from water. The subscripts say what a substance is, so they never change. Only the coefficients in front change, because they say how many particles there are.
:::

In the practice questions you type each coefficient into the box in front of its formula. Leave a box empty when the coefficient is 1.

## State symbols

A symbol equation can also show the state of each substance, with a **state symbol** in brackets after its formula.

:::definition State symbols
**(s)** solid, **(l)** liquid, **(g)** gas, and **(aq)** aqueous: dissolved in water.
:::

$$\ce{Zn(s) + 2HCl(aq) -> ZnCl2(aq) + H2(g)}$$

This says that solid zinc reacts with hydrochloric acid, which is hydrogen chloride dissolved in water, to make zinc chloride, which stays dissolved, and hydrogen gas. A solid that forms when two solutions are mixed, a **precipitate**, is (s).

:::mistake Liquid or aqueous?
Water on its own is a liquid, $\ce{H2O(l)}$. A substance dissolved in water is aqueous, (aq), even though the solution is a liquid: salt water is $\ce{NaCl(aq)}$, not $\ce{NaCl(l)}$. Use (l) only for a pure liquid, such as water or a molten metal.
:::

## Fuels, metals and polyatomic ions

Some equations need a little more planning. Three habits help.

**Combustion: carbon, then hydrogen, then oxygen.** When a fuel burns, oxygen appears in both products, so balance it last.

:::example Burning propane
Balance $\ce{C3H8 + O2 -> CO2 + H2O}$.
@step Carbon: 3 atoms on the left, so write $\ce{3CO2}$.
@step Hydrogen: 8 atoms on the left, so write $\ce{4H2O}$.
@step Oxygen last: the right side now has $3 \times 2 + 4 \times 1 = 10$ oxygen atoms, which is $\ce{5O2}$.
@answer $\ce{C3H8 + 5O2 -> 3CO2 + 4H2O}$. Check: C 3 = 3, H 8 = 8, O 10 = 10.
:::

**Leave a lone element until last.** An element written on its own, such as $\ce{H2}$ or $\ce{Cu}$, appears in only one formula, so changing its coefficient changes nothing else.

:::example Sodium and water
Balance $\ce{Na + H2O -> NaOH + H2}$.
@step Think of water as H–OH. Each $\ce{NaOH}$ takes the OH group of one water molecule, which leaves one hydrogen atom behind.
@step Hydrogen is given off in pairs, as $\ce{H2}$, so two water molecules are needed: $\ce{2H2O}$ gives $\ce{2NaOH}$ and one $\ce{H2}$.
@step $\ce{2NaOH}$ holds 2 sodium atoms, so write $\ce{2Na}$.
@answer $\ce{2Na(s) + 2H2O(l) -> 2NaOH(aq) + H2(g)}$. Check: Na 2 = 2, H 4 = 4, O 2 = 2.
:::

**Keep polyatomic ions together.** A group such as sulfate, $\ce{SO4}$, nitrate, $\ce{NO3}$, or hydroxide, $\ce{OH}$, often passes through a reaction unchanged. Count it as one unit instead of counting its atoms.

:::example Lead(II) nitrate and potassium iodide
Balance $\ce{Pb(NO3)2 + KI -> PbI2 + KNO3}$.
@step Count the nitrate groups as units: 2 on the left, 1 on the right. Write $\ce{2KNO3}$.
@step Now potassium is 2 on the right, so write $\ce{2KI}$. That gives 2 iodine atoms, matching $\ce{PbI2}$.
@answer $\ce{Pb(NO3)2(aq) + 2KI(aq) -> PbI2(s) + 2KNO3(aq)}$. Check: Pb 1 = 1, N 2 = 2, O 6 = 6, K 2 = 2, I 2 = 2.
:::

**If oxygen needs a half, double everything.** Coefficients must be whole numbers.

:::example Burning ethane
Balance $\ce{C2H6 + O2 -> CO2 + H2O}$.
@step Carbon: $\ce{2CO2}$. Hydrogen: 6 atoms, so $\ce{3H2O}$.
@step Oxygen: the right side has $2 \times 2 + 3 = 7$ atoms, which would need $3\tfrac{1}{2}$ molecules of $\ce{O2}$.
@step Double every coefficient: $\ce{2C2H6}$, $\ce{7O2}$, $\ce{4CO2}$ and $\ce{6H2O}$.
@answer $\ce{2C2H6 + 7O2 -> 4CO2 + 6H2O}$. Check: C 4 = 4, H 12 = 12, O 14 = 14.
:::

:::

:::summary
- A **subscript** counts the atom before it; a number after a **bracket** multiplies everything inside; a **coefficient** multiplies the whole formula.
- A **balanced** equation has the same number of atoms of each element on both sides, as the conservation of mass requires.
- Balance by changing **coefficients only**, never subscripts, and use the smallest whole numbers. Finish with an atom check.
- Combustion: carbon, then hydrogen, then oxygen. Keep polyatomic ions together, leave lone elements until last, and double everything if oxygen needs a half.
- **State symbols:** (s) solid, (l) liquid, (g) gas, (aq) dissolved in water. A precipitate is (s).
:::
