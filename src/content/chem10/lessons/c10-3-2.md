In the last lesson, metal atoms lost electrons and non-metal atoms gained them. Those two things happen together: when a metal reacts with a non-metal, the electrons the metal atoms lose are the very ones the non-metal atoms gain. The positive and negative ions that form attract each other strongly and pack together into a crystal. Table salt, the chalk in a classroom and the lime used to make cement are all built this way.

In this lesson you will see how an ionic bond forms, write the formulas of ionic compounds so their charges cancel, and name them, including compounds of a few common ions made of several atoms.

## Electron transfer and the ionic bond

Chemists show how electrons move with a **dot-and-cross diagram**. The electrons of one atom are drawn as dots and those of the other as crosses, so you can see where each electron ends up. For sodium reacting with chlorine:

1. Draw the sodium atom with its outer shell holding one cross, and the chlorine atom with its outer shell holding seven dots.
2. Draw an arrow carrying the cross from sodium's outer shell into chlorine's outer shell.
3. Draw the products. Sodium has lost its outer shell and is left as 2,8; draw it in square brackets with a + at the top right. Chlorine's outer shell now holds seven dots and one cross, eight electrons; draw it in square brackets with a − at the top right.

The sodium ion and the chloride ion now attract each other, because opposite charges attract.

:::definition Ionic bond
An **ionic bond** is the strong attraction between oppositely charged ions. It forms when a metal atom transfers electrons to a non-metal atom.
:::

:::definition Ionic compound
An **ionic compound** is a compound of positive and negative ions, held together by ionic bonds. The ions pack into a regular three-dimensional pattern called a **lattice**: in sodium chloride, each $\ce{Na+}$ ion is surrounded by six $\ce{Cl-}$ ions, and each $\ce{Cl-}$ ion by six $\ce{Na+}$ ions.
:::

A crystal of salt is not made of separate pairs of ions; it is one huge lattice. So the formula $\ce{NaCl}$ does not describe a molecule. It gives the **ratio** of the ions: one sodium ion for every chloride ion.

:::definition Formula unit
A **formula unit** is the smallest group of ions that shows the ratio in an ionic compound's formula. One formula unit of $\ce{MgCl2}$ is one magnesium ion and two chloride ions.
:::

:::example Electron transfer in magnesium chloride
Describe what happens to the electrons when magnesium reacts with chlorine, and explain the formula $\ce{MgCl2}$.
@step A magnesium atom (2,8,2) must lose 2 outer electrons to reach 2,8.
@step A chlorine atom (2,8,7) needs only 1 more electron to reach 2,8,8, so one chlorine atom can take only one of them.
@step In the dot-and-cross diagram, one cross goes to each of two chlorine atoms. The products are one $\ce{Mg^2+}$ ion and two $\ce{Cl-}$ ions.
@answer Each magnesium atom gives one electron to each of two chlorine atoms, so the compound has one $\ce{Mg^2+}$ for every two $\ce{Cl-}$: $\ce{MgCl2}$.
:::

## Writing formulas

An ionic compound has no overall charge: its positive charges cancel its negative charges exactly. To write its formula, find the fewest ions whose charges add up to zero.

1. Write the two ions with their charges (use the groups from Lesson 3.1).
2. Find the smallest total charge both can make: the lowest common multiple of the two charge sizes.
3. Divide that total by each charge to find how many of each ion you need.
4. Write the metal ion first and the non-metal ion second, with the numbers as small subscripts after each symbol. A 1 is not written, and the charges are left out.

:::example Aluminium oxide
Write the formula of aluminium oxide.
@step Aluminium is in group 13 and oxygen in group 16, so the ions are $\ce{Al^3+}$ and $\ce{O^2-}$.
@step The smallest total that both 3 and 2 go into is 6.
@step Two aluminium ions give 2 × 3+ = 6+, and three oxide ions give 3 × 2− = 6−. The charges cancel.
@answer $\ce{Al2O3}$
:::

:::example Magnesium nitride
Write the formula of magnesium nitride.
@step The ions are $\ce{Mg^2+}$ (group 2) and $\ce{N^3-}$ (group 15).
@step The smallest total is 6: three magnesium ions give 6+ and two nitride ions give 6−.
@answer $\ce{Mg3N2}$
:::

When the two charges are the same size, one of each is enough: magnesium oxide is $\ce{MgO}$, and potassium chloride is $\ce{KCl}$.

:::mistake Use the fewest ions
Calcium oxide is $\ce{CaO}$, not $\ce{Ca2O2}$. One $\ce{Ca^2+}$ already cancels one $\ce{O^2-}$, and a formula always uses the smallest whole-number ratio. If you swap the sizes of the charges into subscripts as a shortcut, check whether the numbers can be divided down.
:::

## Naming ionic compounds

The name of an ionic compound is the name of the positive ion, then the name of the negative ion:

- the metal keeps its own name: sodium, calcium, aluminium;
- a non-metal ion ends in **-ide**.

| Element | fluorine | chlorine | bromine | iodine | oxygen | sulfur | nitrogen | phosphorus |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Ion | fluoride $\ce{F-}$ | chloride $\ce{Cl-}$ | bromide $\ce{Br-}$ | iodide $\ce{I-}$ | oxide $\ce{O^2-}$ | sulfide $\ce{S^2-}$ | nitride $\ce{N^3-}$ | phosphide $\ce{P^3-}$ |

So $\ce{KBr}$ is potassium bromide, $\ce{CaS}$ is calcium sulfide and $\ce{Li3N}$ is lithium nitride. (Sulfur and sulfide are spelled *sulphur* and *sulphide* in some countries; both spellings are accepted in your answers.)

:::example Naming from a formula
Name $\ce{Na2O}$ and $\ce{AlCl3}$.
@step $\ce{Na2O}$: the metal is sodium, and oxygen becomes oxide.
@step $\ce{AlCl3}$: the metal is aluminium, and chlorine becomes chloride.
@answer Sodium oxide and aluminium chloride.
:::

:::mistake No counting words in ionic names
The subscripts in an ionic formula are fixed by the charges, so the name never counts the ions. $\ce{MgCl2}$ is magnesium chloride, not magnesium dichloride, and $\ce{Al2O3}$ is aluminium oxide, not dialuminium trioxide. Counting words such as di- and tri- belong to compounds of non-metals only, in the next lesson.
:::

## Polyatomic ions

Some ions are made of several atoms joined together. The whole group carries the charge, and it stays together through reactions, so you treat it as a single ion.

:::definition Polyatomic ion
A **polyatomic ion** is a group of atoms, bonded together, that carries an overall charge. The sulfate ion, $\ce{SO4^2-}$, is one sulfur atom and four oxygen atoms with a total charge of 2−.
:::

These five are the ones you will meet most often in Grade 10:

| Name | Formula | Charge | Found in |
|---|---|---|---|
| ammonium | $\ce{NH4+}$ | 1+ | fertilizers such as ammonium nitrate |
| hydroxide | $\ce{OH-}$ | 1− | sodium hydroxide, used in soap-making |
| nitrate | $\ce{NO3-}$ | 1− | fertilizers |
| sulfate | $\ce{SO4^2-}$ | 2− | Epsom salts, magnesium sulfate |
| carbonate | $\ce{CO3^2-}$ | 2− | chalk and limestone, calcium carbonate |

Ammonium is the only positive one. It takes the place of a metal ion, so ammonium compounds are ionic even though they contain no metal.

:::tip -ide, -ate and the polyatomic ions
An ending of **-ide** usually means a single atom: sulfide is $\ce{S^2-}$ and nitride is $\ce{N^3-}$. An ending of **-ate** means the ion also contains oxygen: sulfate is $\ce{SO4^2-}$ and nitrate is $\ce{NO3-}$. Hydroxide is the one -ide ion in the table with two atoms.
:::

A polyatomic ion keeps its name in a compound: $\ce{NaOH}$ is sodium hydroxide, $\ce{CaCO3}$ is calcium carbonate and $\ce{NH4Cl}$ is ammonium chloride.

To write a formula, cancel the charges as before. When you need **more than one** of a polyatomic ion, put the ion in brackets and write the number after the bracket. With only one, leave the brackets out.

:::example Calcium hydroxide
Write the formula of calcium hydroxide.
@step The ions are $\ce{Ca^2+}$ and $\ce{OH-}$.
@step One calcium ion gives 2+, so it needs two hydroxide ions (2 × 1− = 2−).
@step There is more than one hydroxide ion, so it goes in brackets.
@answer $\ce{Ca(OH)2}$
:::

:::example Ammonium sulfate
Write the formula of ammonium sulfate.
@step The ions are $\ce{NH4+}$ and $\ce{SO4^2-}$.
@step Two ammonium ions give 2+, which cancels one sulfate ion.
@step There are two ammonium ions, so they go in brackets; there is one sulfate ion, so it needs none.
@answer $\ce{(NH4)2SO4}$
:::

:::mistake Keep a polyatomic ion whole
$\ce{CaOH2}$ would mean one oxygen atom and two hydrogen atoms, but two hydroxide ions hold two of each. Never change the small numbers inside a polyatomic ion; show how many of the whole ion there are with brackets: $\ce{Ca(OH)2}$.
:::

:::note Metals with more than one charge
A few metals, such as iron and copper, can form ions with different charges. Their names carry a Roman numeral to say which ion is present, for example iron(III) oxide. You will learn these names in Grade 11; the questions here use only metals with one ion.
:::

:::summary
- An ionic bond is the attraction between oppositely charged ions, formed when a metal transfers electrons to a non-metal. A dot-and-cross diagram shows where each electron goes.
- An ionic compound is a lattice of ions; its formula gives the ratio of the ions (one formula unit).
- Write a formula with the fewest ions whose charges add up to zero, metal first, using the smallest whole-number ratio.
- Name the metal first, then the non-metal with the ending -ide. Ionic names never use counting words such as di-.
- Learn five polyatomic ions: ammonium $\ce{NH4+}$, hydroxide $\ce{OH-}$, nitrate $\ce{NO3-}$, sulfate $\ce{SO4^2-}$ and carbonate $\ce{CO3^2-}$. They keep their names in compounds.
- Put a polyatomic ion in brackets when there is more than one of it: $\ce{Ca(OH)2}$, $\ce{(NH4)2SO4}$.
:::
