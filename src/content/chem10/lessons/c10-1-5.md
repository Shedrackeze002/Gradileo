Chemists work every day with substances that can burn, poison, explode or eat through metal, and accidents are rare. That is not luck. It comes from knowing exactly what each substance can do, reading the warnings on every label, and following a few rules every single time, even when an experiment seems harmless.

In this lesson you will learn to read the **hazard pictograms** used on chemical labels around the world, and to choose safe ways of working in a laboratory.

## Hazard pictograms

Most countries label hazardous chemicals with the same set of symbols, from the **Globally Harmonized System** (GHS). Each **hazard pictogram** is a black symbol on a white background inside a red-bordered diamond (a square standing on one corner). A label shows one pictogram for each kind of hazard the substance has, so a bottle may carry two or three.

:::definition Hazard pictogram
A **hazard pictogram** is a symbol on a chemical's label that shows, at a glance, what kind of harm the substance can do. The label also gives a signal word, **Danger** for the more severe hazards or **Warning** for the less severe ones, and statements describing the hazards and the precautions to take.
:::

| Pictogram | What it shows | What it means | Examples | Precaution |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| flame | a flame | **flammable**: catches fire easily | ethanol, hexane, methane | keep away from flames, sparks and hot surfaces |
| flame over circle | a flame above a circle | **oxidizer**: gives off oxygen and makes other materials burn fiercely | potassium nitrate, concentrated hydrogen peroxide | store away from paper, fuels and anything that burns |
| exploding bomb | a bomb bursting apart | **explosive**: can explode if heated, struck or rubbed | fireworks, some unstable compounds | never heat, grind or strike it |
| corrosion | liquid dripping from two test tubes onto a hand and a metal bar, both being eaten away | **corrosive**: burns skin, damages eyes, eats away metals | concentrated acids, sodium hydroxide | wear goggles and gloves; wash off splashes at once |
| skull and crossbones | a skull and crossbones | **acutely toxic**: a small amount can poison or kill | methanol, many mercury compounds | avoid all contact; never let it near your mouth |
| exclamation mark | an exclamation mark (!) | **harmful** or **irritant**: irritates skin, eyes or airways, or harms you in larger amounts | many dilute solutions, calcium chloride | keep it off your skin and out of your eyes |
| health hazard | a person's head and chest with a star-shaped burst on the chest | **serious long-term health hazard**: may cause cancer, damage organs or cause breathing problems | some solvents, some fine dusts | do not breathe in its dust or vapour; use a fume cupboard |
| gas cylinder | a gas cylinder | **gas under pressure**: the container may burst if heated | cylinders of oxygen, nitrogen or carbon dioxide | keep the cylinder upright, secured and away from heat |
| environment | a dead tree and a dead fish | **hazardous to the environment**: poisonous to fish and other water life | copper(II) sulfate, silver nitrate | never pour it down the sink; use the waste container |

:::definition Flammable, corrosive, toxic and oxidizer
A **flammable** substance catches fire easily. A **corrosive** substance destroys living tissue and metals on contact. A **toxic** substance is a poison: it harms or kills living things when swallowed, breathed in or absorbed through the skin. An **oxidizer** does not need to burn itself, but it releases oxygen that makes other substances burn faster and hotter.
:::

:::definition Safety data sheet
A **safety data sheet** (SDS) is a document supplied with every hazardous chemical. It describes the chemical's hazards and gives full instructions for using, storing and disposing of it safely and for first aid. Your teacher checks it before an experiment is planned.
:::

:::example Reading a label
A bottle of methanol shows three pictograms: a flame, a skull and crossbones, and a person's head and chest with a star-shaped burst on the chest. What hazards does methanol have, and which precautions follow?
@step The flame means methanol is flammable: keep it away from flames and sparks.
@step The skull and crossbones means it is acutely toxic: a small amount, swallowed or absorbed through the skin, can poison. Avoid all contact.
@step The health hazard pictogram means it can cause lasting harm to the body; here, to the eyes and nerves. Do not breathe in its vapour.
@answer Methanol is flammable, acutely toxic and a long-term health hazard: no flames nearby, gloves and goggles, and work in a fume cupboard.
:::

:::mistake The exclamation mark is not the skull
The exclamation mark warns of a **less** severe hazard: irritation, or harm from a larger amount. The skull and crossbones warns that even a **small** amount can kill. And a bottle with no pictogram is not automatically harmless: still read the label, and never taste or sniff anything to find out.
:::

## Working safely in the laboratory

Most laboratory accidents come from a few simple mistakes. These rules prevent them.

**Protect yourself.**

- Wear **safety goggles** whenever chemicals are being heated, mixed or poured, and keep them over your eyes, not on your forehead.
- **Tie back long hair** and tuck in loose clothing before lighting a Bunsen burner.
- **Never eat, drink or taste** anything in a laboratory, and wash your hands at the end of every practical.
- To smell a substance, only when your teacher tells you to, hold it away from your face and **waft** a little of the vapour towards your nose with your hand.

**Heat things safely.**

- When heating a liquid in a test tube, hold the tube in a test-tube holder, heat it gently, and **point the open end away from yourself and everyone else**: the liquid can boil suddenly and shoot out.
- Never heat a container that is sealed, because the gas inside expands and can burst it.
- When a Bunsen burner is lit but not in use, close the air hole to give the yellow **safety flame**, which is easy to see, or turn it off. Never leave a lit burner unattended.
- Keep flammable liquids such as ethanol well away from flames.
- Put hot glassware down on a heat-proof mat; hot glass looks exactly like cold glass.

**Handle chemicals safely.**

- To dilute a concentrated acid, **add the acid slowly to the water**, stirring, never water to acid. Mixing gives out a lot of heat; adding the acid a little at a time to plenty of water lets the water absorb the heat.
- Carry out reactions that give off harmful gases in a **fume cupboard**, which draws the gas away.
- Never put unused chemicals back in the stock bottle, where they could contaminate it.
- Dispose of waste as your teacher tells you, in the **waste containers** provided. Do not pour chemicals down the sink unless you are told it is safe.

**Report everything.**

- Tell your teacher **straight away** about any spill, breakage, cut or burn, however small. Broken glass goes in a special container, not the paper bin.
- If a chemical splashes into an eye, rinse it with plenty of clean running water at the eyewash station for several minutes, and tell the teacher.

:::example Spotting the unsafe actions
During a practical, Noah (1) puts on safety goggles, (2) lights a Bunsen burner and leaves it on the hot blue flame while he fetches a test tube, (3) heats a solution in the test tube, pointing it towards the wall with nobody near, and (4) pours the leftover solution back into the stock bottle. Which actions were unsafe?
@step (1) is safe: goggles protect his eyes.
@step (2) is unsafe: the blue flame is hard to see and he left it unattended. He should have used the yellow safety flame or turned it off.
@step (3) is safe: the open end pointed away from people.
@step (4) is unsafe: putting the solution back could contaminate the stock bottle. It should go in the waste container.
@answer Actions (2) and (4) were unsafe.
:::

:::mistake Never add water to concentrated acid
Pouring water into concentrated acid can make the first drops of water boil instantly and spit hot acid out of the container. Always add the acid to the water, slowly, so that there is plenty of water to absorb the heat.
:::

:::summary
- **Hazard pictograms** are black symbols in red-bordered diamonds; a label shows one for each kind of hazard. Learn all nine: flame (**flammable**), flame over circle (**oxidizer**), exploding bomb (explosive), corrosion (**corrosive**), skull and crossbones (acutely **toxic**), exclamation mark (harmful or irritant), health hazard (long-term harm), gas cylinder (gas under pressure) and environment (poisonous to water life).
- A **safety data sheet** gives the full hazards of a chemical and how to use, store and dispose of it safely.
- Wear goggles, tie back long hair, and never eat, drink or taste anything in the laboratory. Waft to smell.
- Point a heated test tube away from people, never heat a sealed container, and use the safety flame when a burner is not in use.
- Add acid to water, never water to acid. Use a fume cupboard for harmful gases and the waste containers for leftover chemicals.
- Report every spill, breakage and accident to your teacher straight away.
:::
