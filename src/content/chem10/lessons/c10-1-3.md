Sea water covers most of the planet, yet you cannot drink it: it is a mixture of water and dissolved salts. Crude oil is no use in a car until it has been separated into fuels. A forensic scientist who wants to know which pen wrote a note needs to pull apart the dyes in its ink. In each case, the job is to separate a mixture into its parts.

Because the substances in a mixture are not chemically joined, they can be separated by **physical** methods. In this lesson you will choose the right method for a mixture from the properties of its parts, and use **paper chromatography** and **$R_f$ values** to identify the substances in a mixture.

## Choosing a method

Every separation method works because the parts of the mixture differ in some property: whether they dissolve, their boiling points, whether they are magnetic, their densities. The first step is always to ask how the parts differ.

| Method | Separates | The difference it relies on |
|---|---|---|
| filtration | an insoluble solid from a liquid | one part does not dissolve |
| evaporation | a dissolved solid from its solution (the solvent is lost) | the solvent evaporates, the solid does not |
| crystallization | crystals of a dissolved solid from its solution | crystals form as a hot, concentrated solution cools |
| simple distillation | a liquid from a solution (the liquid is collected) | very different boiling points |
| fractional distillation | liquids that mix completely | different boiling points |
| paper chromatography | dissolved substances, such as the dyes in an ink | they travel along paper at different speeds |
| a magnet | a magnetic solid from a non-magnetic one | magnetism |
| a separating funnel | liquids that do not mix | they form layers |
| decanting | a liquid from a dense solid that has settled | the solid sinks to the bottom |

## Filtration, evaporation and crystallization

**Filtration** separates a solid that does not dissolve from a liquid. The mixture is poured through filter paper in a funnel. The paper has tiny holes: water particles and dissolved particles pass through, but the grains of the insoluble solid are too big.

:::definition Filtrate and residue
The **filtrate** is the liquid that passes through the filter paper. The **residue** is the solid left on the paper.
:::

**Evaporation** recovers a dissolved solid from a solution. The solution is heated in an evaporating dish until all the solvent has evaporated, leaving the solid behind.

**Crystallization** is used when a solid would be damaged by heating it to dryness. Copper(II) sulfate crystals, for example, hold water within them; heating them dry drives off that water and turns the blue crystals into a white powder. Instead, the solution is heated only until it is concentrated, then left to cool. As it cools, crystals form. They are filtered off and left to dry. Sugar is crystallized for the same kind of reason: strong heating chars it.

:::example Separating sand and salt
Ines has a mixture of sand and salt. Plan how to obtain dry salt and dry sand.
@step Salt dissolves in water and sand does not, so add water and stir until all the salt has dissolved.
@step Filter the mixture. The sand stays on the filter paper as the residue; the salt solution passes through as the filtrate. Rinse the sand with a little water and leave it to dry.
@step Heat the filtrate in an evaporating dish to evaporate the water, leaving the salt.
@answer Dissolve in water, filter off the sand, then evaporate the water from the filtrate to obtain the salt.
:::

:::mistake Filter paper cannot catch dissolved particles
A dissolved solid passes straight through filter paper with the water. Filtering salt water gives salt water. To get the salt back you must evaporate the water; to get the water back you must distil it.
:::

## Distillation and other methods

**Simple distillation** collects the pure solvent from a solution. The solution is boiled in a flask. The vapour travels into a **condenser**, a tube cooled by cold water flowing around it, where it condenses back into a liquid, the **distillate**, which drips into a collecting flask. The dissolved solid, which has a much higher boiling point, stays behind in the flask. Distilling sea water gives pure water.

**Fractional distillation** separates liquids that mix completely but have different boiling points, such as ethanol (boiling point 78 °C) and water (100 °C). A tall **fractionating column** above the flask helps separate the vapours. The liquid with the **lowest boiling point** reaches the top of the column and distils over first; as the temperature rises, the next liquid follows. Fractional distillation separates crude oil into fuels and separates liquid air into nitrogen and oxygen.

Some mixtures need simpler methods:

- **A magnet** pulls iron or steel out of a mixture with non-magnetic solids, such as iron filings from sand.
- **A separating funnel** separates liquids that do not mix, such as oil and water. The liquids settle into two layers, the denser one at the bottom; opening the tap at the bottom runs off the lower layer first.
- **Decanting** means carefully pouring a liquid off a dense solid that has settled to the bottom, such as water off gravel.

:::example Two liquids
Liquid P boils at 65 °C and liquid Q at 118 °C. They mix completely. Which method separates them, and which liquid is collected first?
@step The liquids mix completely, so they do not form layers: a separating funnel will not work.
@step They have different boiling points, so fractional distillation separates them.
@step P has the lower boiling point, so it boils off and is collected first.
@answer Fractional distillation; liquid P is collected first.
:::

## Paper chromatography

**Paper chromatography** separates and identifies the dissolved substances in a mixture, such as the dyes in an ink or a food colouring.

1. A line, the **baseline**, is drawn in pencil about $1\ \text{cm}$ above the bottom of a strip of chromatography paper. Pencil is used because graphite does not dissolve and run.
2. A small spot of each sample is placed on the baseline.
3. The paper is stood in a container with a little solvent (often water) in the bottom. The solvent must be **below** the baseline, or the spots would dissolve into it. A lid stops the solvent evaporating.
4. The solvent soaks up the paper, carrying the dissolved substances with it. Substances that dissolve better in the solvent, or stick less to the paper, travel further.
5. Before the solvent reaches the top, the paper is taken out and the furthest point the solvent reached, the **solvent front**, is marked in pencil.

The pattern of spots is called a **chromatogram**. A pure substance gives one spot; a mixture gives a spot for each substance.

:::definition Rf value
The **$R_f$ value** of a substance is the distance it moves divided by the distance the solvent moves, both measured from the baseline:
$$R_f = \frac{\text{distance moved by the spot}}{\text{distance moved by the solvent}}$$
An $R_f$ value has no unit and is always less than 1. In the same solvent (and on the same kind of paper), a substance always has the same $R_f$ value, so it can be used to identify the substance.
:::

:::example Finding Rf values
Use the chromatogram. Find the $R_f$ values of the spots of ink X, and say which dyes the ink contains.
@step Distances are measured from the baseline, which is $1\ \text{cm}$ above the bottom edge. The solvent moved $9 - 1 = 8\ \text{cm}$.
@step The lower spot of X moved $3 - 1 = 2\ \text{cm}$: $R_f = \dfrac{2}{8} = 0.25$.
@step The upper spot moved $7 - 1 = 6\ \text{cm}$: $R_f = \dfrac{6}{8} = 0.75$.
@step Dye A's spot is level with the lower spot ($R_f = 0.25$) and dye B's with the upper spot ($R_f = 0.75$).
@answer The $R_f$ values are 0.25 and 0.75; ink X is a mixture of dyes A and B.
:::

:::example Using an Rf value to find a distance
In a certain solvent, a red dye has an $R_f$ value of 0.40. If the solvent moves $9.5\ \text{cm}$ from the baseline, how far will the red spot move?
@step Rearrange: distance moved by the spot $= R_f \times$ distance moved by the solvent.
@step $0.40 \times 9.5 = 3.8\ \text{cm}$
@answer The red spot moves $3.8\ \text{cm}$ from the baseline.
:::

:::mistake Measure from the baseline
Both distances in an $R_f$ value are measured from the **baseline**, where the spots started, not from the bottom edge of the paper. Measure to the centre of each spot. If your $R_f$ comes out greater than 1, you have divided the wrong way round: the spot can never travel further than the solvent.
:::

To identify an unknown substance, compare its $R_f$ value with the $R_f$ values of known substances measured **in the same solvent**. A substance's $R_f$ value changes from one solvent to another, so values from different solvents cannot be compared.

:::summary
- Mixtures are separated by physical methods that use a difference between their parts.
- **Filtration** removes an insoluble solid (the **residue**) from a liquid (the **filtrate**). **Evaporation** recovers a dissolved solid. **Crystallization** gives crystals of a dissolved solid that heating to dryness would damage.
- **Simple distillation** collects the solvent from a solution. **Fractional distillation** separates liquids that mix, by boiling point; the lowest boiling point distils first.
- **A magnet** removes magnetic solids, **a separating funnel** separates liquids that do not mix (the denser layer runs off first), and **decanting** pours a liquid off a settled solid.
- **Paper chromatography** separates dissolved substances. $R_f = \dfrac{\text{distance moved by the spot}}{\text{distance moved by the solvent}}$, both from the baseline; it is less than 1, and matching $R_f$ values in the same solvent identifies a substance.
:::
