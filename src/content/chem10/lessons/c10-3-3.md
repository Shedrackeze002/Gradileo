Water, the carbon dioxide you breathe out and the methane burned on a gas stove contain no metal at all. Every atom in them is a non-metal, and non-metal atoms all hold on to their electrons tightly, so none of them can pull an electron right off another. Instead they **share** electrons. A shared pair of electrons holds two atoms together, and atoms joined this way form molecules.

In this lesson you will decide from its elements whether a substance is ionic, covalent or metallic, count the shared pairs in some simple molecules, and name compounds of two non-metals with counting prefixes.

## Ionic, covalent or metallic?

The kind of bonding in a substance depends on what its elements are. Look at the periodic table: metals are on the left and in the middle, non-metals on the right (with hydrogen, a non-metal, at the top).

:::definition Covalent bond
A **covalent bond** is a pair of electrons shared between two atoms. Each atom counts the shared electrons as its own, which helps both fill their outer shells. Non-metal atoms bond to each other this way.
:::

| The elements | What happens to the electrons | Bonding | Examples |
|---|---|---|---|
| a metal and a non-metal | the metal atoms transfer electrons to the non-metal atoms | ionic | $\ce{NaCl}$, $\ce{MgO}$, $\ce{CaCl2}$ |
| non-metals only | the atoms share pairs of electrons | covalent | $\ce{H2O}$, $\ce{CO2}$, $\ce{Cl2}$ |
| a metal on its own | the atoms share a "sea" of electrons that move freely | metallic | $\ce{Cu}$, $\ce{Fe}$, $\ce{Al}$ |

You will look more closely at metallic bonding in the next lesson. Notice that covalent bonds can join atoms of the same element as well as different ones: chlorine gas is made of $\ce{Cl2}$ molecules.

:::example Which kind of bonding?
What kind of bonding is in calcium oxide ($\ce{CaO}$), methane ($\ce{CH4}$) and zinc ($\ce{Zn}$)?
@step Calcium is a metal and oxygen a non-metal: ionic.
@step Carbon and hydrogen are both non-metals: covalent.
@step Zinc is a metal on its own: metallic.
@answer Calcium oxide is ionic, methane is covalent and zinc is metallic.
:::

:::note Ammonium compounds
Compounds of the ammonium ion, such as ammonium chloride, $\ce{NH4Cl}$, contain only non-metals, yet they are ionic: the $\ce{NH4+}$ ion plays the part of a metal ion. The atoms inside the ammonium ion are held together by covalent bonds.
:::

## Sharing electrons

:::definition Molecule
A **molecule** is a group of atoms held together by covalent bonds. A molecule of water, $\ce{H2O}$, is one oxygen atom bonded to two hydrogen atoms.
:::

A dot-and-cross diagram shows sharing too. Draw each atom's outer shell as a circle, with one atom's electrons as dots and the other's as crosses. Where two atoms bond, their circles overlap, and the shared pair sits in the overlap.

- **Hydrogen, $\ce{H2}$.** Each hydrogen atom has one electron and needs two to fill its only shell. Two hydrogen atoms overlap, with one dot and one cross in the overlap. That shared pair is a single covalent bond, and each atom now counts two electrons.
- **Hydrogen chloride, $\ce{HCl}$.** Chlorine has seven outer electrons and needs one more; hydrogen needs one more. The overlap holds one dot from chlorine and one cross from hydrogen. Chlorine's circle now holds eight electrons, and hydrogen's two.
- **Water, $\ce{H2O}$.** Oxygen has six outer electrons and needs two more. It overlaps with two hydrogen atoms, sharing one pair with each: two single bonds.

The number of bonds an atom usually forms is the number of electrons it needs to fill its outer shell:

| Atom | H | C | N | O | Cl |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Outer electrons | 1 | 4 | 5 | 6 | 7 |
| Electrons needed | 1 | 4 | 3 | 2 | 1 |
| Bonds it forms | 1 | 4 | 3 | 2 | 1 |

So nitrogen forms three bonds in ammonia, $\ce{NH3}$, and carbon forms four in methane, $\ce{CH4}$.

:::definition Single, double and triple bonds
A **single bond** is one shared pair of electrons. Two atoms can also share two pairs, a **double bond**, or three pairs, a **triple bond**.
:::

- **Oxygen, $\ce{O2}$.** Each oxygen atom needs two more electrons, so the two atoms share two pairs: a double bond.
- **Nitrogen, $\ce{N2}$.** Each nitrogen atom needs three more, so the two atoms share three pairs: a triple bond.
- **Carbon dioxide, $\ce{CO2}$.** Carbon needs four more and each oxygen needs two, so carbon shares two pairs with each oxygen: two double bonds.

| Molecule | $\ce{H2}$ | $\ce{Cl2}$ | $\ce{HCl}$ | $\ce{H2O}$ | $\ce{NH3}$ | $\ce{CH4}$ | $\ce{O2}$ | $\ce{N2}$ | $\ce{CO2}$ |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Bonds | 1 single | 1 single | 1 single | 2 single | 3 single | 4 single | 1 double | 1 triple | 2 double |
| Shared pairs | 1 | 1 | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 2 | 3 | 4 |
| Bonding electrons | 2 | 2 | 2 | 4 | 6 | 8 | 4 | 6 | 8 |

Every shared pair is two electrons, so **bonding electrons = 2 × shared pairs**.

:::example Counting the bonds in carbon dioxide
How many shared pairs, and how many bonding electrons, are in a molecule of $\ce{CO2}$?
@step Carbon has 4 outer electrons and needs 4 more; each oxygen has 6 and needs 2 more.
@step Carbon shares two pairs with each oxygen atom: two double bonds.
@step Shared pairs: $2 + 2 = 4$. Bonding electrons: $4 \times 2 = 8$.
@answer 4 shared pairs and 8 bonding electrons. Carbon counts 4 + 4 = 8 outer electrons, and each oxygen counts 4 + 4 = 8.
:::

:::mistake Pairs are not electrons
A double bond is two shared **pairs**, which is **four** electrons, not two. Read the question carefully: "how many bonds" or "shared pairs" counts pairs, while "bonding electrons" counts every electron in them.
:::

:::tip The diatomic elements
Seven elements exist as molecules of two atoms: hydrogen $\ce{H2}$, nitrogen $\ce{N2}$, oxygen $\ce{O2}$, fluorine $\ce{F2}$, chlorine $\ce{Cl2}$, bromine $\ce{Br2}$ and iodine $\ce{I2}$. When you write one of these elements on its own, write the molecule: oxygen gas is $\ce{O2}$.
:::

## Naming molecular compounds

Ion charges fix the formula of an ionic compound, so its name does not need to count anything. Two non-metals, though, can often join in more than one way: carbon and oxygen make both $\ce{CO}$ and $\ce{CO2}$, and they are very different gases. So the name of a molecular compound counts its atoms with a **prefix**, a word part in front of an element's name.

| Number of atoms | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 | 6 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Prefix | mono | di | tri | tetra | penta | hexa |

To name a compound of two non-metals, take the elements in the order the formula gives them:

1. Name the first element in full. Add a prefix only when there are two or more of its atoms: the first element **never** takes "mono".
2. Name the second element with the ending **-ide** (oxide, chloride, fluoride, sulfide), and always give it a prefix, even "mono".
3. When "mono" meets "oxide", the two o's run together: **monoxide**.

:::example Naming with prefixes
Name $\ce{CO}$, $\ce{CCl4}$ and $\ce{N2O4}$.
@step $\ce{CO}$: one carbon atom, so just "carbon"; one oxygen atom, "mono" + "oxide" = "monoxide".
@step $\ce{CCl4}$: one carbon atom, "carbon"; four chlorine atoms, "tetrachloride".
@step $\ce{N2O4}$: two nitrogen atoms, "dinitrogen"; four oxygen atoms, "tetraoxide".
@answer Carbon monoxide, carbon tetrachloride and dinitrogen tetraoxide.
:::

| Formula | Name | Formula | Name |
|---|---|---|---|
| $\ce{CO2}$ | carbon dioxide | $\ce{PCl3}$ | phosphorus trichloride |
| $\ce{SO2}$ | sulfur dioxide | $\ce{PCl5}$ | phosphorus pentachloride |
| $\ce{SO3}$ | sulfur trioxide | $\ce{SF6}$ | sulfur hexafluoride |
| $\ce{NO2}$ | nitrogen dioxide | $\ce{N2O}$ | dinitrogen monoxide |
| $\ce{SiCl4}$ | silicon tetrachloride | $\ce{P2O5}$ | diphosphorus pentaoxide |

:::note Two spellings
Gradileo writes the prefixes in full before "oxide": tetraoxide, pentaoxide. Many books drop the last vowel of the prefix instead: dinitrogen tetroxide, diphosphorus pentoxide. Both spellings are accepted in your answers.
:::

To write a formula from a name, turn each prefix back into a subscript. No prefix on the first element means one atom, and a 1 is never written. There are no charges to cancel, and you must not simplify the formula: it gives the actual atoms in one molecule.

:::example From a name to a formula
Write the formulas of nitrogen trifluoride and dinitrogen pentaoxide.
@step Nitrogen trifluoride: no prefix, so one nitrogen atom; "tri", so three fluorine atoms.
@step Dinitrogen pentaoxide: "di", so two nitrogen atoms; "penta", so five oxygen atoms.
@answer $\ce{NF3}$ and $\ce{N2O5}$.
:::

:::mistake Prefixes are for non-metal compounds only
Check the elements before you name anything. A metal with a non-metal is ionic and takes no prefixes: $\ce{CaCl2}$ is calcium chloride. Non-metals only means prefixes: $\ce{SCl2}$ is sulfur dichloride. And never put "mono" on the first element: $\ce{CO}$ is carbon monoxide, not monocarbon monoxide.
:::

:::summary
- Metal + non-metal: ionic bonding. Non-metals only: covalent bonding. A metal on its own: metallic bonding.
- A covalent bond is a shared pair of electrons. Atoms joined by covalent bonds form molecules.
- An atom usually forms as many bonds as the electrons it needs: H 1, O 2, N 3, C 4, Cl 1.
- A single bond is one shared pair, a double bond two and a triple bond three: $\ce{O2}$ has a double bond, $\ce{N2}$ a triple bond and $\ce{CO2}$ two double bonds.
- Bonding electrons = 2 × shared pairs.
- Name compounds of two non-metals with prefixes (mono 1, di 2, tri 3, tetra 4, penta 5, hexa 6). The first element never takes "mono"; the second ends in -ide; mono + oxide = monoxide.
:::
