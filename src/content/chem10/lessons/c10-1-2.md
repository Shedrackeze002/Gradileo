A bottle of spring water may say "pure" on its label, but to a chemist that water is far from pure: it contains dissolved minerals, a little dissolved air, and traces of many other substances. In chemistry, **pure** has a strict meaning: one substance and nothing else.

In this lesson you will sort all matter into **elements**, **compounds** and **mixtures**, tell **homogeneous** mixtures from **heterogeneous** ones, and look closely at one very common kind of mixture: the **solution**.

## Elements and compounds

:::definition Pure substance
A **pure substance** contains only one substance. Every sample of it has the same composition and the same properties, and it melts and boils at fixed temperatures. Elements and compounds are pure substances.
:::

:::definition Element
An **element** is a pure substance made of only one kind of atom. It cannot be broken down into simpler substances by any chemical reaction. There are more than a hundred elements, each with its own symbol: $\ce{C}$ for carbon, $\ce{Fe}$ for iron, $\ce{Na}$ for sodium.
:::

The atoms of some elements are joined in small groups called **molecules**. Oxygen gas is made of $\ce{O2}$ molecules, and nitrogen of $\ce{N2}$ molecules. They are still elements, because every atom in them is the same kind.

:::definition Compound
A **compound** is a pure substance made of two or more elements **chemically joined** in a **fixed ratio**. Water, $\ce{H2O}$, always has two hydrogen atoms for every oxygen atom. A compound can be broken down into simpler substances only by a chemical reaction.
:::

A compound has its own properties, usually very different from those of its elements. Sodium is a soft, shiny metal that reacts violently with water, and chlorine is a poisonous, yellow-green gas; chemically joined, they make sodium chloride, $\ce{NaCl}$, the table salt that you sprinkle on food. Hydrogen is a gas that burns, and oxygen is a gas that helps things burn, yet the compound they make, water, puts fires out.

A formula tells you which kind of substance you have:

- one element symbol, with or without a number after it ($\ce{Fe}$, $\ce{O2}$, $\ce{S8}$): an **element**;
- two or more different symbols ($\ce{H2O}$, $\ce{CO2}$, $\ce{CaCO3}$): a **compound**.

:::example Elements or compounds?
Classify each substance as an element or a compound: chlorine, $\ce{Cl2}$; methane, $\ce{CH4}$; helium, $\ce{He}$; carbon dioxide, $\ce{CO2}$.
@step $\ce{Cl2}$ contains only chlorine atoms (in pairs), so it is an element.
@step $\ce{CH4}$ contains carbon and hydrogen atoms joined together, so it is a compound.
@step $\ce{He}$ contains only helium atoms: an element.
@step $\ce{CO2}$ contains carbon and oxygen atoms joined together: a compound.
@answer Elements: chlorine and helium. Compounds: methane and carbon dioxide.
:::

:::mistake A molecule is not always a compound
$\ce{O2}$, $\ce{N2}$ and $\ce{Cl2}$ are molecules, but they are **elements**, not compounds: each contains only one kind of atom. A compound needs at least two different elements.
:::

## Mixtures

:::definition Mixture
A **mixture** contains two or more substances that are **not chemically joined**. Each substance keeps its own properties, the substances can be present in any proportions, and they can be separated by physical methods such as filtering, evaporating or using a magnet.
:::

Iron filings and sulfur powder show the difference between a mixture and a compound. Stirred together, they make a grey-yellow mixture: the iron is still magnetic, and a magnet pulls it out. Heated together, they glow and react to form a black solid, iron sulfide, $\ce{FeS}$: a compound, which a magnet does not attract and which cannot be separated by any physical method.

| | Mixture of iron and sulfur | Compound, iron sulfide |
|---|---|---|
| Are the elements chemically joined? | no | yes |
| Proportions | any | fixed: one iron atom for each sulfur atom |
| Properties | those of iron and sulfur | new properties of its own |
| Separated by | a magnet (a physical method) | only a chemical reaction |

:::definition Homogeneous and heterogeneous mixtures
A **homogeneous** mixture is the same all the way through: you cannot see its separate parts, even with a magnifying glass. Salt water, air, vinegar and brass are homogeneous. A **heterogeneous** mixture is not the same all the way through: you can see its different parts, or they separate into layers. Sand in water, oil and water, granite and soil are heterogeneous.
:::

:::example Classifying mixtures
Is each of these a homogeneous or a heterogeneous mixture: sugar dissolved in water; muddy water; steel (iron with a little carbon)?
@step Dissolved sugar is spread evenly through the water and cannot be seen, so sugar water is homogeneous.
@step Muddy water has bits of soil that you can see, and that settle to the bottom if it is left to stand, so it is heterogeneous.
@step Steel looks the same all the way through; the carbon is spread evenly through the iron. It is homogeneous.
@answer Sugar water and steel are homogeneous; muddy water is heterogeneous.
:::

:::mistake Clear does not mean pure
Salt water looks just like pure water, but it is a mixture. Looking the same all the way through makes a mixture **homogeneous**; it does not make it a pure substance. A pure substance has fixed melting and boiling points; salt water boils at a higher temperature than pure water, and its boiling point changes as the water boils away.
:::

## Solutions

Many homogeneous mixtures are **solutions**: one substance dissolved in another.

:::definition Solution, solute and solvent
A **solution** is a homogeneous mixture of a **solute**, the substance that dissolves, in a **solvent**, the substance it dissolves in. When both are liquids, or both are gases, the solvent is the one present in the larger amount. A solution with water as its solvent is an **aqueous** solution.
:::

In salt water, salt is the solute and water is the solvent. Solutes and solvents need not be a solid and a liquid:

| Solution | Solute | Solvent |
|---|---|---|
| sparkling water | carbon dioxide (a gas) | water (a liquid) |
| vinegar | acetic acid (a liquid) | water (a liquid) |
| brass | zinc (a solid) | copper (a solid) |
| air | oxygen, argon and other gases | nitrogen (a gas) |

Solid solutions of metals, such as brass and steel, are called **alloys**.

:::example Solute and solvent
A cleaning liquid is made by mixing $30\ \text{mL}$ of ethanol with $70\ \text{mL}$ of water. The two liquids mix completely. Name the solute and the solvent.
@step Both substances are liquids, so the solvent is the one present in the larger amount.
@step There is more water ($70\ \text{mL}$) than ethanol ($30\ \text{mL}$).
@answer Water is the solvent and ethanol is the solute.
:::

:::check c10-solutions 1
:::

When a solute dissolves, its particles spread out between the particles of the solvent. No particles are made or destroyed, so no mass is lost or gained:

$$\text{mass of solution} = \text{mass of solute} + \text{mass of solvent}$$

Dissolving is a physical change: evaporate the water from salt water and the salt is left behind, with the same mass as before.

:::example Mass of a solution
Kwame dissolves $12.5\ \text{g}$ of sugar in $150.0\ \text{g}$ of water. What is the mass of the solution?
@step Mass of solution = mass of solute + mass of solvent.
@step $12.5 + 150.0 = 162.5\ \text{g}$
@answer $162.5\ \text{g}$
:::

:::example Finding the mass of a solute
An empty beaker has a mass of $95.0\ \text{g}$. With water in it, the mass is $245.0\ \text{g}$. After some salt has dissolved in the water, the beaker and solution have a mass of $263.4\ \text{g}$. What mass of salt dissolved?
@step Mass of water: $245.0 - 95.0 = 150.0\ \text{g}$.
@step Mass of solution: $263.4 - 95.0 = 168.4\ \text{g}$.
@step Mass of salt = mass of solution − mass of water $= 168.4 - 150.0 = 18.4\ \text{g}$.
@answer $18.4\ \text{g}$ of salt dissolved.
:::

:::mistake A dissolved solid still has mass
When sugar dissolves it seems to disappear, but its particles are all still there, spread through the water. The solution weighs exactly as much as the sugar and the water did before. Volumes, though, do not always add up: $50\ \text{mL}$ of ethanol mixed with $50\ \text{mL}$ of water makes a little less than $100\ \text{mL}$, because the two kinds of particle pack together more closely than either liquid does on its own. Masses always add up.
:::

:::summary
- A **pure substance** contains one substance only, with fixed melting and boiling points. Elements and compounds are pure substances.
- An **element** contains only one kind of atom ($\ce{Fe}$, $\ce{O2}$). A **compound** contains two or more elements chemically joined in a fixed ratio ($\ce{H2O}$, $\ce{NaCl}$) and has properties of its own.
- A **mixture** contains substances that are not chemically joined. They keep their own properties, can be in any proportions, and can be separated by physical methods.
- A **homogeneous** mixture is the same all the way through (salt water, air, brass); a **heterogeneous** mixture has parts you can see (sand and water, granite).
- A **solution** is a homogeneous mixture of a **solute** dissolved in a **solvent**; the solvent is the substance present in the larger amount.
- Mass of solution = mass of solute + mass of solvent.
:::
