Fireworks get their colours from compounds of metals: strontium gives red, sodium gives yellow-orange and copper gives blue-green. The heat of the explosion pushes electrons in the metal atoms out to higher energy levels, further from the nucleus. As the electrons drop back, they give out the extra energy as light, and each element gives its own colours.

So electrons in an atom are not just a cloud around the nucleus: they sit at particular energy levels, called **shells**. In this lesson you will learn how the electrons of the first 20 elements fill these shells, and see how the arrangement of an atom's electrons decides where its element sits in the periodic table.

## Electron shells

:::definition Electron shell
An **electron shell**, or **energy level**, is a region at a particular distance from the nucleus where electrons are found. The shell closest to the nucleus has the lowest energy and fills first.
:::

Each shell can hold only a limited number of electrons. For the first 20 elements:

- the **first** shell holds up to **2** electrons;
- the **second** shell holds up to **8**;
- the **third** shell holds **8**, and then the next electrons go into the **fourth** shell, which takes the 19th and 20th electrons.

Electrons fill the shells from the inside out: a new shell starts only when the one inside it is full.

:::definition Electron arrangement
The **electron arrangement** of an atom lists the number of electrons in each shell, from the innermost shell outwards, separated by commas. Sodium has 11 electrons, arranged **2,8,1**: 2 in the first shell, 8 in the second and 1 in the third.
:::

A **shell diagram** shows the same thing as a picture: the nucleus at the centre, each shell as a ring, and each electron as a dot on its ring.

:::note Beyond the first 20 elements
The third shell can in fact hold up to 18 electrons. In potassium and calcium, though, the 19th and 20th electrons go into the fourth shell before the third shell fills any further. From element 21, scandium, onwards the third shell takes more electrons, as you will see in later courses. In this course you only need the first 20 elements: **2, 8, 8, 2**.
:::

:::example Writing an electron arrangement
Write the electron arrangement of chlorine, atomic number 17.
@step A neutral chlorine atom has 17 electrons.
@step The first shell takes 2, leaving $17 - 2 = 15$.
@step The second shell takes 8, leaving $15 - 8 = 7$.
@step The third shell takes the last 7.
@answer 2,8,7
:::

:::example The fourth shell
Write the electron arrangement of calcium, atomic number 20.
@step 20 electrons: the first shell takes 2, leaving 18.
@step The second shell takes 8, leaving 10.
@step The third shell takes 8, leaving 2.
@step The last 2 go into the fourth shell.
@answer 2,8,8,2
:::

## From an arrangement to an element

The electron arrangement also works backwards. Add up the electrons in all the shells to get the total number of electrons. A neutral atom has as many protons as electrons, so that total is the atomic number, and the atomic number tells you the element.

:::example Which element?
A neutral atom has the electron arrangement 2,8,5. Which element is it?
@step Electrons: $2 + 8 + 5 = 15$.
@step A neutral atom has as many protons as electrons, so the atomic number is 15.
@answer Phosphorus, the element with atomic number 15.
:::

A question may give you the nucleus instead. An atom with a mass number of 24 and 12 neutrons has $24 - 12 = 12$ protons, so as a neutral atom it has 12 electrons, arranged 2,8,2: it is magnesium.

:::

## Outer electrons and the periodic table

The electrons in the outermost shell matter most, because they are the ones that meet other atoms when atoms react.

:::definition Outer electrons
The **outer electrons**, also called **valence electrons**, are the electrons in the outermost occupied shell of an atom: the last number in its electron arrangement. Chlorine, 2,8,7, has 7 outer electrons.
:::

Look back at the table of arrangements and two patterns appear.

- **Down a group**, every element has the same number of outer electrons: lithium (2,1), sodium (2,8,1) and potassium (2,8,8,1) each have 1. That is why elements in the same group behave alike.
- **Across a period**, every element has the same number of occupied shells: all the elements in period 3 have 3 shells.

So, for the main groups,

- the **period** number is the number of occupied shells;
- in **groups 1 and 2**, the group number is the number of outer electrons;
- in **groups 13 to 18**, the group number is the number of outer electrons **plus 10**.

| Group | 1 | 2 | 13 | 14 | 15 | 16 | 17 | 18 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Outer electrons | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 | 6 | 7 | 8 |

Groups 3 to 12, the transition metals, sit in between from period 4 onwards; you will meet them in lesson 2.4.

:::note Two exceptions and another numbering
Helium has only 2 electrons, so its single shell is full with 2 outer electrons. It still belongs in group 18, with the other elements whose outer shells are full. Hydrogen, with 1 outer electron, is usually placed at the top of group 1. Some older periodic tables number only the main groups, from 1 to 8 (or I to VIII); on those tables the group number is simply the number of outer electrons.
:::

:::example Group and period from the arrangement
Sulfur has atomic number 16. In which group and which period is it?
@step The arrangement of 16 electrons is 2,8,6.
@step 3 occupied shells: period 3.
@step 6 outer electrons: group $6 + 10 = 16$.
@answer Group 16, period 3.
:::

:::example From the group and period back to the element
One of the first 20 elements is in group 17 and period 2. What is its atomic number?
@step Group 17 means $17 - 10 = 7$ outer electrons.
@step Period 2 means 2 shells. The inner shell is full with 2.
@step The arrangement is 2,7, so the atom has $2 + 7 = 9$ electrons.
@answer Atomic number 9: fluorine.
:::

:::mistake Outer electrons are the last number, not the total
Aluminium, 2,8,3, has 13 electrons in all but only **3** outer electrons. And the number of shells is how many numbers the arrangement has (3 for aluminium), not the largest number in it.
:::

:::summary
- Electrons occupy **shells** (energy levels) around the nucleus, filling from the inside out.
- For the first 20 elements the shells hold **2, 8, 8, 2**: the 19th and 20th electrons start the fourth shell.
- An **electron arrangement** such as 2,8,7 lists the electrons in each shell from the inside out; its numbers add up to the atomic number.
- The **outer electrons** are those in the outermost shell, the last number of the arrangement.
- The number of occupied shells is the **period**. The number of outer electrons gives the **group**: the same number for groups 1 and 2, and 10 more for groups 13 to 18.
- Elements in the same group have the same number of outer electrons.
:::
