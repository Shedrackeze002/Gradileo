Sodium is a soft metal that fizzes and darts across water, and chlorine is a choking green gas. Yet the salt on your dinner table is made of nothing but sodium and chlorine. The difference between the dangerous elements and the harmless salt comes down to a few electrons. In salt, every sodium atom has lost one electron and every chlorine atom has gained one, and the charged particles that result, called **ions**, behave very differently from the atoms they came from.

In this lesson you will see why atoms gain or lose electrons, predict the charge of the ion an element forms from its place in the periodic table, and count the electrons in an ion.

## Why atoms form ions

An atom has no overall charge. Its nucleus holds positive protons, and around it are just as many negative electrons, so the charges cancel. The electrons sit in shells: up to 2 in the first shell, then up to 8 in the second and 8 in the third. Sodium, with 11 electrons, is arranged 2,8,1.

The noble gases in group 18 (helium, neon, argon and the rest) hardly react at all. Each has a **full outer shell**: helium 2, neon 2,8 and argon 2,8,8. A full outer shell is a very stable arrangement, and atoms of many other elements reach one by gaining or losing electrons.

:::definition Ion
An **ion** is an atom (or a group of atoms) that has gained or lost electrons, so it has an electric charge. Its number of electrons no longer equals its number of protons.
:::

An atom takes the shorter route to a full outer shell. A sodium atom (2,8,1) could gain seven electrons or lose one; losing one is far easier, and it leaves 2,8, the same arrangement as neon. A chlorine atom (2,8,7) could lose seven or gain one; it gains one and becomes 2,8,8, like argon.

:::mistake Only the electrons change
When an atom becomes an ion, its nucleus stays exactly the same. A sodium ion still has 11 protons, so it is still sodium. If the number of protons changed, the atom would become a different element.
:::

## Positive and negative ions

**Metals lose electrons.** Metal atoms have only one, two or three electrons in their outer shell, and they lose them all. A sodium atom loses its single outer electron. It still has 11 protons but now only 10 electrons, so it has one more positive charge than negative: a charge of 1+. We write the sodium ion as $\ce{Na+}$.

**Non-metals gain electrons.** Non-metal atoms have five, six or seven outer electrons, and they gain enough to make eight. A chlorine atom gains one electron. It has 17 protons and now 18 electrons, one more negative charge than positive: a charge of 1−. We write the chloride ion as $\ce{Cl-}$.

:::definition Cation and anion
A **cation** is a positive ion. It forms when an atom **loses** electrons; metals form cations.

An **anion** is a negative ion. It forms when an atom **gains** electrons; non-metals form anions.
:::

Because the elements in a group have the same number of outer electrons, they all form ions with the same charge. The group tells you the charge:

| Group | 1 | 2 | 13 | 15 | 16 | 17 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Outer electrons | 1 | 2 | 3 | 5 | 6 | 7 |
| What the atom does | loses 1 | loses 2 | loses 3 | gains 3 | gains 2 | gains 1 |
| Charge of the ion | 1+ | 2+ | 3+ | 3− | 2− | 1− |
| Examples | $\ce{Li+}$, $\ce{Na+}$, $\ce{K+}$ | $\ce{Mg^2+}$, $\ce{Ca^2+}$ | $\ce{Al^3+}$ | $\ce{N^3-}$, $\ce{P^3-}$ | $\ce{O^2-}$, $\ce{S^2-}$ | $\ce{F-}$, $\ce{Cl-}$, $\ce{Br-}$, $\ce{I-}$ |

:::note Numbering the groups
Gradileo numbers the groups 1 to 18, across the whole periodic table. Some books skip the transition metals and number the main groups 1 to 8 instead, so group 13 is "group 3" and group 17 is "group 7" (and the noble gases may be "group 0" or "group 8"). Either way, for these main-group elements the outer electrons are 1, 2, 3, 5, 6 and 7, and the charges are the same.
:::

Two groups are missing from the table. Carbon and silicon, in group 14, would have to gain or lose four electrons, which is too many, so they share electrons instead (Lesson 3.3). The noble gases already have full outer shells, so they do not form ions at all.

:::example Predicting a positive ion
Magnesium is in group 2. Which ion does it form?
@step Group 2 atoms have 2 outer electrons.
@step Magnesium is a metal, so its atoms lose both outer electrons.
@step Each electron lost leaves one extra positive charge, so the charge is 2+.
@answer $\ce{Mg^2+}$, the magnesium ion.
:::

:::example Predicting a negative ion
Oxygen is in group 16. Which ion does it form?
@step Group 16 atoms have 6 outer electrons.
@step Oxygen is a non-metal, so its atoms gain $8 - 6 = 2$ electrons to fill the outer shell.
@step Each electron gained adds one negative charge, so the charge is 2−.
@answer $\ce{O^2-}$, the oxide ion.
:::

## Writing and naming ions

Write the charge at the top right of the symbol: the number first, then the sign. $\ce{Ca^2+}$ has a charge of 2+, and $\ce{N^3-}$ a charge of 3−. A charge of 1 is written with the sign alone: $\ce{K+}$, not $\ce{K^1+}$.

:::tip Typing a charge
In an answer box, type the charge after ^, number first and then the sign: `Mg^2+`, `O^2-` or `F^-`.
:::

Names follow two simple rules:

- A **positive ion keeps the name of its element**: $\ce{Na+}$ is the sodium ion and $\ce{Al^3+}$ is the aluminium ion (spelled *aluminum* in North America).
- A **negative ion changes the end of the element's name to -ide**: chlorine becomes chloride ($\ce{Cl-}$), oxygen becomes oxide ($\ce{O^2-}$), sulfur becomes sulfide ($\ce{S^2-}$), nitrogen becomes nitride ($\ce{N^3-}$) and phosphorus becomes phosphide ($\ce{P^3-}$).

:::mistake The sign goes after the number
Write $\ce{Mg^2+}$, not "Mg+2" or "Mg2". The number says how many charges and the sign says which kind, so both are needed, number first. And remember how the charge arose: a positive ion has **lost** electrons; it has not gained protons.
:::

You can also work out the ion from the atomic number alone. Write the electron arrangement, find the outer electrons, then decide whether the atom loses or gains.

:::example From the atomic number to the ion
Potassium has atomic number 19. Which ion does it form?
@step 19 electrons fill the shells as 2,8,8,1.
@step One outer electron means potassium is a metal in group 1, so the atom loses that electron.
@step It is left with 2,8,8, a full outer shell like argon, and a charge of 1+.
@answer $\ce{K+}$, the potassium ion.
:::

## Counting electrons in an ion

An ion has the same number of protons as its atom, the atomic number. Only the electrons change:

- a **positive** ion has lost electrons: electrons = atomic number − the size of the charge;
- a **negative** ion has gained electrons: electrons = atomic number + the size of the charge.

:::example Electrons in a positive ion
How many electrons are in $\ce{Al^3+}$, and how are they arranged? (Aluminium has atomic number 13.)
@step The atom has 13 protons and 13 electrons, arranged 2,8,3.
@step A charge of 3+ means it has lost 3 electrons: $13 - 3 = 10$.
@step The 3 outer electrons have gone, leaving the shells 2,8.
@answer 10 electrons, arranged 2,8 (like neon).
:::

:::example Electrons in a negative ion
How many electrons are in $\ce{S^2-}$, and how are they arranged? (Sulfur has atomic number 16.)
@step The atom has 16 electrons, arranged 2,8,6.
@step A charge of 2− means it has gained 2 electrons: $16 + 2 = 18$.
@step The 2 new electrons go into the outer shell, which now holds 8: 2,8,8.
@answer 18 electrons, arranged 2,8,8 (like argon).
:::

Notice that $\ce{Na+}$, $\ce{Mg^2+}$, $\ce{Al^3+}$, $\ce{O^2-}$ and $\ce{F-}$ all have 10 electrons arranged 2,8. Ions of different elements can share an electron arrangement; what tells them apart is the number of protons, and so the charge.

:::mistake Gaining electrons makes a negative ion
Electrons carry negative charge, so gaining them makes an ion **more negative**. $\ce{O^2-}$ has $8 + 2 = 10$ electrons, not $8 - 2 = 6$. If you are unsure, check: 8 protons and 10 electrons give $8 - 10 = -2$, the charge in the symbol.
:::

:::summary
- An ion is an atom that has gained or lost electrons, so it has a charge. The protons never change.
- Atoms gain or lose electrons to reach a full outer shell, like a noble gas.
- Metals lose electrons and form positive ions (cations); non-metals gain electrons and form negative ions (anions).
- Charges by group: 1+, 2+ and 3+ for groups 1, 2 and 13; 3−, 2− and 1− for groups 15, 16 and 17.
- Write the charge at the top right, number then sign: $\ce{Mg^2+}$, $\ce{Cl-}$. Negative ions end in -ide.
- Electrons in an ion: atomic number − charge for a positive ion, atomic number + charge for a negative ion. Many ions are arranged 2,8 (like neon) or 2,8,8 (like argon).
:::
