An indigestion tablet, a bag of lime spread on a farmer's field, and toothpaste all do the same job: they cancel out an acid. When an acid meets a base, the two react and neither is left over. The reaction is called **neutralization**, and it makes a new kind of compound, a **salt**.

In this lesson you will predict the products of neutralization, name the salt that an acid and a base make and write its formula, describe how acids react with metal carbonates, and see how to neutralize an acid safely.

## Acids meet bases

:::definition Neutralization
**Neutralization** is the reaction of an acid with a base. It makes a salt and water:

acid + base → salt + water
:::

:::definition Salt
A **salt** is an ionic compound made when the hydrogen of an acid is replaced by a metal. Sodium chloride, common table salt, is only one of many salts.
:::

In water, an acid forms hydrogen ions, $\ce{H+}$, and an alkali forms hydroxide ions, $\ce{OH-}$ (lesson 5.3). In neutralization, these ions join to make water, $\ce{H+ + OH- -> H2O}$. The ions that are left, the metal ion from the base and the negative ion from the acid, make up the salt.

:::example Hydrochloric acid and sodium hydroxide
What are the products when hydrochloric acid reacts with sodium hydroxide?
@step An acid and a base make a salt and water.
@step The salt contains the metal from the base, sodium, and the chloride from hydrochloric acid.
@step Word equation: hydrochloric acid + sodium hydroxide → sodium chloride + water. Symbol equation: $\ce{HCl + NaOH -> NaCl + H2O}$.
@answer Sodium chloride and water
:::

## Naming salts

A salt's name has two parts:

1. The **first word** is the metal from the base: sodium from sodium hydroxide, magnesium from magnesium oxide.
2. The **second word** comes from the acid:

| Acid | Formula | Salts it makes | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| hydrochloric acid | $\ce{HCl}$ | chlorides | sodium chloride |
| sulfuric acid | $\ce{H2SO4}$ | sulfates | magnesium sulfate |
| nitric acid | $\ce{HNO3}$ | nitrates | potassium nitrate |

Metal oxides neutralize acids in just the same way as metal hydroxides: the products are still a salt and water.

:::example Sulfuric acid and magnesium oxide
Write the word equation for the reaction of sulfuric acid with magnesium oxide.
@step The metal from the base is magnesium.
@step Sulfuric acid makes sulfates.
@answer sulfuric acid + magnesium oxide → magnesium sulfate + water
:::

:::example Working backwards
Which acid and which metal hydroxide react to make potassium nitrate and water?
@step The metal in the salt, potassium, comes from the base: potassium hydroxide.
@step Nitrates come from nitric acid.
@answer nitric acid + potassium hydroxide → potassium nitrate + water
:::

:::mistake Chloride, not chlorine
The second word of a salt's name is not the name of an element. Hydrochloric acid makes sodium **chloride**, not "sodium chlorine", and sulfuric acid makes sodium **sulfate**, not "sodium sulfur" or "sodium sulfide". A sulfide contains only sulfur; a sulfate also contains oxygen.
:::

## Formulas of salts

A salt is an ionic compound, so its formula comes from the charges of its ions, as in lesson 3.2: take just enough of each ion for the charges to add up to zero.

| Positive ion (from the base) | Negative ion (from the acid) |
|---|---|
| sodium, $\ce{Na+}$ | chloride, $\ce{Cl-}$ |
| potassium, $\ce{K+}$ | nitrate, $\ce{NO3-}$ |
| calcium, $\ce{Ca^2+}$ | sulfate, $\ce{SO4^2-}$ |
| magnesium, $\ce{Mg^2+}$ | |

:::example Calcium nitrate
Calcium hydroxide reacts with nitric acid. Name the salt and write its formula.
@step The salt is calcium nitrate, made of $\ce{Ca^2+}$ and $\ce{NO3-}$ ions.
@step One $\ce{Ca^2+}$ (2+) needs two $\ce{NO3-}$ (2 × 1− = 2−). A polyatomic ion taken more than once goes in brackets.
@answer Calcium nitrate, $\ce{Ca(NO3)2}$
:::

:::example Sodium sulfate
Sulfuric acid reacts with sodium hydroxide. Name the salt and write its formula, then the balanced symbol equation.
@step The salt is sodium sulfate, made of $\ce{Na+}$ and $\ce{SO4^2-}$ ions. Two $\ce{Na+}$ (2+) balance one $\ce{SO4^2-}$ (2−): $\ce{Na2SO4}$.
@step Two sodium ions need $\ce{2NaOH}$, and the two hydroxide ions make two water molecules.
@answer Sodium sulfate, $\ce{Na2SO4}$: $\ce{H2SO4 + 2NaOH -> Na2SO4 + 2H2O}$. Check: 2 Na, 1 S, 4 H and 6 O on each side.
:::

## Acids and metal carbonates

Metal carbonates, such as calcium carbonate (limestone and chalk), also neutralize acids. They make the same salt and water, and a gas as well: carbon dioxide. That is why a carbonate fizzes when it is dropped into an acid.

acid + metal carbonate → salt + water + carbon dioxide

To show that the gas is carbon dioxide, bubble it through **limewater**, a solution of calcium hydroxide. Carbon dioxide turns limewater cloudy, because tiny grains of solid calcium carbonate form.

:::example Calcium carbonate and hydrochloric acid
What forms when calcium carbonate reacts with hydrochloric acid?
@step A metal carbonate and an acid make a salt, water and carbon dioxide.
@step The salt contains calcium from the carbonate and chloride from hydrochloric acid.
@step Symbol equation: $\ce{CaCO3 + 2HCl -> CaCl2 + H2O + CO2}$. Check: 1 Ca, 1 C, 3 O, 2 H and 2 Cl on each side.
@answer calcium carbonate + hydrochloric acid → calcium chloride + water + carbon dioxide
:::

:::

## Neutralizing safely

Neutralization is useful wherever there is too much acid. An **antacid** tablet for indigestion contains a base such as magnesium hydroxide or calcium carbonate, which neutralizes some of the hydrochloric acid in the stomach. Farmers spread lime (calcium hydroxide or calcium carbonate) on soil that is too acidic for their crops.

To make a neutral salt solution in the laboratory, a student adds an alkali to an acid **a little at a time**, stirring, with a few drops of universal indicator. As the acid is used up, the pH rises and the indicator changes from red towards green. The student stops adding alkali as soon as the indicator just turns green, at pH 7. If too much alkali is added, the solution becomes basic and the indicator turns blue or purple. Evaporating some of the water from the neutral solution, then leaving it to cool, gives crystals of the salt.

A small spill of dilute acid on a lab bench can be neutralized with a weak base such as sodium hydrogen carbonate powder: when the fizzing stops, the acid is used up, and the spill can be wiped up with plenty of water. Acid or alkali on skin is different. Rinse it off at once with plenty of cold running water, and tell the teacher.

:::mistake More base is not safer
Pouring a concentrated alkali onto an acid spill, or onto skin, adds a second corrosive chemical, and neutralization gives out heat. Use a weak base on a bench spill, and only water on skin. In the laboratory, add alkali to acid a little at a time, so you can stop when the solution is neutral.
:::

:::summary
- **Neutralization**: acid + base → salt + water. The $\ce{H+}$ ions from the acid and the $\ce{OH-}$ ions from the alkali make water.
- A **salt** is named from the metal in the base and the acid: hydrochloric acid makes **chlorides**, sulfuric acid **sulfates** and nitric acid **nitrates**.
- Metal oxides and metal hydroxides are bases: hydrochloric acid + magnesium oxide → magnesium chloride + water.
- A salt's formula balances the charges of its ions: calcium nitrate is $\ce{Ca(NO3)2}$ and sodium sulfate is $\ce{Na2SO4}$.
- Acid + metal carbonate → salt + water + carbon dioxide. Carbon dioxide turns limewater cloudy.
- Neutralize safely: add alkali to acid a little at a time with an indicator, use a weak base on an acid spill, and rinse skin with plenty of cold running water.
:::
