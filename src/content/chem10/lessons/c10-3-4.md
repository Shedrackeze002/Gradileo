Why does salt stay solid in a hot frying pan, while the wax of a candle melts in the gentle heat of its own flame? Why are electrical wires made of copper, and why can diamond cut glass? The answers all come from the same place: the particles a substance is made of, and how strongly they hold on to each other.

In this lesson you will meet the four main kinds of structure, ionic, simple molecular, giant covalent and metallic, explain their melting points, electrical conductivity and solubility, and use test results to decide which structure an unknown substance has.

## Two questions that explain properties

Almost every property in this lesson comes down to two questions.

**How strong are the forces that must be overcome?** To melt a solid, its particles must be shaken loose from their fixed places. If the forces between them are strong, that takes a lot of energy, and the melting point is high. If they are weak, the substance melts at a low temperature, and it may already be a liquid or a gas at room temperature.

**Are there charged particles that are free to move?** An electric current is a flow of charge. A substance conducts electricity only if it contains charged particles (electrons or ions) that can move through it.

:::definition Melting point and conductivity
The **melting point** is the temperature at which a solid turns into a liquid. **Electrical conductivity** is how well a substance lets an electric current pass through it.
:::

## Ionic compounds

An ionic compound is a giant lattice of positive and negative ions, and every ion is attracted to all the oppositely charged ions around it.

- **High melting points.** Pulling the ions out of the lattice means overcoming a huge number of strong attractions. Sodium chloride melts at 801 °C, and magnesium oxide, with its 2+ and 2− ions, at about 2800 °C.
- **Conduct only when molten or dissolved.** The ions carry charge, but in the solid they are locked in place. When the compound melts (becomes **molten**) or dissolves in water, the ions are free to move, and it conducts.
- **Often soluble in water.** Many ionic compounds, such as sodium chloride and potassium nitrate, dissolve in water; some, such as calcium carbonate, do not.
- **Hard but brittle.** A sharp blow shifts one layer of ions along the next, bringing ions of the same charge face to face. They repel, and the crystal shatters.

## Simple molecular substances

:::definition Simple molecular substance
A **simple molecular substance** is made of small, separate molecules, such as $\ce{H2O}$, $\ce{CO2}$, $\ce{CH4}$ or $\ce{I2}$. The atoms inside each molecule are held by strong covalent bonds, but the forces **between** the molecules are weak.
:::

- **Low melting and boiling points.** Melting and boiling only separate the molecules from each other; the covalent bonds inside the molecules do not break. The weak forces between molecules are easily overcome, so many simple molecular substances are gases or liquids at room temperature: oxygen, methane, water. Solid ones melt at fairly low temperatures: iodine at 114 °C and sulfur at about 115 °C.
- **Do not conduct.** The molecules have no overall charge, and their electrons are held in bonds, so there is nothing to carry a current, whether the substance is solid, liquid or dissolved.
- **Solubility varies.** Some dissolve in water (sugar, ethanol); many do not (wax, iodine hardly at all). A sugar solution still does not conduct, because the sugar stays as neutral molecules.

:::mistake Melting does not break covalent bonds
When ice melts or water boils, the water molecules move apart, but each one is still $\ce{H2O}$: no O–H bond breaks. The low boiling point of a molecular substance shows that the forces between its molecules are weak, not that its covalent bonds are.
:::

## Giant covalent structures

:::definition Giant covalent structure
A **giant covalent structure** is a huge network of atoms, every one joined to its neighbours by covalent bonds, with no separate molecules. Diamond is a network of carbon atoms, each bonded to four others. Silicon dioxide, found as quartz and in sand, is a network of silicon and oxygen atoms.
:::

- **Very high melting points.** To melt the substance, many strong covalent bonds must break, not just weak forces between molecules. Diamond and quartz stay solid far above 1000 °C.
- **Hard.** Diamond is the hardest natural substance, which is why diamond-tipped blades can cut glass and rock.
- **Do not conduct.** Every outer electron is held in a bond between two atoms, so none is free to move.
- **Insoluble in water.** Water cannot break the network apart.

:::note Graphite is the exception
Graphite, the "lead" in a pencil, is also pure carbon. Its atoms form flat layers in which each carbon is bonded to only three others. The fourth outer electron of every atom is free to move along the layer, so graphite conducts electricity. The layers are held to each other only weakly, so they slide apart and leave a mark on paper.
:::

## Metals

:::definition Metallic bonding
In a metal, the atoms give up their outer electrons into a "sea" of electrons that move freely through the whole metal. **Metallic bonding** is the attraction between the positive metal ions and this sea of shared, moving electrons.
:::

- **Conduct as solids and when molten.** The free electrons carry the current, so copper and aluminium are used for electrical wires.
- **Malleable.** Layers of metal ions can slide over each other without the metal breaking, because the sea of electrons still holds them together. Metals can be hammered into sheets and drawn into wires.
- **Usually high melting points.** The attraction between the ions and the electron sea is strong. Iron melts at about 1540 °C, though a few metals melt easily: mercury is a liquid at room temperature.
- **Insoluble in water.** Metals do not dissolve, although a few very reactive ones, such as sodium, react with water.

## Telling the structures apart

| | Ionic | Simple molecular | Giant covalent | Metallic |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Particles | positive and negative ions in a lattice | small molecules | atoms in a covalent network | positive ions in a sea of electrons |
| Melting point | high | low | very high | usually high |
| Conducts as a solid? | no | no | no (except graphite) | yes |
| Conducts when molten? | yes | no | no | yes |
| Dissolves in water? | often, and the solution conducts | some do; the solution does not conduct | no | no |
| Examples | $\ce{NaCl}$, $\ce{MgO}$ | $\ce{H2O}$, $\ce{CO2}$, $\ce{I2}$ | diamond, $\ce{SiO2}$ | $\ce{Cu}$, $\ce{Fe}$ |

To identify an unknown substance from test results, work through the clues in this order:

1. **Does it conduct as a solid?** If yes, it is a metal.
2. **Does it conduct when molten (or dissolved)?** If yes, but not as a solid, it is ionic.
3. **It never conducts:** look at the melting point. Low means simple molecular; very high means giant covalent.

:::example Identifying one substance
A white solid melts at 1610 °C. It does not conduct electricity as a solid or when molten, and it does not dissolve in water. What kind of structure does it have?
@step It does not conduct as a solid, so it is not a metal.
@step It does not conduct when molten, so it has no ions: it is not ionic.
@step Its melting point is very high, so strong covalent bonds must break to melt it, not weak forces between molecules.
@answer It has a giant covalent structure.
:::

:::example Comparing four substances
Four substances were tested. Which is ionic, which is metallic, which is simple molecular and which is giant covalent?

| Substance | Melting point | Conducts as a solid? | Conducts when molten? | In water |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| P | 660 °C | yes | yes | does not dissolve |
| Q | −95 °C | no | no | does not dissolve |
| R | 2070 °C | no | yes | does not dissolve |
| S | 3500 °C | no | no | does not dissolve |
@step P conducts as a solid, so it is the metal.
@step R conducts only when molten, so it is ionic; its very high melting point does not change that.
@step Q and S never conduct. Q melts far below room temperature, so it is simple molecular; S has a very high melting point, so it is giant covalent.
@answer P is metallic, Q is simple molecular, R is ionic and S is giant covalent.
:::

:::mistake A high melting point alone is not enough
Ionic, giant covalent and most metallic substances all have high melting points, so the melting point cannot tell them apart. Look at conductivity first: as a solid, then when molten. Use the melting point last, to separate simple molecular from giant covalent.
:::

:::summary
- Strong forces between particles mean a high melting point; weak forces mean a low one.
- A substance conducts only if it has charged particles that can move: free electrons (metals, graphite) or ions that are molten or dissolved.
- Ionic: high melting point; conducts when molten or dissolved, not as a solid; often soluble; brittle.
- Simple molecular: low melting point, because only weak forces between molecules are overcome; never conducts.
- Giant covalent (diamond, silicon dioxide): very high melting point, because covalent bonds must break; does not conduct (except graphite); insoluble.
- Metallic: positive ions in a sea of moving electrons; conducts as a solid and when molten; malleable.
- To identify a structure, check conductivity as a solid, then when molten, then the melting point.
:::
