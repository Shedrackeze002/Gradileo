Look up chlorine in the periodic table and you will find the number 35.45 beside it. But an atom's mass number counts whole particles, so how can any atom of chlorine have a mass of 35.45? The answer is that it doesn't. Chlorine is a mixture of two kinds of atom, one a little heavier than the other, and 35.45 is their average.

In this lesson you will meet these different kinds of atom of the same element, called **isotopes**, and use them to explain where the numbers in the periodic table come from.

## Isotopes

All atoms of an element have the same number of protons; that is what makes them the same element. They do not all have the same number of neutrons.

:::definition Isotopes
**Isotopes** are atoms of the same element that have the same number of protons but different numbers of neutrons. Because their neutrons differ, isotopes have different mass numbers.
:::

An isotope is named by its element and its mass number. Hydrogen, the simplest element, has three isotopes:

| Isotope | Nuclide notation | Protons | Neutrons | Electrons |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| hydrogen-1 | $\ce{^{1}_{1}H}$ | 1 | 0 | 1 |
| hydrogen-2 | $\ce{^{2}_{1}H}$ | 1 | 1 | 1 |
| hydrogen-3 | $\ce{^{3}_{1}H}$ | 1 | 2 | 1 |

Almost all hydrogen atoms are hydrogen-1, which has no neutrons at all. Hydrogen-2 is sometimes called deuterium, and hydrogen-3, which is radioactive, is called tritium.

Carbon has isotopes too. Carbon-12 and carbon-13 are stable, while carbon-14 is radioactive. Living things take in carbon-14 all their lives; after they die, it slowly decays. By measuring how much carbon-14 is left in a piece of wood, bone or cloth, scientists can work out its age, for objects up to about 50 000 years old.

:::example Neutrons in two isotopes
Chlorine has two common isotopes, chlorine-35 and chlorine-37. The atomic number of chlorine is 17. How many protons, neutrons and electrons does a neutral atom of each isotope have?
@step Both are chlorine, so both have 17 protons and, when neutral, 17 electrons.
@step Chlorine-35: neutrons $= 35 - 17 = 18$.
@step Chlorine-37: neutrons $= 37 - 17 = 20$.
@answer Both have 17 protons and 17 electrons. Chlorine-35 has 18 neutrons and chlorine-37 has 20.
:::

### Isotopes react in the same way

When atoms react, it is their electrons that meet and rearrange; the nucleus is not involved. Isotopes of an element have the same number of electrons, arranged in the same way, so they have the **same chemical properties**. Chlorine-35 and chlorine-37 both react with sodium to make sodium chloride, and you could not tell the two kinds of salt apart by how they behave in a reaction.

Isotopes do differ in **physical** properties that depend on mass, such as density, and some isotopes are radioactive while others are not.

:::mistake The same mass number does not make isotopes
Argon-40, $\ce{^{40}_{18}Ar}$, and calcium-40, $\ce{^{40}_{20}Ca}$, have the same mass number, but they are **not** isotopes: one has 18 protons and the other 20, so they are different elements. Isotopes must have the same atomic number. Always check the number of protons first.
:::

## Relative atomic mass

A sample of chlorine from anywhere on Earth holds both isotopes, in almost exactly the same proportions every time: about three atoms of chlorine-35 for every atom of chlorine-37. The share of each isotope is called its **abundance**, usually given as a percentage of the atoms.

:::figure

:::

The mass that matters in chemistry is the average mass of all these atoms. Because the two isotopes are not present in equal numbers, it is a **weighted mean**: the more common isotope counts for more.

:::definition Relative atomic mass
The **relative atomic mass**, $A_r$, of an element is the weighted mean mass of its atoms, taking into account the abundance of each isotope. It is measured on a scale where an atom of carbon-12 has a mass of exactly 12, so it has no units.
:::

For an element with two isotopes,

$$A_r = \frac{(\text{mass of isotope 1} \times \text{percentage}) + (\text{mass of isotope 2} \times \text{percentage})}{100}$$

In simple calculations we take the mass of each isotope to be its mass number. More precise calculations use the measured mass of each isotope, which is very close to its mass number but not exactly equal to it.

:::example Chlorine
A sample of chlorine is 75% chlorine-35 and 25% chlorine-37. Taking the mass of each isotope to be its mass number, calculate the relative atomic mass of chlorine to the nearest tenth.
@step Multiply each mass by its percentage: $35 \times 75 = 2625$ and $37 \times 25 = 925$.
@step Add the results and divide by 100: $A_r = \dfrac{2625 + 925}{100} = \dfrac{3550}{100} = 35.5$.
@step Check: most of the atoms are chlorine-35, so the answer should be closer to 35 than to 37. It is.
@answer $A_r = 35.5$
:::

:::mistake Do not just average the mass numbers
Adding 35 and 37 and dividing by 2 gives 36, which would be right only if there were equal numbers of the two isotopes. There are three times as many chlorine-35 atoms, so the relative atomic mass is pulled towards 35. Always weight each mass by its abundance.
:::

:::example Copper, with measured masses
Copper is 69.2% copper-63, with a relative mass of 62.93, and 30.8% copper-65, with a relative mass of 64.93. Calculate the relative atomic mass of copper to the nearest hundredth.
@step $62.93 \times 69.2 = 4354.756$ and $64.93 \times 30.8 = 1999.844$.
@step $A_r = \dfrac{4354.756 + 1999.844}{100} = \dfrac{6354.6}{100} = 63.546$.
@answer $A_r = 63.55$, which is the value you will find for copper in the periodic table.
:::

:::tip The percentages add up to 100
Sometimes a question gives the abundance of only one isotope and says "the rest" are the other. Subtract from 100 to find the missing percentage before you start.
:::

## Working backwards: finding an abundance

If you know the relative atomic mass and the masses of the two isotopes, you can work back to the abundances. Let $x$ be the percentage of one isotope; the other then makes up $100 - x$ percent.

:::example Boron
Boron has two isotopes, boron-10 and boron-11. A sample of boron has a relative atomic mass of 10.8. Taking the mass of each isotope to be its mass number, find the percentage of boron-10 atoms.
@step Let $x$ be the percentage of boron-10, so $100 - x$ percent are boron-11.
@step $\dfrac{10x + 11(100 - x)}{100} = 10.8$
@step Multiply both sides by 100: $10x + 1100 - 11x = 1080$, so $1100 - x = 1080$.
@step $x = 20$
@answer 20% of the atoms are boron-10, and 80% are boron-11. Check: $\dfrac{10 \times 20 + 11 \times 80}{100} = \dfrac{1080}{100} = 10.8$.
:::

A quick estimate helps you check: 10.8 is much closer to 11 than to 10, so boron-11 must be the more common isotope.

:::summary
- **Isotopes** are atoms of the same element with the same number of protons but different numbers of neutrons, and so different mass numbers.
- Isotopes of an element have the same electrons, so they have the same chemical properties.
- Atoms with the same mass number but different numbers of protons are different elements, not isotopes.
- The **relative atomic mass**, $A_r$, is the weighted mean mass of an element's atoms: $A_r = \dfrac{(m_1 \times p_1) + (m_2 \times p_2)}{100}$, where $m$ is each isotope's mass and $p$ its percentage abundance.
- The relative atomic mass lies closer to the mass of the more abundant isotope.
- To find an abundance, let $x$ be one percentage and $100 - x$ the other, and solve.
:::
