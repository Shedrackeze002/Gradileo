Lemon juice and vinegar taste sour. Soap feels slippery, and an oven cleaner can strip baked-on grease. Pure water does neither. Chemists sort solutions like these into **acids**, **bases** and **neutral** solutions, and measure how acidic or basic each one is on a single scale, the **pH scale**. Coloured dyes called indicators make that measurement quick and easy.

In this lesson you will classify solutions as acidic, neutral or basic from their pH, put solutions in order of acidity, see why one step on the pH scale is a tenfold change, and predict the colours of litmus, universal indicator and phenolphthalein.

## Acids and bases

:::definition Acid
An **acid** is a substance that forms hydrogen ions, $\ce{H+}$, when it dissolves in water. Acids react with many metals, giving off hydrogen gas, and with metal carbonates, giving off carbon dioxide.
:::

:::definition Base and alkali
A **base** is a substance that reacts with an acid and cancels it out. Metal oxides and metal hydroxides are bases. An **alkali** is a base that dissolves in water, forming hydroxide ions, $\ce{OH-}$. Sodium hydroxide and potassium hydroxide are alkalis.
:::

A solution that is neither acidic nor basic is **neutral**. Pure water, salt (sodium chloride) solution and sugar solution are neutral.

| Acidic | Basic | Neutral |
|---|---|---|
| lemon juice (citric acid) | soapy water | pure water |
| vinegar (acetic acid) | baking soda (sodium hydrogen carbonate) solution | salt solution |
| stomach acid (hydrochloric acid) | ammonia solution | sugar solution |
| fizzy drinks (carbonic acid) | oven cleaner (sodium hydroxide) | |

:::note Never taste or touch to test
A sour taste and a slippery feel are clues in the kitchen, but acids and alkalis in the laboratory can burn skin and damage eyes. Never taste or touch a chemical to find out what it is. Wear eye protection, and use an indicator instead.
:::

## The pH scale

:::definition pH scale
The **pH scale** shows how acidic or basic a solution is, usually on a scale from 0 to 14. A pH below 7 is **acidic**, a pH of exactly 7 is **neutral**, and a pH above 7 is **basic**. The further the pH is from 7, the more acidic or basic the solution.
:::

Pure water at room temperature has a pH of 7. The number line shows the pH of some everyday solutions. Real samples vary, so these are typical values.

To put solutions in order of acidity, compare their pH values: the **lower** the pH, the **more acidic** the solution; the **higher** the pH, the **more basic**.

:::example Putting solutions in order
Put these in order from the most acidic to the most basic: vinegar (pH 2.8), milk (pH 6.6), oven cleaner (pH 13.5) and lemon juice (pH 2.2).
@step The most acidic has the lowest pH: lemon juice, at 2.2.
@step Then vinegar (2.8) and milk (6.6), and last the highest pH, oven cleaner (13.5).
@answer Lemon juice, vinegar, milk, oven cleaner

## Ten times for every step

The pH scale is not like a ruler, where each step is the same amount. Each step of 1 pH unit is a change of **ten times** in acidity. A solution with a pH of 3 is 10 times more acidic than one with a pH of 4, and $10 \times 10 = 100$ times more acidic than one with a pH of 5.

| Difference in pH | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Times more acidic | $10$ | $100$ | $1000$ | $10\,000$ |

:::example Comparing two acids
Solution A has a pH of 2, and solution B has a pH of 5. How many times more acidic is solution A than solution B?
@step The pH values differ by $5 - 2 = 3$.
@step Each unit is a factor of 10: $10 \times 10 \times 10 = 1000$.
@answer Solution A is 1000 times more acidic.
:::

Adding water to an acid spreads it out, so it becomes less acidic and its pH rises towards 7. Diluting an acid until its pH rises from 2 to 3 makes it 10 times less acidic.

:::mistake Multiply, do not add
A difference of 3 pH units does not mean 3 times, or 30 times, more acidic. Multiply by 10 once for each unit: $10 \times 10 \times 10 = 1000$ times.
:::

## Indicators

:::definition Indicator
An **indicator** is a dye that has a different colour in acidic and basic solutions. Litmus, phenolphthalein and universal indicator are common indicators.
:::

**Litmus** comes as red or blue paper. Acids turn blue litmus red, and bases turn red litmus blue. In a neutral solution neither paper changes colour. **Phenolphthalein** is a liquid indicator: it is colourless in acidic and neutral solutions and turns pink in basic ones.

| Indicator | Acidic | Neutral | Basic |
|---|---|---|---|
| blue litmus paper | turns red | stays blue | stays blue |
| red litmus paper | stays red | stays red | turns blue |
| phenolphthalein | colourless | colourless | pink |

**Universal indicator** is a mixture of dyes that changes through a whole range of colours, so it shows roughly what the pH is, not just whether a solution is acidic or basic. Charts differ a little from one maker to another; this course uses this one:

:::

:::example Predicting colours
A solution has a pH of 12. What colour does it turn red litmus paper, phenolphthalein and universal indicator?
@step A pH of 12 is above 7, so the solution is basic.
@step Bases turn red litmus blue and phenolphthalein pink.
@step On the chart, pH 12 is in the range 11 to 14.
@answer Red litmus turns blue, phenolphthalein turns pink, and universal indicator turns purple.
:::

:::mistake Colourless does not mean acidic
Phenolphthalein is colourless in acidic **and** neutral solutions, so a colourless result does not prove a solution is acidic. In the same way, red litmus stays red in both acidic and neutral solutions. To show that a solution is acidic, use blue litmus, which turns red, or universal indicator.
:::

## Using two indicators

Each indicator changes colour over its own small range of pH. Below the range it shows one colour, above it another, and in between a mixture of the two. Testing a solution with two indicators can narrow down its pH.

| Indicator | Colour at low pH | Changes between | Colour at high pH |
|---|---|---|---|
| methyl orange | red | pH 3.1 and 4.4 (orange) | yellow |
| bromothymol blue | yellow | pH 6.0 and 7.6 (green) | blue |
| phenolphthalein | colourless | pH 8.2 and 10.0 (pale pink) | pink |

:::example Two clues
A solution is tested with two indicators: methyl orange is yellow in it, and phenolphthalein is colourless. What is its pH?
@step Methyl orange is yellow, so the pH is above 4.4.
@step Phenolphthalein is colourless, so the pH is below 8.2.
@answer The pH is between 4.4 and 8.2.
:::

:::summary
- An **acid** forms $\ce{H+}$ ions in water. A **base** cancels out an acid; an **alkali** is a base that dissolves in water, forming $\ce{OH-}$ ions.
- On the **pH scale**, below 7 is acidic, 7 is neutral and above 7 is basic. The lower the pH, the more acidic; the higher the pH, the more basic.
- Each step of 1 pH unit is a **tenfold** change: a difference of 2 units is 100 times, and of 3 units 1000 times.
- **Litmus**: acids turn blue litmus red, bases turn red litmus blue. **Phenolphthalein** is colourless in acidic and neutral solutions, pink in basic ones.
- **Universal indicator** is red, orange or yellow in acids (red is the most acidic), green when neutral, and blue or purple in bases.
- Two indicators with known colour-change ranges can narrow down a pH: the pH must fit both colours.
:::
