Everything you can touch, from the water in a glass to the air in your lungs, is made of atoms. Atoms are almost unimaginably small: the full stop at the end of this sentence is more than a million atoms wide. For a long time people thought atoms were the smallest things there could be, tiny solid balls that could not be split.

Experiments since the end of the 1800s have shown that atoms are built from even smaller particles. In this lesson you will meet those particles, find out where each one sits inside the atom, and learn to count them from two numbers that the periodic table and the name of an atom give you.

## Three particles

An **atom** is the smallest particle of an element that still has the chemical properties of that element. Atoms are made of three kinds of smaller particle: **protons**, **neutrons** and **electrons**. (The commonest kind of hydrogen atom is the one exception: it has a proton and an electron, but no neutron.)

:::definition Nucleus
The **nucleus** is the tiny, dense centre of an atom. It holds the protons and the neutrons. The electrons move around the nucleus in regions called **shells**.
:::

The particles are far too small to weigh in grams in an everyday way, so chemists compare them with one another. A proton is given a **relative mass** of 1, and the other masses are measured against it. In the same way, the charge on a proton is called $+1$, and the other charges are compared with it.

| Particle | Relative charge | Relative mass | Where it is found |
|---|---|---|---|
| proton | $+1$ | 1 | in the nucleus |
| neutron | $0$ (no charge) | 1 | in the nucleus |
| electron | $-1$ | about $\tfrac{1}{1836}$ | in shells around the nucleus |

A few things stand out in this table.

- Protons are **p**ositive and neutrons are **n**eutral; electrons are negative.
- Protons and neutrons have almost the same mass. An electron is about 1836 times lighter, so light that its mass hardly counts.
- Both of the heavy particles are in the nucleus, so **almost all the mass of an atom is in its nucleus**.

## An atom is mostly empty space

In 1909, a team working with Ernest Rutherford fired tiny, fast-moving positive particles at a sheet of gold foil far thinner than a human hair. Almost all of the particles passed straight through, as if the foil were not there. A few were pushed off course, and a very few bounced almost straight back. Rutherford explained this in 1911: each atom has a tiny, heavy, positively charged nucleus, with a great deal of empty space around it. Most of the particles passed through the empty space; the few that came close to a nucleus were pushed away by its positive charge.

The nucleus really is tiny. It is about 100 000 times smaller across than the whole atom. If an atom were the size of a sports stadium, its nucleus would be about the size of a pea on the centre spot, and the electrons would move through the stands and the space above them.

:::note Shells
Electrons are not scattered at random. They move in **shells**, also called **energy levels**, at different distances from the nucleus. Lesson 2.3 shows how the electrons fill the shells.
:::

### Why an atom has no overall charge

The nucleus is positive, because it holds the protons. An atom as a whole, though, is **neutral**: it has no overall charge. That is because a neutral atom has exactly as many electrons as protons, and each electron's charge of $-1$ cancels the $+1$ of one proton.

:::example The charges in a lithium atom
An atom of lithium has 3 protons, 4 neutrons and 3 electrons. Find the relative charge of its nucleus and of the whole atom.
@step The nucleus holds the 3 protons and the 4 neutrons. The protons give $3 \times (+1) = +3$, and the neutrons add nothing.
@step The 3 electrons give $3 \times (-1) = -3$.
@step The whole atom: $(+3) + (-3) = 0$.
@answer The nucleus has a relative charge of $+3$; the whole atom has no overall charge.
:::

:::mistake Neutrons do not cancel the charge
It is tempting to think that the neutrons balance the protons, because they sit beside them in the nucleus. They cannot: neutrons have no charge at all. It is the **electrons** that balance the charge of the protons.
:::

:::mistake An electron's mass is tiny, not zero
Electrons do have mass, but it is so small that we usually leave it out when we work out the mass of an atom. An atom with 17 protons, 18 neutrons and 17 electrons has a relative mass of $17 + 18 = 35$; its electrons add only about $17 \times \tfrac{1}{1836} \approx 0.009$.
:::

## Atomic number and mass number

What makes an atom of carbon different from an atom of gold? Only the number of protons in its nucleus.

:::definition Atomic number
The **atomic number** is the number of protons in the nucleus of an atom. Every atom of the same element has the same atomic number: every carbon atom has 6 protons, and any atom with 6 protons is carbon. The periodic table lists the elements in order of atomic number.
:::

:::definition Mass number
The **mass number** is the number of protons plus the number of neutrons in the nucleus. It is always a whole number, because it counts particles.
:::

These two numbers are all you need to count the particles in a neutral atom:

- **protons** = atomic number
- **electrons** = protons = atomic number (because the atom is neutral)
- **neutrons** = mass number − atomic number

An atom can be named by its element and its mass number, such as **sodium-23**. It can also be written in **nuclide notation**: the symbol of the element with the mass number at the top left and the atomic number at the bottom left.

$$\ce{^{23}_{11}Na} \qquad \text{mass number } 23, \text{ atomic number } 11$$

:::example Counting particles from a name
How many protons, neutrons and electrons are in a neutral atom of sodium-23? (The atomic number of sodium is 11.)
@step The atomic number is 11, so the atom has 11 protons.
@step The atom is neutral, so it also has 11 electrons.
@step The mass number is 23. Neutrons $= 23 - 11 = 12$.
@answer 11 protons, 12 neutrons and 11 electrons.
:::

:::example Counting particles from nuclide notation
How many protons, neutrons and electrons are in a neutral atom of $\ce{^{31}_{15}P}$?
@step The bottom number is the atomic number, 15: the atom has 15 protons.
@step The top number is the mass number, 31. Neutrons $= 31 - 15 = 16$.
@step The atom is neutral, so it has 15 electrons.
@answer 15 protons, 16 neutrons and 15 electrons.
:::

:::mistake Which number is which?
In nuclide notation the **larger** number is the mass number, because it counts the protons and the neutrons together. If you read $\ce{^{31}_{15}P}$ as 31 protons, you have swapped the two numbers. The mass number is never smaller than the atomic number.
:::

## Identifying an element

Because the number of protons decides the element, you can always work out which element an atom is once you know its protons. Sometimes a question gives the protons directly; sometimes you have to find them first.

- From the **electrons**: a neutral atom has as many protons as electrons.
- From the **mass number and neutrons**: protons = mass number − neutrons.

Then look up the element with that atomic number.

:::example Which element is it?
An atom has a mass number of 27 and contains 14 neutrons. Which element is it? (Atomic numbers: silicon 14, aluminium 13, cobalt 27.)
@step Protons $= 27 - 14 = 13$.
@step The element with atomic number 13 is aluminium.
@answer Aluminium (spelled aluminum in North America), $\ce{^{27}_{13}Al}$. (Silicon would need 14 protons, and cobalt 27; neither the neutrons nor the mass number tells you the element.)
:::

:::tip Count on the protons
Whatever a question gives you, aim first for the number of protons. Once you have it, everything else follows: the element, the electrons of the neutral atom, and, with the mass number, the neutrons.
:::

:::

:::summary
- An atom has a tiny, dense **nucleus** holding **protons** (charge $+1$, mass 1) and **neutrons** (charge 0, mass 1). **Electrons** (charge $-1$, mass about $\tfrac{1}{1836}$) move in shells around it.
- Almost all of an atom's mass is in its nucleus; most of its volume is empty space.
- A neutral atom has as many electrons as protons, so its charges cancel.
- **Atomic number** = number of protons; it decides the element.
- **Mass number** = protons + neutrons, so neutrons = mass number − atomic number.
- In nuclide notation, such as $\ce{^{23}_{11}Na}$, the mass number is at the top left and the atomic number at the bottom left.
:::
