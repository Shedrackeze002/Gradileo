Snap the bag of a hand warmer and it soon feels hot; squeeze an instant cold pack and it turns icy. Both are chemical changes, but one gives energy to its surroundings and the other takes energy from them. Every reaction moves energy one way or the other, and that is often the reason we carry it out: we burn fuels for their heat, not for the carbon dioxide.

In this lesson you will classify changes as exothermic or endothermic, from a temperature change, from everyday examples and from the sign of the energy change, explain the energy change with bond breaking and bond making, and read energy level diagrams, including how a catalyst changes them.

## Exothermic and endothermic

When chemists talk about energy changes, they split the world in two: the **system**, the reacting substances, and the **surroundings**, everything else, including the water they are dissolved in, the container, the thermometer and the air.

:::definition Exothermic
An **exothermic** change gives out energy to its surroundings, usually as heat. The surroundings get warmer, so the temperature of the mixture rises.
:::

:::definition Endothermic
An **endothermic** change takes in energy from its surroundings. The surroundings get colder, so the temperature of the mixture falls, or the change keeps going only while energy is supplied, by heating, by light or by electricity.
:::

| Exothermic | Endothermic |
|---|---|
| burning fuels (combustion) | photosynthesis (takes in light) |
| respiration in living cells | thermal decomposition, such as heating calcium carbonate |
| neutralization of an acid by an alkali | splitting water with an electric current |
| a metal reacting with an acid | citric acid reacting with sodium hydrogen carbonate |
| a hand warmer: iron reacting with oxygen | an instant cold pack: a solid dissolving in water |

The **energy change** of a reaction is the energy the system gains. It is **negative** for an exothermic reaction, which loses energy to its surroundings, and **positive** for an endothermic one. An energy change of $-890\ \text{kJ}$ means $890\ \text{kJ}$ is given out.

:::example Reading a thermometer
Sodium hydrogen carbonate powder is stirred into citric acid solution in a polystyrene cup. The temperature falls from $21.0^\circ\text{C}$ to $14.5^\circ\text{C}$. Is the reaction exothermic or endothermic?
@step The temperature of the mixture falls, so the reaction takes energy in from its surroundings.
@answer Endothermic.
:::

:::check c10-exo-endo 1
:::

To measure a temperature change, record the temperature before the reactants are mixed and the highest (or lowest) temperature reached afterwards. A polystyrene cup with a lid keeps heat from leaking in or out.

$$\text{temperature change} = \text{final temperature} - \text{initial temperature}$$

:::example Neutralization in a cup
Dilute hydrochloric acid is at $19.5^\circ\text{C}$. Sodium hydroxide solution at the same temperature is added, and the highest temperature reached is $26.3^\circ\text{C}$. Find the temperature change and classify the reaction.
@step Temperature change $= 26.3 - 19.5 = 6.8^\circ\text{C}$
@step The change is positive: the mixture got warmer, so energy was given out.
@answer $+6.8^\circ\text{C}$: exothermic.
:::

:::mistake Cold is not "taking in cold"
In an endothermic change the mixture feels cold because it is taking heat **from** your hand and from everything around it. Nothing called "cold" is given out. Always describe the energy flow: into the system for endothermic, out of the system for exothermic.
:::

:::check c10-exo-endo 2
:::

## Bonds and energy

Why do some reactions give out energy and others take it in? A reaction breaks the bonds holding the reactant particles together and makes the new bonds of the products.

- **Breaking bonds takes in energy.** You have to pull the atoms apart.
- **Making bonds gives out energy.** The atoms pull together.

If making the new bonds gives out more energy than breaking the old ones takes in, the extra is released and the reaction is exothermic. If breaking takes in more than making gives out, the reaction is endothermic.

$$\text{energy change} = \text{energy taken in breaking bonds} - \text{energy given out making bonds}$$

:::example Bonds broken and made
In a reaction, breaking the bonds of the reactants takes in $1370\ \text{kJ}$, and making the bonds of the products gives out $1850\ \text{kJ}$. Find the energy change and classify the reaction.
@step Energy change $= 1370 - 1850 = -480\ \text{kJ}$
@step More energy is given out making bonds than is taken in breaking them.
@answer $-480\ \text{kJ}$: exothermic.
:::

:::

## Energy level diagrams

An **energy level diagram** shows the energy of the reactants and the products as the reaction goes from left to right. Even an exothermic reaction does not start by itself: a match must be struck before it burns. The reactants first have to climb an energy hill, shown as a hump, before they can become products.

:::definition Activation energy
The **activation energy** is the smallest amount of energy that particles must have when they collide for the reaction to happen. On an energy level diagram it is the height of the hump above the reactants.
:::

In this exothermic reaction the products are **lower** than the reactants: the difference has been given out to the surroundings. Read the levels against the energy scale:

- **Activation energy** = top of the hump − reactants $= 350 - 250 = 100\ \text{kJ}$
- **Energy change** = products − reactants $= 100 - 250 = -150\ \text{kJ}$

In an endothermic reaction the products are **higher** than the reactants: energy has been taken in from the surroundings, and the energy change is positive, here $250 - 100 = +150\ \text{kJ}$.

:::example Reading a diagram
On an energy level diagram, the reactants are at $120\ \text{kJ}$, the top of the hump is at $200\ \text{kJ}$ and the products are at $40\ \text{kJ}$. Find the activation energy and the energy change, and say whether the reaction is exothermic or endothermic.
@step Activation energy $= 200 - 120 = 80\ \text{kJ}$
@step Energy change $= 40 - 120 = -80\ \text{kJ}$
@answer Activation energy $80\ \text{kJ}$; energy change $-80\ \text{kJ}$. The products are lower than the reactants, so the reaction is exothermic.
:::

:::mistake Measure the hump from the reactants
The activation energy is the climb **from the reactants** to the top of the hump, not the height of the hump above zero, and not the climb from the products. In the example above, the top of the hump is at $200\ \text{kJ}$, but the activation energy is only $80\ \text{kJ}$.
:::

## Catalysts on an energy level diagram

A **catalyst** speeds up a reaction without being used up (lesson 4.5). It does this by giving the reaction a different route with a lower activation energy. On the diagram, the hump is lower, but the reactants and the products stay exactly where they were, so the energy change does not change.

:::summary
- An **exothermic** change gives out energy: the surroundings get warmer and the energy change is negative. An **endothermic** change takes energy in: the surroundings get colder (or energy must be supplied) and the energy change is positive.
- Temperature change = final temperature − initial temperature. A rise means exothermic; a fall means endothermic.
- Breaking bonds takes in energy; making bonds gives it out. Energy change = energy taken in − energy given out.
- On an **energy level diagram**, exothermic products are lower than the reactants and endothermic products are higher. Energy change = products − reactants.
- The **activation energy** is the height of the hump above the reactants. A catalyst lowers the hump but does not change the energy change.
:::
