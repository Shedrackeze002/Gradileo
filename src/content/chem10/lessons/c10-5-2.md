A chemist who needs $0.50\ \text{mol}$ of water cannot count out $3.01 \times 10^{23}$ molecules, but can weigh out $9.0\ \text{g}$ on a balance in a few seconds. The molar mass is the bridge between the mass you can weigh and the number of particles you cannot see. A balanced equation then works like a recipe: it says how many moles of each substance react and form.

In this lesson you will convert between mass and moles, go on from a mass to a number of particles, and use the mole ratio of a balanced equation to find the moles, and then the mass, of another substance.

## From mass to moles and back

:::definition Amount of substance
The **amount of substance**, $n$, is how much of a substance there is, measured in moles. It is found from the mass, $m$, in grams, and the molar mass, $M$, in grams per mole:

$$n = \dfrac{m}{M} \qquad m = n \times M$$
:::

Divide a mass by the molar mass to get moles. Multiply moles by the molar mass to get grams.

:::example Mass to moles
How many moles are in $54.0\ \text{g}$ of water, $\ce{H2O}$? Give the answer to the nearest hundredth. The molar mass of water is $18.0\ \text{g/mol}$.
@step $n = \dfrac{m}{M} = \dfrac{54.0\ \text{g}}{18.0\ \text{g/mol}}$
@answer $3.00\ \text{mol}$
:::

:::example Moles to mass
What is the mass of $0.25\ \text{mol}$ of sodium chloride, $\ce{NaCl}$, to the nearest tenth? The molar mass of sodium chloride is $58.5\ \text{g/mol}$.
@step $m = n \times M = 0.25\ \text{mol} \times 58.5\ \text{g/mol} = 14.625\ \text{g}$
@answer $14.6\ \text{g}$
:::

:::tip Let the units decide
$\text{g} \div \text{g/mol} = \text{mol}$, and $\text{mol} \times \text{g/mol} = \text{g}$. If your units come out as $\text{mol/g}$ or $\text{g}^2/\text{mol}$, you have divided the wrong way round.
:::

When the molar mass is not given, work it out first from the molar masses of the elements, as in lesson 5.1.

:::example Molar mass first
A piece of chalk is $25.0\ \text{g}$ of calcium carbonate, $\ce{CaCO3}$. Find the molar mass to the nearest tenth, then the amount in moles to the nearest hundredth. Use these molar masses: Ca $40.1\ \text{g/mol}$, C $12.0\ \text{g/mol}$ and O $16.0\ \text{g/mol}$.
@step $M = 40.1 + 12.0 + 3 \times 16.0 = 40.1 + 12.0 + 48.0 = 100.1\ \text{g/mol}$
@step $n = \dfrac{25.0}{100.1} = 0.2498\ \text{mol}$
@answer $M = 100.1\ \text{g/mol}$ and $n = 0.25\ \text{mol}$
:::

## From mass to particles

Moles connect mass to particles, so a mass can be turned into a number of particles in two steps: divide by the molar mass, then multiply by Avogadro's number.

$$m \xrightarrow{\ \div M\ } n \xrightarrow{\ \times 6.02 \times 10^{23}\ } N$$

:::example Molecules in a mass of water
How many molecules are in $9.0\ \text{g}$ of water, $\ce{H2O}$? Give the answer in scientific notation to three significant figures. Use these molar masses: H $1.0\ \text{g/mol}$ and O $16.0\ \text{g/mol}$.
@step $M(\ce{H2O}) = 2 \times 1.0 + 16.0 = 18.0\ \text{g/mol}$
@step $n = \dfrac{9.0}{18.0} = 0.50\ \text{mol}$
@step $N = 0.50 \times 6.02 \times 10^{23} = 3.01 \times 10^{23}$
@answer $3.01 \times 10^{23}$ molecules
:::

:::mistake Go through moles
Avogadro's number counts the particles in one **mole**, not in one gram. Multiplying the mass straight by $6.02 \times 10^{23}$, as in $9.0 \times 6.02 \times 10^{23}$, gives an answer 18 times too big for water. Change the mass to moles first.
:::

## Mole ratios

In lesson 4.2 you balanced equations with coefficients, the big numbers in front of the formulas. The coefficients count particles, so they also count moles. The equation for hydrogen burning,

$$\ce{2H2 + O2 -> 2H2O}$$

says that 2 molecules of hydrogen react with 1 molecule of oxygen to make 2 molecules of water. Multiply every number by $6.02 \times 10^{23}$ and it says the same thing in moles: **2 mol of $\ce{H2}$ react with 1 mol of $\ce{O2}$ to make 2 mol of $\ce{H2O}$**.

:::definition Mole ratio
The **mole ratio** of two substances in a reaction is the ratio of their coefficients in the balanced equation. In $\ce{2H2 + O2 -> 2H2O}$, the mole ratio of $\ce{H2}$ to $\ce{O2}$ is $2 : 1$.
:::

To find the moles of one substance from the moles of another, multiply by the ratio of their coefficients, with the substance you want on top:

$$n(\text{wanted}) = n(\text{given}) \times \dfrac{\text{coefficient of wanted}}{\text{coefficient of given}}$$

:::example Making ammonia
Nitrogen and hydrogen react to make ammonia: $\ce{N2 + 3H2 -> 2NH3}$. How many moles of $\ce{NH3}$ form from $6\ \text{mol}$ of $\ce{H2}$?
@step The mole ratio of $\ce{H2}$ to $\ce{NH3}$ is $3 : 2$.
@step $n(\ce{NH3}) = 6 \times \dfrac{2}{3} = 4$
@answer $4\ \text{mol}$
:::

:::example Burning magnesium
Magnesium burns in oxygen: $\ce{2Mg + O2 -> 2MgO}$. How many moles of $\ce{O2}$ react with $0.36\ \text{mol}$ of $\ce{Mg}$? Give the answer to the nearest hundredth.
@step The mole ratio of $\ce{Mg}$ to $\ce{O2}$ is $2 : 1$.
@step $n(\ce{O2}) = 0.36 \times \dfrac{1}{2} = 0.18$
@answer $0.18\ \text{mol}$
:::

:::mistake Ratios are in moles, not grams
The coefficients compare **moles**. In $\ce{2H2 + O2 -> 2H2O}$, 2 mol of hydrogen react with 1 mol of oxygen, but 2 g of hydrogen do not react with 1 g of oxygen. And the coefficients are a mole ratio only once the equation is balanced: balance it first.
:::

## From mass to mass

A balance measures grams, but an equation counts moles. To find the mass of a product from the mass of a reactant, go through moles in three steps:

1. **Mass to moles**: $n = \dfrac{m}{M}$ for the substance you know.
2. **Mole ratio**: use the coefficients to find the moles of the substance you want.
3. **Moles to mass**: $m = n \times M$ for the substance you want.

:::example Magnesium oxide from magnesium
Magnesium burns in oxygen: $\ce{2Mg + O2 -> 2MgO}$. What mass of $\ce{MgO}$ forms from $7.29\ \text{g}$ of $\ce{Mg}$? Give the mass to the nearest tenth. The molar masses are $\ce{Mg}$ $24.3\ \text{g/mol}$ and $\ce{MgO}$ $40.3\ \text{g/mol}$.
@step $n(\ce{Mg}) = \dfrac{7.29}{24.3} = 0.30\ \text{mol}$
@step The mole ratio of $\ce{Mg}$ to $\ce{MgO}$ is $2 : 2$, so $n(\ce{MgO}) = 0.30 \times \dfrac{2}{2} = 0.30\ \text{mol}$.
@step $m(\ce{MgO}) = 0.30 \times 40.3 = 12.09\ \text{g}$
@answer $12.1\ \text{g}$
:::

:::tip Check with the conservation of mass
In the example, the oxygen used is $0.30 \times \dfrac{1}{2} = 0.15\ \text{mol}$, which is $0.15 \times 32.0 = 4.80\ \text{g}$. The reactants have a total mass of $7.29 + 4.80 = 12.09\ \text{g}$, exactly the mass of magnesium oxide formed: mass is conserved, as lesson 4.1 showed.
:::

:::

:::summary
- Mass and moles: $n = \dfrac{m}{M}$ and $m = n \times M$. Work out the molar mass first if it is not given.
- From a mass to a number of particles: divide by the molar mass, then multiply by $6.02 \times 10^{23}$. Never multiply a mass by Avogadro's number directly.
- The coefficients of a balanced equation give the **mole ratio**: in $\ce{2H2 + O2 -> 2H2O}$, 2 mol of $\ce{H2}$ react with 1 mol of $\ce{O2}$ to make 2 mol of $\ce{H2O}$.
- $n(\text{wanted}) = n(\text{given}) \times \dfrac{\text{coefficient of wanted}}{\text{coefficient of given}}$
- Mass to mass in three steps: mass → moles, mole ratio, moles → mass.
:::
