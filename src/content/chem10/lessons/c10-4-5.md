A firework explodes in a fraction of a second; an iron gate takes years to rust. Both are chemical reactions, but they run at wildly different speeds. Controlling that speed matters everywhere: in a factory a slow reaction wastes time and money, and in a kitchen a fridge slows down the reactions that make food go bad.

In this lesson you will calculate the mean rate of a reaction from measurements, use collision theory to explain why particles react, and explain how temperature, concentration, pressure, surface area and catalysts change the rate.

## Measuring the rate of a reaction

:::definition Rate of reaction
The **rate of reaction** is how quickly the reactants are used up or the products are formed.
:::

To find a rate, measure how much of a product forms, or how much of a reactant is used up, over a measured time. Three common methods:

- **Collect a gas** in a gas syringe, or in an upside-down measuring cylinder full of water, and read its volume every 10 or 20 seconds.
- **Weigh an open flask** on a balance as a gas escapes; the mass lost is the mass of gas made.
- **Time a colour change or cloudiness**, such as how long a mixture takes to turn so cloudy that a cross drawn under the flask can no longer be seen.

:::definition Mean rate of reaction
The **mean rate** over a period of time is the amount made, or used up, divided by the time taken:

$$\text{mean rate} = \frac{\text{amount of product formed}}{\text{time taken}} \quad\text{or}\quad \frac{\text{amount of reactant used}}{\text{time taken}}$$
:::

The unit comes from the measurements: a volume in $\text{cm}^3$ divided by a time in $\text{s}$ gives $\text{cm}^3\text{/s}$; a mass in $\text{g}$ divided by a time in $\text{s}$ gives $\text{g/s}$.

:::example Hydrogen in a gas syringe
Magnesium ribbon reacts with dilute hydrochloric acid, and $48\ \text{cm}^3$ of hydrogen is collected in a gas syringe in the first $30\ \text{s}$. What is the mean rate of reaction over this time?
@step Mean rate $= \dfrac{48\ \text{cm}^3}{30\ \text{s}}$
@answer $1.6\ \text{cm}^3\text{/s}$
:::

:::example Reading a table
Marble chips react with dilute hydrochloric acid, and the carbon dioxide is collected in a gas syringe.

| Time (s) | 0 | 20 | 40 | 60 | 80 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Volume of gas ($\text{cm}^3$) | 0 | 34 | 56 | 69 | 76 |

Find the mean rate during the first $20\ \text{s}$ and between $40\ \text{s}$ and $80\ \text{s}$.
@step First $20\ \text{s}$: $\dfrac{34}{20} = 1.70\ \text{cm}^3\text{/s}$
@step From $40\ \text{s}$ to $80\ \text{s}$, the volume made is $76 - 56 = 20\ \text{cm}^3$ in $80 - 40 = 40\ \text{s}$: $\dfrac{20}{40} = 0.50\ \text{cm}^3\text{/s}$
@answer $1.70\ \text{cm}^3\text{/s}$ at first, falling to $0.50\ \text{cm}^3\text{/s}$ as the acid and marble are used up.
:::

The same relationship, rearranged, finds a time or an amount: time = amount ÷ mean rate. Change minutes to seconds first if the rate is per second.

:::example How long will it take?
Oxygen is produced at a mean rate of $0.8\ \text{cm}^3\text{/s}$. How long does it take to collect $60\ \text{cm}^3$?
@step Time $= \dfrac{60\ \text{cm}^3}{0.8\ \text{cm}^3\text{/s}}$
@answer $75\ \text{s}$

## Collision theory

Why should a reaction go faster or slower? The particle model of lesson 1.1 gives the answer.

:::definition Collision theory
Particles react only when they **collide**, and only if the collision has at least the **activation energy** (lesson 4.4). Most collisions are too gentle, and the particles just bounce apart. The rate depends on how **often** particles collide and on what **fraction** of the collisions have enough energy to succeed.
:::

So anything that makes collisions more frequent, or makes more of them energetic enough, makes the reaction faster.

| Change | Effect on rate | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Higher **temperature** | faster | particles move faster, so they collide more often, and more of the collisions have at least the activation energy |
| Higher **concentration** of a solution | faster | more reacting particles in the same volume, so they collide more often |
| Higher **pressure** of a gas | faster | gas particles are crowded into a smaller space, so they collide more often |
| Larger **surface area** (smaller pieces or a powder) | faster | more of the solid's particles are exposed, so collisions with them happen more often |
| A **catalyst** | faster | a different route with a lower activation energy, so more of the collisions succeed |

:::definition Catalyst
A **catalyst** is a substance that speeds up a reaction without being used up. It lowers the activation energy, so a bigger fraction of the collisions succeed. The same catalyst can be used again and again; the enzymes in living things are catalysts.
:::

Temperature is the only factor that changes the energy of the collisions as well as how often they happen, which is why even a small rise in temperature can make a reaction much faster. Concentration, pressure and surface area change only how often particles collide. A catalyst changes neither: it lowers the energy barrier instead.

:::example Powder or lumps?
The graph above shows the same mass of marble reacting with the same acid as lumps and as a powder. Explain why the powder curve is steeper at the start, and why both curves level off at the same volume.
@step Powder has a much larger surface area than lumps, so more marble particles are exposed to the acid, and collisions happen more often.
@step Both use the same mass of marble with the same acid, so the same amount of carbon dioxide forms in the end. The powder just makes it sooner.
@answer The powder reacts faster because its larger surface area makes collisions more frequent; both make $80\ \text{cm}^3$ because the amounts of reactants are the same.
:::

:::mistake Hotter particles are not bigger
Heating does not make particles grow, and a concentrated solution does not hold more energetic particles. Explain a rate with collisions: say whether the change makes the particles collide **more often**, makes **more of the collisions** have at least the activation energy, or both.
:::

## Comparing experiments

To find how one factor changes the rate, a fair test changes only that factor and keeps the others the same: the same mass and size of solid, the same volume and concentration of solution, and the same temperature. When several factors change at once, the experiment that is at least as good as every other one on every factor is the fastest.

:::example Which is fastest?
Three experiments use $2.0\ \text{g}$ of marble and $50\ \text{cm}^3$ of hydrochloric acid each. Experiment 1 uses lumps in $1.0\ \text{mol/L}$ acid at $20^\circ\text{C}$; experiment 2 uses powder in $1.0\ \text{mol/L}$ acid at $20^\circ\text{C}$; experiment 3 uses powder in $2.0\ \text{mol/L}$ acid at $30^\circ\text{C}$. Which has the fastest rate at the start?
@step Experiment 2 beats experiment 1: the same acid and temperature, but powder.
@step Experiment 3 beats experiment 2: powder in both, but a higher concentration and a higher temperature.
@answer Experiment 3: the largest surface area, the highest concentration and the highest temperature.
:::

:::

:::summary
- The **rate of reaction** is how quickly reactants are used up or products form. **Mean rate** = amount formed (or used) ÷ time, in units such as $\text{cm}^3\text{/s}$ or $\text{g/s}$.
- A reaction is fastest at the start and slows down as the reactants are used up.
- **Collision theory:** particles react only when they collide with at least the activation energy.
- Higher concentration, higher gas pressure and a larger surface area make collisions more frequent. A higher temperature makes them more frequent and more energetic.
- A **catalyst** lowers the activation energy, so more collisions succeed; it is not used up.
:::
