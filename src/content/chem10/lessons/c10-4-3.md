Millions of different reactions are known, but most of the ones you will meet follow a handful of patterns. Once you recognize the pattern, you can say what kind of reaction you are looking at, and often predict what it will make before you have seen the products.

In this lesson you will sort reactions into five types: synthesis, decomposition, single displacement, double displacement and combustion. Then you will predict the products of burning, of metals reacting with oxygen, of displacement with a reactivity order, and of mixing two solutions that form a precipitate.

## Five types of reactions

Sort each reactant and product into an element or a compound. An element's formula has one symbol: $\ce{Na}$, $\ce{O2}$, $\ce{Cu}$. A compound's formula has two or more: $\ce{NaCl}$, $\ce{H2O}$, $\ce{CH4}$. The pattern then tells you the type.

| Type | Pattern | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Synthesis** | A + B → AB | $\ce{2Na + Cl2 -> 2NaCl}$ |
| **Decomposition** | AB → A + B | $\ce{CaCO3 -> CaO + CO2}$ |
| **Single displacement** | A + BC → AC + B | $\ce{Zn + CuSO4 -> ZnSO4 + Cu}$ |
| **Double displacement** | AB + CD → AD + CB | $\ce{AgNO3 + NaCl -> AgCl + NaNO3}$ |
| **Combustion** | fuel + oxygen → carbon dioxide + water | $\ce{CH4 + 2O2 -> CO2 + 2H2O}$ |

:::definition Synthesis and decomposition
In a **synthesis**, two or more reactants combine into a single product. A **decomposition** is the reverse: one reactant breaks down into two or more simpler products, usually when it is heated or when electricity passes through it.
:::

:::definition Single and double displacement
In a **single displacement**, an element takes the place of another element in a compound: zinc pushes copper out of copper(II) sulfate. In a **double displacement**, two compounds, usually dissolved in water, swap partners: the silver of silver nitrate pairs with the chloride of sodium chloride, and the sodium pairs with the nitrate.
:::

:::definition Combustion
In a **combustion**, a fuel burns in oxygen and gives out energy. When the fuel is a **hydrocarbon**, a compound of carbon and hydrogen only, such as methane, $\ce{CH4}$, complete combustion in plenty of oxygen makes only carbon dioxide and water. Fuels that also contain oxygen, such as ethanol, $\ce{C2H5OH}$, burn to the same products.
:::

:::example Name the type
What type of reaction is $\ce{2Al + 3CuCl2 -> 2AlCl3 + 3Cu}$?
@step Reactants: $\ce{Al}$ is an element and $\ce{CuCl2}$ is a compound. Products: $\ce{AlCl3}$ is a compound and $\ce{Cu}$ is an element.
@step Element + compound → compound + element: the aluminium has taken the place of the copper.
@answer Single displacement.
:::

:::example Heating potassium chlorate
What type of reaction is $\ce{2KClO3 -> 2KCl + 3O2}$?
@step There is one reactant, and it forms two products.
@answer Decomposition.
:::

:::note Burning a metal or hydrogen
When magnesium burns, $\ce{2Mg + O2 -> 2MgO}$, two elements make one compound, so it is a synthesis; but it is also burning in oxygen. Hydrogen burning, $\ce{2H2 + O2 -> 2H2O}$, fits both patterns too. Because they fit two types at once, the practice questions that ask for a type leave them out: the combustions they ask about are fuels containing carbon.
:::

## Predicting the type from the reactants

The reactants alone decide the type, so you can name it before you know the products:

1. **One compound** on its own: decomposition.
2. **Two elements:** synthesis.
3. **A fuel and oxygen:** combustion.
4. **An element and a compound** (not a fuel with oxygen): single displacement.
5. **Two compounds** dissolved in water: double displacement.

:::example Chlorine and potassium bromide
A reaction has the reactants chlorine, $\ce{Cl2}$, and potassium bromide, $\ce{KBr}$. What type of reaction is it?
@step $\ce{Cl2}$ is an element, and $\ce{KBr}$ is a compound that is not a fuel.
@step Element + compound: chlorine takes the place of bromine, $\ce{Cl2 + 2KBr -> 2KCl + Br2}$.
@answer Single displacement.
:::

:::mistake A fuel with oxygen is not a single displacement
Oxygen is an element and butane, $\ce{C4H10}$, is a compound, so butane and oxygen look like element + compound. But nothing is displaced: the fuel burns, and all its carbon and hydrogen end up in carbon dioxide and water, $\ce{2C4H10 + 13O2 -> 8CO2 + 10H2O}$. Look for a fuel with oxygen before you call a reaction a single displacement.
:::

## Predicting products: burning

Two patterns let you predict the products of burning:

- **A hydrocarbon** burns completely to **carbon dioxide and water**. Each carbon atom ends up in $\ce{CO2}$ and each hydrogen atom in $\ce{H2O}$.
- **A metal** heated in oxygen forms a **metal oxide**: magnesium makes magnesium oxide, and copper makes copper(II) oxide. Only the metal and oxygen react, so the product contains only those two elements.

:::example Burning pentane
Predict the products of the complete combustion of pentane, $\ce{C5H12}$, and write the balanced equation.
@step Pentane is a hydrocarbon, so it burns to carbon dioxide and water.
@step Carbon: $\ce{5CO2}$. Hydrogen: 12 atoms, $\ce{6H2O}$. Oxygen: $5 \times 2 + 6 = 16$ atoms, $\ce{8O2}$.
@answer $\ce{C5H12 + 8O2 -> 5CO2 + 6H2O}$
:::

With too little oxygen, burning is **incomplete**: carbon monoxide, a toxic gas, or soot (carbon) forms as well. The questions here ask about complete combustion.

## Predicting products: displacement

Whether a single displacement happens depends on how reactive the elements are. A **reactivity series** lists metals from the most reactive to the least. A more reactive metal displaces a less reactive metal from a solution of its compound; a less reactive metal cannot displace a more reactive one, so then there is no reaction. A question always gives you the order to use, such as:

magnesium, zinc, iron, hydrogen, copper, silver (most reactive first)

Hydrogen is in the list because metals react with acids by displacing the hydrogen of the acid. A metal above hydrogen reacts with a dilute acid to make a **salt and hydrogen**; a metal below hydrogen does not react.

:::example Zinc in copper(II) sulfate solution
A piece of zinc is placed in copper(II) sulfate solution. What forms?
@step Zinc is above copper in the order, so it is more reactive and displaces the copper.
@step The zinc takes copper's place with the sulfate: zinc sulfate forms, and copper coats the zinc.
@answer zinc + copper(II) sulfate → zinc sulfate + copper: $\ce{Zn(s) + CuSO4(aq) -> ZnSO4(aq) + Cu(s)}$.
:::

:::example Silver in copper(II) sulfate solution
A piece of silver is placed in copper(II) sulfate solution. What forms?
@step Silver is below copper in the order, so it is less reactive and cannot displace copper.
@answer No reaction happens.
:::

:::example Iron in acid
Iron filings are added to dilute hydrochloric acid. What forms?
@step Iron is above hydrogen, so it displaces the hydrogen of the acid. Iron forms iron(II) compounds here.
@answer iron + hydrochloric acid → iron(II) chloride + hydrogen: $\ce{Fe + 2HCl -> FeCl2 + H2}$.
:::

Non-metals can displace each other too. The halogens get less reactive down group 17, so chlorine displaces bromine and iodine, and bromine displaces iodine: $\ce{Br2 + 2KI -> 2KBr + I2}$.

## Predicting products: precipitates

In a double displacement, two solutions are mixed and the compounds swap partners. If one of the new compounds does not dissolve in water, it appears as a solid, a precipitate.

:::definition Precipitate
A **precipitate** is a solid that forms when two solutions are mixed. Its state symbol is (s).
:::

To predict a precipitate:

1. **Swap partners**: the metal of each compound joins the non-metal part of the other. Write the formulas so that the charges balance, as in lesson 3.2.
2. **Check solubility**: the question gives the rules. Compounds of sodium and potassium, and all nitrates, dissolve in water, so they never precipitate.
3. The new compound that **does not dissolve** is the precipitate. The other stays dissolved.

:::example Barium chloride and sodium sulfate
Solutions of barium chloride, $\ce{BaCl2}$, and sodium sulfate, $\ce{Na2SO4}$, are mixed. Sodium compounds dissolve; most sulfates dissolve, but barium sulfate does not. What forms?
@step Swap partners: barium with sulfate gives barium sulfate, $\ce{BaSO4}$; sodium with chloride gives sodium chloride, $\ce{NaCl}$.
@step Sodium chloride dissolves. Barium sulfate does not, so it forms a white precipitate.
@answer $\ce{BaCl2(aq) + Na2SO4(aq) -> BaSO4(s) + 2NaCl(aq)}$
:::

:::mistake The precipitate is a new compound
The two solutions you mix were already dissolved, so neither starting compound can be the precipitate. Look only at the new pairs made by swapping partners, and check those against the rules.
:::

| Solutions mixed | Precipitate | Colour |
|---|---|---|
| silver nitrate and sodium chloride | silver chloride, $\ce{AgCl}$ | white |
| lead(II) nitrate and potassium iodide | lead(II) iodide, $\ce{PbI2}$ | bright yellow |
| copper(II) sulfate and sodium hydroxide | copper(II) hydroxide, $\ce{Cu(OH)2}$ | pale blue |
| iron(III) chloride and sodium hydroxide | iron(III) hydroxide, $\ce{Fe(OH)3}$ | orange-brown |

:::summary
- **Synthesis:** A + B → AB. **Decomposition:** AB → A + B. **Single displacement:** A + BC → AC + B. **Double displacement:** AB + CD → AD + CB. **Combustion:** a fuel burns in oxygen.
- The reactants decide the type: one compound, two elements, a fuel with oxygen, an element and a compound, or two compounds.
- A hydrocarbon burns completely to carbon dioxide and water. A metal heated in oxygen forms a metal oxide.
- A more reactive metal displaces a less reactive one from its compound; otherwise there is no reaction. A metal above hydrogen reacts with a dilute acid to give a salt and hydrogen.
- In a double displacement the compounds swap partners. A new compound that does not dissolve forms a **precipitate**, (s).
:::
