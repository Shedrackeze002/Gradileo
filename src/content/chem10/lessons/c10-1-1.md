Leave a glass of iced water on a table on a warm day. Within minutes the outside of the glass is covered in droplets, although nothing has leaked through it. An hour later the ice has gone, and in a few days, if you leave it long enough, the water will have disappeared from the glass altogether. Where did the droplets come from, and where did the water go?

One simple idea answers both questions, and many more: everything is made of tiny particles that are always moving. In this lesson you will use this **particle model** to describe solids, liquids and gases, to explain their properties, and to explain what happens when a substance changes from one state to another.

## The particle model

:::definition Particle model
The **particle model** says that all matter is made of particles far too small to see (atoms, molecules or ions), and that:

- the particles are always moving;
- there are forces of attraction between the particles, which are stronger the closer the particles are;
- the particles themselves never change size, shape or mass. Only their spacing, their arrangement and how fast they move can change.
:::

The three **states of matter** are solid, liquid and gas. The same substance can be in any of the three: water can be ice, liquid water or steam (water vapour). What changes from one state to another is how the particles are arranged and how they move, not the particles themselves.

:::definition Solid, liquid and gas
- In a **solid**, the particles are packed closely together, usually in a regular pattern. Strong forces hold each particle in place, so the particles can only **vibrate** about fixed positions.
- In a **liquid**, the particles are still close together and touching, but they are arranged randomly. The forces between them are weaker, so the particles can **slide past one another**.
- In a **gas**, the particles are far apart compared with their size, and the forces between them are very weak. The particles **move quickly in all directions**, in straight lines until they hit another particle or the wall of the container.
:::

The arrangement and movement of the particles explain the properties of each state:

| | Solid | Liquid | Gas |
|---|---|---|---|
| Arrangement of particles | close together, regular pattern | close together, random | far apart, random |
| Movement of particles | vibrate about fixed positions | slide past one another | move quickly in all directions |
| Forces between particles | strong | weaker | very weak |
| Shape | fixed | takes the shape of its container | fills its whole container |
| Volume | fixed | fixed | not fixed: fills the container |
| Can it be compressed? | no | hardly at all | yes, easily |
| Does it flow? | no | yes | yes |

:::example Naming a state from its particles
In a sample of a substance, the particles are touching one another but are not in any pattern, and they keep changing places with their neighbours. Which state is the substance in?
@step The particles are touching, so it is a solid or a liquid, not a gas.
@step They are not in a pattern and they change places, so they are not held in fixed positions.
@answer The substance is a liquid.
:::

:::mistake Particles in a solid still move
It is tempting to say that the particles in a solid do not move, because the solid itself stays still. In fact the particles vibrate all the time; they just cannot leave their positions. The particles of every substance are always moving.
:::

## Explaining properties with particles

The particle model does more than describe the states; it explains why they behave as they do.

- **Gases can be compressed** because their particles are far apart, with empty space between them. Pushing in the plunger of a sealed syringe of air squeezes the particles closer together.
- **Liquids and solids hardly compress** because their particles are already touching. A sealed syringe full of water cannot be pushed in.
- **Liquids flow and take the shape of their container** because their particles can slide past one another, but they keep a fixed volume because the particles stay close together.
- **Solids keep their shape** because their particles are held in fixed positions.
- **Gases spread out** to fill every part of their container because nothing holds their particles together.

The spreading of particles from where there are many to where there are few is called **diffusion**. A smell spreads across a room in a few minutes, while a drop of food colouring takes far longer to spread through a glass of still water, because gas particles move faster and have more space to move into.

:::example Explaining a property
A sealed syringe of air can be pushed in a long way, but a sealed syringe of water cannot be pushed in at all. Explain the difference.
@step In air (a gas), the particles are far apart, with lots of empty space between them. Pushing the plunger pushes them closer together.
@step In water (a liquid), the particles are already touching, so there is almost no space to push them into.
@answer The gas can be compressed because its particles are far apart; the liquid cannot because its particles are already touching.
:::

:::mistake Particles do not grow or shrink
When a gas is compressed, its particles do not get smaller; the spaces between them do. When a solid is heated and expands, its particles do not get bigger; they vibrate more and push one another slightly further apart.
:::

## Changes of state

Heating a substance gives its particles more energy, so they move faster. If enough energy is taken in, the particles can overcome some of the forces holding them together, and the substance changes state. Cooling does the reverse.

:::definition The six changes of state
- **Melting**: solid → liquid.
- **Freezing** (also called solidifying): liquid → solid.
- **Evaporation**: liquid → gas, from the surface of a liquid, at any temperature. **Boiling** is the same change happening throughout the liquid, with bubbles of gas forming inside it, at one temperature: the boiling point.
- **Condensation**: gas → liquid.
- **Sublimation**: solid → gas, without becoming a liquid first.
- **Deposition**: gas → solid, without becoming a liquid first.
:::

Each change of state either takes in energy or gives it out. To move the particles apart and let them move more freely, energy must be **taken in** to overcome the forces between them. When the particles come back together, the same energy is **given out** to the surroundings.

| Change | From → to | Energy |
|---|---|---|
| melting | solid → liquid | taken in |
| evaporation or boiling | liquid → gas | taken in |
| sublimation | solid → gas | taken in |
| freezing | liquid → solid | given out |
| condensation | gas → liquid | given out |
| deposition | gas → solid | given out |

This explains the glass of iced water. Water vapour in the air touches the cold glass, gives out energy to it, and condenses into droplets. The water left in the glass slowly evaporates: the fastest particles at its surface escape into the air.

Dry ice (solid carbon dioxide, $\ce{CO2}$) sublimes: at ordinary pressure it turns straight into a gas without melting. Frost is the opposite: on a freezing night, water vapour in the air turns straight into ice crystals on a cold surface, by deposition.

:::example Naming a change and its energy
On a cool morning, drops of dew form on the grass from the water vapour in the air. Name the change of state, and say whether energy is taken in or given out by the water.
@step The water starts as a gas (water vapour) and ends as a liquid (dew).
@step Gas → liquid is condensation.
@step The particles come closer together and move less freely, so energy is given out.
@answer Condensation; energy is given out by the water.
:::

## Melting points and boiling points

When a pure solid is heated steadily, its temperature rises until it starts to melt, then stays the same until it has all melted, even though it is still being heated. The energy taken in during melting goes into separating the particles, not into making them move faster. The same thing happens at the boiling point. A graph of temperature against time, a **heating curve**, shows these flat sections.

:::

In the heating curve, the substance is a solid in section A, melts in section B (solid and liquid together, at 50 °C), is a liquid in section C, boils in section D (liquid and gas together, at 120 °C) and is a gas in section E.

:::definition Melting point and boiling point
The **melting point** of a substance is the temperature at which it melts. A liquid freezes at the same temperature, so the melting point is also its freezing point. The **boiling point** is the temperature at which a liquid boils (at normal atmospheric pressure). Every pure substance has its own fixed melting and boiling points.
:::

So you can tell the state of a substance at any temperature from its melting and boiling points:

- **below** its melting point, it is a solid;
- **between** its melting point and its boiling point, it is a liquid;
- **above** its boiling point, it is a gas.

| Substance | Melting point (°C) | Boiling point (°C) |
|---|---|---|
| oxygen | −218 | −183 |
| ethanol | −114 | 78 |
| mercury | −39 | 357 |
| water | 0 | 100 |
| sulfur | 115 | 445 |
| iron | 1538 | 2862 |

:::example The state at a temperature
Use the table. What is the state of ethanol at −120 °C, at 20 °C and at 90 °C?
@step −120 °C is below ethanol's melting point (−114 °C), so ethanol is a solid.
@step 20 °C is between the melting point (−114 °C) and the boiling point (78 °C), so ethanol is a liquid.
@step 90 °C is above the boiling point (78 °C), so ethanol is a gas.
@answer A solid at −120 °C, a liquid at 20 °C and a gas at 90 °C.
:::

:::example Heating through two changes
A sample of mercury is heated from −50 °C to 400 °C. Which changes of state happen?
@step At −50 °C, below its melting point of −39 °C, mercury is a solid.
@step At 400 °C, above its boiling point of 357 °C, mercury is a gas.
@step To get from solid to gas, it passes through the liquid state.
@answer It melts at −39 °C, then boils at 357 °C.
:::

:::mistake Take care with negative temperatures
A temperature further below zero is colder: −120 °C is colder than −114 °C, so ethanol is still solid at −120 °C. Oxygen boils at −183 °C, so at −150 °C, which is warmer, oxygen is already a gas.
:::

:::summary
- The **particle model**: matter is made of tiny particles that are always moving and attract one another. Heating or cooling changes how they are arranged and how fast they move, never their size.
- **Solid**: particles close together in a regular pattern, vibrating about fixed positions; fixed shape and volume. **Liquid**: particles close together but random, sliding past one another; fixed volume, flows. **Gas**: particles far apart, moving quickly in all directions; fills its container and is easily compressed.
- Changes of state: **melting** (s → l), **freezing** (l → s), **evaporation** or **boiling** (l → g), **condensation** (g → l), **sublimation** (s → g) and **deposition** (g → s).
- Melting, evaporation, boiling and sublimation **take in** energy; freezing, condensation and deposition **give out** energy.
- A pure substance melts and boils at fixed temperatures, and its temperature stays the same while it changes state. Below the melting point it is a solid, between the melting and boiling points a liquid, and above the boiling point a gas.
:::
