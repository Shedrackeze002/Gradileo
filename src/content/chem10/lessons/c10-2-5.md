Drop a small piece of sodium into water and it fizzes, melts into a shining ball and races across the surface. Do the same with potassium and it bursts into a lilac flame. A piece of iron, on the other hand, sits in water for days before it even begins to rust. Chlorine is reactive enough to kill the germs in drinking water, while the argon inside some light bulbs is so unreactive that the glowing-hot filament cannot burn.

These differences are not random. Elements in the same group form **families** that share properties, and within each family the properties change gradually from the top of the group to the bottom. In this lesson you will meet the main families of the periodic table and use their patterns to make predictions.

## The families of the periodic table

Several groups have family names of their own.

| Group | Family | Outer electrons | Examples | Type |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **alkali metals** | 1 | lithium, sodium, potassium | very reactive metals |
| 2 | **alkaline earth metals** | 2 | magnesium, calcium | reactive metals |
| 3 to 12 | **transition metals** | (varies) | iron, copper, zinc, silver | hard, less reactive metals |
| 17 | **halogens** | 7 | fluorine, chlorine, bromine, iodine | reactive non-metals |
| 18 | **noble gases** | 8 (helium 2) | helium, neon, argon | very unreactive gases |

Hydrogen sits at the top of group 1 because it has 1 outer electron, but it is a non-metal gas and is not an alkali metal.

## The alkali metals

The group 1 metals are unusual metals. They are soft enough to cut with a knife, and their freshly cut surfaces are shiny but tarnish within seconds as they react with the air. They are stored under oil to keep air and water away. Lithium, sodium and potassium are even less dense than water, so they float on it.

All the alkali metals react with water, giving off hydrogen gas and leaving an alkaline solution of the metal hydroxide:

$$\text{sodium} + \text{water} \rightarrow \text{sodium hydroxide} + \text{hydrogen}$$
$$\ce{2Na + 2H2O -> 2NaOH + H2}$$

How vigorously they react depends on where they are in the group:

| Metal | Melting point | With cold water |
|---|---|---|
| lithium | 181 °C | fizzes steadily |
| sodium | 98 °C | melts into a ball and whizzes around, fizzing |
| potassium | 63 °C | whizzes around and catches fire with a lilac flame |
| rubidium | 39 °C | reacts violently |
| caesium (cesium) | 28 °C | reacts explosively |

So, going **down** group 1, the metals become **more reactive**, and their melting points fall.

:::definition Reactivity
**Reactivity** describes how readily an element takes part in chemical reactions: how quickly and vigorously it reacts. A more reactive element reacts faster and more violently with the same substance.
:::

Why does reactivity increase down group 1? Each alkali metal reacts by losing its single outer electron. Going down the group, the atoms have more shells, so the outer electron is further from the nucleus and is held less strongly. It is lost more easily, so the metal reacts more readily.

:::example Putting metals in order
Put potassium (atomic number 19), lithium (3) and caesium (55), all alkali metals, in order of reactivity, most reactive first.
@step A larger atomic number means further down the group: lithium, then potassium, then caesium.
@step Reactivity increases down group 1, so the lowest is the most reactive.
@answer Caesium, potassium, lithium.
:::

## Alkaline earth metals and transition metals

The **alkaline earth metals** in group 2 have 2 outer electrons. They are harder and denser than the group 1 metals beside them, and less reactive, because they must lose two electrons instead of one. Like group 1, they become more reactive down the group: magnesium reacts only very slowly with cold water (though it burns in air with a brilliant white flame), while calcium, below it, fizzes steadily in cold water.

The **transition metals**, groups 3 to 12, are the metals of everyday life: iron in steel, copper in electrical wires, zinc on galvanized buckets and gold and silver in jewellery. Compared with the alkali metals, they

- are harder, stronger and denser, with much higher melting points (mercury is the exception: it is a liquid);
- are far less reactive: iron rusts slowly, and copper, silver and gold hardly react with water at all;
- often form **coloured compounds**, such as blue copper(II) sulfate and green nickel compounds;
- are often useful as **catalysts**, which speed up reactions: iron is used in making ammonia, and nickel in turning vegetable oils into spreads;
- can often form more than one ion: iron can form $\ce{Fe^2+}$ and $\ce{Fe^3+}$.

## The halogens

The **halogens** in group 17 have 7 outer electrons, one short of a full shell. Their name means "salt-former", because they react with metals to make salts such as sodium chloride. They exist as molecules of two atoms, $\ce{F2}$, $\ce{Cl2}$, $\ce{Br2}$ and $\ce{I2}$, and they are toxic.

| Halogen | Colour and state at room temperature | Boiling point |
|---|---|---|
| fluorine, $\ce{F2}$ | pale yellow gas | −188 °C |
| chlorine, $\ce{Cl2}$ | yellow-green gas | −34 °C |
| bromine, $\ce{Br2}$ | red-brown liquid | 59 °C |
| iodine, $\ce{I2}$ | grey-black solid (purple vapour) | 184 °C |

Going down the group, the colours get darker and the melting and boiling points rise. Reactivity goes the other way: **reactivity decreases down group 17**, and fluorine is the most reactive of all the non-metals.

A halogen reacts by **gaining** one electron to fill its outer shell. Going down the group, the atoms get larger, so the outer shell is further from the nucleus, and an extra electron is attracted less strongly. The larger halogens gain an electron less readily, so they are less reactive.

:::mistake The trends in groups 1 and 17 run in opposite directions
Reactivity **increases** down group 1 but **decreases** down group 17. Both trends have the same cause: larger atoms hold their outer electrons less strongly. That makes it easier for a metal to **lose** an electron, but harder for a halogen to **gain** one.
:::

### Displacement reactions

A halogen dissolved in water can react with a solution of a **halide**, a compound containing a halide ion such as chloride ($\ce{Cl-}$), bromide ($\ce{Br-}$) or iodide ($\ce{I-}$). A more reactive halogen takes the electron from the halide ion of a less reactive halogen, and pushes it out of the compound. This is a **displacement reaction**.

When chlorine water is added to colourless potassium bromide solution, the mixture turns orange as bromine forms:

$$\text{chlorine} + \text{potassium bromide} \rightarrow \text{potassium chloride} + \text{bromine}$$
$$\ce{Cl2 + 2KBr -> 2KCl + Br2}$$

A less reactive halogen cannot displace a more reactive one. Iodine added to potassium bromide solution leaves it unchanged.

| Added to the solution of… | potassium chloride | potassium bromide | potassium iodide |
|---|---|---|---|
| chlorine water | — | reacts: turns orange (bromine) | reacts: turns brown (iodine) |
| bromine water | no reaction | — | reacts: turns brown (iodine) |
| iodine solution | no reaction | no reaction | — |

Fluorine is left out of these tests because it is so reactive that it reacts with the water itself.

:::example Predicting a displacement
Bromine water is added to sodium iodide solution. Does a reaction happen? If so, name the products.
@step Bromine is above iodine in group 17, so bromine is more reactive.
@step A more reactive halogen displaces a less reactive one from its halide, so bromine displaces the iodide.
@answer Yes. Sodium bromide and iodine form, and the solution turns brown: $\ce{Br2 + 2NaI -> 2NaBr + I2}$.
:::

:::example When nothing happens
Iodine solution is added to potassium chloride solution. Does a reaction happen?
@step Iodine is below chlorine in group 17, so iodine is less reactive.
@step It cannot take an electron from the chloride ions.
@answer No reaction: the iodine stays as iodine and the chloride stays as chloride.
:::

## The noble gases

The **noble gases** in group 18 have full outer shells: 2 electrons for helium and 8 for the others. A full outer shell is a very stable arrangement, so the noble gases have almost no tendency to gain, lose or share electrons. They are colourless gases made of single atoms, and they hardly react at all; chemists have made a few compounds of xenon and krypton, but only under special conditions.

Their lack of reactivity makes them useful.

- **Helium** fills party balloons and airships: it is much less dense than air, and unlike hydrogen it cannot burn.
- **Argon** fills some light bulbs and surrounds the metal in welding, so that the hot metal cannot react with oxygen.
- **Neon** glows red-orange when electricity passes through it, in advertising signs.

Like the halogens, the noble gases have boiling points that rise down the group, from helium (−269 °C, the lowest of any element) to radon.

:::summary
- **Alkali metals** (group 1): soft, low-density, very reactive metals with 1 outer electron. They react with water to form an alkaline hydroxide and hydrogen. Reactivity **increases** down the group, and melting points fall.
- **Alkaline earth metals** (group 2): 2 outer electrons, less reactive than group 1; reactivity increases down the group.
- **Transition metals** (groups 3 to 12): hard, dense, high-melting and much less reactive; they often form coloured compounds and act as catalysts.
- **Halogens** (group 17): reactive, toxic non-metals with 7 outer electrons, made of molecules of two atoms. Colours darken and boiling points rise down the group, while reactivity **decreases**.
- A more reactive halogen **displaces** a less reactive halogen from a solution of its halide: chlorine displaces bromide and iodide, and bromine displaces iodide.
- **Noble gases** (group 18): unreactive, colourless, single-atom gases with full outer shells.
:::
