Graphs are everywhere: in ads, news stories and school reports. A good graph makes data easy to understand. A badly chosen or badly drawn graph can hide the truth or even mislead you. In this lesson you will learn to choose a graph and to spot a graph that misleads.

## Four kinds of graphs

:::definition Bar graph, line graph, circle graph and pictograph
- A **bar graph** uses bars to compare amounts in separate categories.
- A **line graph** joins points with line segments to show how one quantity changes over time.
- A **circle graph** shows how one whole is split into parts. The parts add up to $100\%$.
- A **pictograph** uses symbols to compare counts. A key tells how many items each symbol stands for.
:::

The line graph above shows the temperature in Riverton every two hours on a spring day. The line makes it easy to see the temperature rise, peak in the afternoon and then fall.

:::example Choosing a graph
Which graph is best for each set of data? (a) How Kai spends the $24$ hours of a school day. (b) The number of goals each player on a hockey team scored. (c) A puppy’s mass each week for $12$ weeks.
@step (a) The hours are parts of one whole day, so use a **circle graph**.
@step (b) The players are separate categories, so use a **bar graph**.
@step (c) The mass changes over time, so use a **line graph**.
@answer Circle graph, bar graph, line graph
:::

:::tip Ask three questions
Is the data a change over time? Use a line graph. Is it parts of one whole? Use a circle graph. Is it separate categories? Use a bar graph, or a pictograph when the counts are simple and you want an eye-catching display.
:::

## Giving reasons

A good choice comes with a reason that fits the data. Sometimes a graph cannot be used at all.

:::example When a circle graph will not work
Ms. Okafor asked $20$ students which after-school activities they do. Students could choose more than one. Sports: $12$, Music: $7$, Art: $6$. Can she use a circle graph?
@step Sports: $12 \div 20 = 60\%$. Music: $7 \div 20 = 35\%$. Art: $6 \div 20 = 30\%$.
@step Total: $60\% + 35\% + 30\% = 125\%$
@answer No. The percents add up to more than $100\%$, because students could choose more than one activity. A bar graph would work instead.
:::

:::mistake A line between categories
A line graph of “favourite sport” joins hockey to soccer to basketball. There is nothing *between* two sports, so the line has no meaning. Use a line graph only when the data changes over time.
:::

## A vertical axis that does not start at 0

:::definition Misleading graph
A **misleading graph** gives a wrong impression of the data, even when the numbers on it are correct.
:::

:::figure
{"type":"plane","grid":{"xMin":2021.4,"xMax":2025.6,"yMin":40,"yMax":60,"xStep":1,"yStep":2,"xLabel":"Year","yLabel":"Band members"},"polylines":[{"points":[[2021.7,40],[2021.7,42],[2022.3,42],[2022.3,40],[2021.7,40]],"fill":true},{"points":[[2022.7,40],[2022.7,45],[2023.3,45],[2023.3,40],[2022.7,40]],"fill":true},{"points":[[2023.7,40],[2023.7,49],[2024.3,49],[2024.3,40],[2023.7,40]],"fill":true},{"points":[[2024.7,40],[2024.7,52],[2025.3,52],[2025.3,40],[2024.7,40]],"fill":true}]}
:::

:::example Reading the real change
A school put this graph of its band members on a poster. The $2025$ bar looks $6$ times as tall as the $2022$ bar. How much did the band really grow?
@step The vertical axis starts at $40$, not $0$, so the bottom of every bar is cut off.
@step $2022$: $42$ members. $2025$: $52$ members. Increase: $52 - 42 = 10$
@step Compared with $42$: $10 \div 42 \times 100 \approx 24\%$
@answer The band grew by $10$ members, about $24\%$. It did not grow to $6$ times its size.
:::

:::figure
{"type":"barChart","categories":["2022","2023","2024","2025"],"values":[42,45,49,52],"valueLabel":"Band members","categoryLabel":"Year","max":60,"step":10}
:::

Drawn fairly, with the axis starting at $0$, the same data shows a steady, modest growth.

## Other ways a graph can mislead

:::figure
{"type":"barChart","categories":["2000","2010","2020","2022","2024"],"values":[70,80,90,104,120],"valueLabel":"Members","categoryLabel":"Year"}
:::

- **Unequal intervals:** in the graph above, the first gaps are $10$ years and the last gaps are $2$ years, but they are drawn the same distance apart. The growth at the end looks sudden.
- **Symbols of different sizes:** in a pictograph, every symbol must be the same size. Bigger symbols make a short row look long.
- **A circle graph that does not add to $100\%$:** the parts of one whole must add to exactly $100\%$.
- **An uneven scale:** the numbers on an axis must go up by equal steps, such as $0, 10, 20, 30$.

:::figure
{"type":"circleGraph","slices":[{"label":"Bus 40%","value":40},{"label":"Car 35%","value":35},{"label":"Walk 30%","value":30},{"label":"Bike 15%","value":15}],"show":"none"}
:::

:::example Checking a circle graph
A school newspaper printed the circle graph above of how students get to school. What is wrong with it?
@step Add the percents: $40 + 35 + 30 + 15 = 120$
@step The parts of one whole must add to $100\%$.
@answer The percents add to $120\%$, so the graph cannot be correct.
:::

:::

:::summary
- Bar graph: compare categories. Line graph: change over time. Circle graph: parts of one whole. Pictograph: compare counts with symbols.
- A circle graph only works when the parts add up to exactly $100\%$.
- Check every graph: Does the vertical axis start at $0$? Are the intervals and steps equal? Are the symbols the same size? Do the percents add to $100\%$?
- Read the real values from the scale before you believe what the picture seems to show.
:::
