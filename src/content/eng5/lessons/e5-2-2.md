Writers often explain something by comparing it to something else. “The lake was as smooth as glass” tells you more in a few words than a long description could. In this lesson you will learn the two most common kinds of comparison, **similes** and **metaphors**, and what they tell the reader.

:::definition Literal and figurative language
**Literal** language means exactly what the words say: *The lake was calm.* **Figurative language** uses words in an unusual, imaginative way to create a picture or feeling, so the meaning is not exactly what the words say: *The lake was as smooth as glass.* Similes and metaphors are two kinds of figurative language.
:::

## Similes

:::definition Simile
A **simile** compares two different things using the word **like** or **as**.
- *Her hands were as cold as ice.*
- *The baby slept like a log.*
:::

The two things being compared must be different kinds of things. “My dog is as big as your dog” uses *as*, but it compares two dogs, so it is a plain comparison, not a simile.

## Metaphors

:::definition Metaphor
A **metaphor** compares two different things by saying one thing **is** the other, without *like* or *as*.
- *The classroom was a zoo.*
- *Grandpa’s beard is a snowy cloud.*
:::

The classroom was not really full of animals. The metaphor tells you it was noisy and wild, the way a zoo is.

:::example Simile, metaphor or neither?
> The stars were diamonds scattered across the sky.

@step Look for *like* or *as*: there are none.
@step The sentence says the stars *were* diamonds, comparing two different things.
@answer A metaphor.
:::

:::mistake Not every "like" makes a simile
In “I like pizza”, *like* means *enjoy*; nothing is being compared. Check that two different things are being compared before you call it a simile.
:::

## What a comparison means

To understand a simile or metaphor, ask: **what do the two things have in common?** That shared quality is what the writer wants you to notice.

| Comparison | The two things share | So the writer means |
|---|---|---|
| *as busy as a bee* | bees never stop working | very busy |
| *Her smile was sunshine.* | sunshine is warm and bright | her smile was warm and cheerful |
| *He ran like the wind.* | wind moves very fast | he ran very fast |

:::example Explaining a metaphor
> When the bell rang, the hallway was a river of students.

What does the metaphor tell the reader?
@step Think about a river: it is long, and it flows steadily in one direction.
@step The students must be moving the same way, in a long, steady stream.
@answer A crowd of students was flowing through the hallway together.
:::

:::summary
- A **simile** compares two different things using *like* or *as*.
- A **metaphor** says one thing *is* another.
- Ask what the two things have in common to find what the comparison means.
