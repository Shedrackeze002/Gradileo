When you read a good poem, you can almost see, hear, smell, taste or feel what the poet describes. Words that create these pictures in your mind are called **images**, or **imagery**. In this lesson you will learn which senses an image speaks to, and how exact words make images stronger.

## Images and the five senses

:::definition Imagery
**Imagery** is language that appeals to one of the five senses: **sight**, **hearing**, **smell**, **taste** or **touch**.
:::

| Sense | Example image |
|---|---|
| Sight | *a red kite against the grey sky* |
| Hearing | *the crunch of boots on frozen snow* |
| Smell | *the smoky scent of the campfire* |
| Taste | *sour lemonade that made us squint* |
| Touch | *the rough, scratchy wool of the blanket* |

Christina Rossetti’s poem uses sight to show something you cannot see at all:

> Who has seen the wind?\
> Neither I nor you:\
> But when the leaves hang trembling,\
> The wind is passing through.
>
> — Christina Rossetti, “Who Has Seen the Wind?” (1872)

We cannot see the wind, but we can **see** the leaves trembling, so we know it is there.

## Vivid words

A **vivid** word is exact. It helps the reader picture one particular thing instead of something vague.

| Vague | Vivid |
|---|---|
| The dog *went* across the yard. | The dog *bounded* across the yard. |
| She ate a *nice* apple. | She bit into a *crisp, juicy* apple. |
| The *big* wave hit the boat. | The *towering* wave slammed into the boat. |

:::mistake Vivid does not mean long
Piling up adjectives does not make writing vivid. *A very nice, really good, big cake* tells you less than *a three-layer chocolate cake*. Choose one exact word over three vague ones.
:::
