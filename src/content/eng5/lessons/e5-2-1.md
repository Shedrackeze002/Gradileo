Poems are written to be heard. Two of the things that make a poem sound like music are **rhyme**, words that end with the same sound, and **rhythm**, the pattern of beats you hear when you read it aloud. In this lesson you will learn to hear both.

## Words that rhyme

:::definition Rhyme
Two words **rhyme** when their endings sound the same, from the last vowel sound to the end: c**at** and h**at**, n**ight** and k**ite**. The letters do not have to match; only the sound does.
:::

Listen for the sound, not the spelling. *Kite* and *night* rhyme even though they are spelled differently, while *love* and *move* look alike but do not rhyme.

| Word | Rhymes with | Looks similar but does not rhyme |
|---|---|---|
| love | glove | move |
| great | late | treat |
| bear | chair | fear |
| shoe | blue | toe |

## The rhyme pattern

To show which lines of a poem rhyme, give the first line’s last sound the letter **A**. Every line that rhymes with it is also **A**. The next new sound is **B**, then **C**, and so on.

:::example Finding a rhyme pattern
> The puppy chased a yellow ball\
> Across the yard and down the hall.\
> He skidded on the kitchen floor\
> And landed by the open door.
>
> — written for Gradileo

What is the rhyme pattern?
@step *ball* is the first sound: A. *hall* rhymes with it: A.
@step *floor* is a new sound: B. *door* rhymes with it: B.
@answer AABB
:::

In Robert Louis Stevenson’s poem “Rain”, only the second and fourth lines rhyme:

> The rain is raining all around,\
> It falls on field and tree,\
> It rains on the umbrellas here,\
> And on the ships at sea.
>
> — Robert Louis Stevenson, “Rain” (1885)

*around* is A, *tree* is B, *here* is a new sound, C, and *sea* rhymes with *tree*, so the pattern is **ABCB**.

:::mistake Give every new sound a new letter
A line that does not rhyme with anything still gets a letter. In “Rain”, *here* is not left out; it is C. Skipping it would give the wrong pattern.
:::

## Syllables and beats

:::definition Syllable
A **syllable** is one beat of sound in a word. *Cat* has one syllable, *pup-py* has two, and *um-brel-la* has three.
:::

A good way to count syllables is to put your hand under your chin and say the word slowly. Each time your chin drops, that is one syllable. You can also clap once for each beat.

:::example Counting the beats in a line
> In winter I get up at night

How many syllables are in this line?
@step Clap each word: In (1) win-ter (2) I (1) get (1) up (1) at (1) night (1).
@step Add them: 1 + 2 + 1 + 1 + 1 + 1 + 1.
@answer 8 syllables.
:::

When the lines of a poem have about the same number of beats, the poem has a steady **rhythm**, like a drum. That is why Stevenson’s lines are easy to read aloud and remember.

:::summary
- Words **rhyme** when their ending sounds match; spelling does not decide it.
- A **rhyme pattern** gives each new end sound a new letter: AABB, ABAB, ABCB.
- A **syllable** is one beat; clap or feel your chin to count them.
- Lines with a similar number of beats give a poem its **rhythm**.
:::
