*Jane Eyre* is Charlotte Brontë’s novel of 1847, first published under the name Currer Bell. Jane, an orphan, tells her own life story: a cruel childhood, a harsh school, work as a governess at Thornfield Hall, her love for its master Mr Rochester, and the terrible secret that drives her away. Through it all she insists on her own worth and her freedom to choose.

In this lesson you will learn who the characters are and the order of the story, read its key quotations closely, and connect the novel to the lives of Victorian women.

## The story in five homes

| Home | What happens |
|---|---|
| Gateshead | Jane is bullied by her cousin John Reed, locked in the red-room, and sent to school by her aunt, Mrs Reed. |
| Lowood | Mr Brocklehurst shames her; Helen Burns befriends her and dies; Jane grows up and becomes a teacher. |
| Thornfield | As governess, Jane meets Mr Rochester, saves him from a fire, and accepts his proposal; the wedding is stopped when it is revealed he is already married. |
| Moor House | Jane finds her cousins, the Rivers family; St John asks her to marry him and become a missionary. |
| Ferndean | Jane hears Rochester calling, returns to find him blinded, and marries him. |

## Key quotations

As a child Jane already speaks out against cruelty, calling John Reed “like a murderer”. At Lowood, Helen Burns teaches another way: “Life appears to me too short to be spent in nursing animosity or registering wrongs.”

At Thornfield, Jane argues that “Women are supposed to be very calm generally: but women feel just as men feel”. When she believes Rochester will marry someone else, she demands:

> “Do you think, because I am poor, obscure, plain, and little, I am soulless and heartless?”

:::example Reading a quotation
Rochester tells Jane to be still, like a frantic bird. She replies: *“I am no bird; and no net ensnares me; I am a free human being with an independent will”*. What does this show?
@step A bird in a net is trapped and owned.
@step Jane rejects that image and names her own will.
@answer Jane insists that she is free and equal, and refuses to be possessed by anyone, even the man she loves.
:::

## Context and Brontë’s methods

A **governess** was educated like a lady but paid like a servant, so she belonged neither upstairs nor below. Women had few rights in marriage and few ways to earn a living. Lowood is based on the school where Brontë’s two elder sisters fell fatally ill. Jane narrates as an adult, often speaking to the reader directly, so the novel feels like an autobiography. Bertha Mason, locked in the attic and described like an animal, is read very differently today.

:::mistake Jane does not simply give in to love
Jane leaves Rochester when staying would cost her self-respect, and refuses St John when marriage would mean labour without love. She marries only as an equal.
:::

:::summary
- Five homes: **Gateshead, Lowood, Thornfield, Moor House, Ferndean**, each a stage in Jane’s growth.
- **Freedom and equality**: “I am no bird”, and “Reader, I married him.”
- **Social class and gender**: a poor governess claims the same soul and heart as her master.
- **Religion**: Helen’s forgiveness and St John’s cold duty are both tested against Jane’s own principles.
:::
