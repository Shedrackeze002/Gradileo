*The Strange Case of Dr Jekyll and Mr Hyde* is a novella that Robert Louis Stevenson published in 1886. A respected London doctor makes a drug that turns him into another man, Edward Hyde, through whom he can do what he likes without risking his good name. The story is told as a mystery: we follow the lawyer Utterson as he tries to understand Hyde, and only the two letters at the end reveal the truth.

In this lesson you will learn who the characters are and the order of the story, read its key quotations closely, and connect the novella to Victorian ideas about reputation, science and evolution.

## The story in ten chapters

| Chapters | What happens |
|---|---|
| 1 to 3 | Enfield tells Utterson how a man trampled a girl and paid with a respectable man’s cheque. Utterson rereads Jekyll’s will, which leaves everything to Hyde, meets Hyde, and is told by Jekyll that he can be rid of Hyde when he chooses. |
| 4 to 6 | Hyde beats Sir Danvers Carew to death. Mr Guest notices that Hyde’s handwriting is like Jekyll’s. Lanyon has a terrible shock and dies. |
| 7 and 8 | Utterson sees terror on Jekyll’s face at a window. Poole fetches him, and they break into the cabinet to find Hyde dead. |
| 9 and 10 | Lanyon’s letter describes Hyde turning into Jekyll before his eyes, and Jekyll’s statement explains everything. |

## The characters

**Utterson**, a loyal and serious lawyer, is our guide. **Jekyll** is a wealthy, respected doctor; **Hyde** is small, violent and gives everyone who meets him a feeling of disgust. **Enfield**, **Lanyon**, **Poole** and **Mr Guest** each hold a piece of the puzzle. Almost all of them are professional men who value discretion, which is why the secret stays hidden so long.

## Key quotations

Enfield describes Hyde trampling a child: “It wasn’t like a man; it was like some damned Juggernaut.” From the start Hyde seems less than human. Utterson cannot name what is wrong with him, only that there is “Something troglodytic”, like a cave-dweller, and that “if ever I read Satan’s signature upon a face, it is on that of your new friend.”

:::example Reading a quotation
Jekyll tells Utterson: *“the moment I choose, I can be rid of Mr. Hyde.”* Why is this line important?
@step The reader does not yet know that Jekyll and Hyde are one man.
@step Later, Hyde begins to appear without the drug, and Jekyll cannot stop him.
@answer It is dramatic irony: Jekyll believes he controls his darker self, but Hyde will soon control him.
:::

In his statement, Jekyll explains that “man is not truly one, but truly two”, and that while all of us are “commingled out of good and evil”, Hyde alone “was pure evil.” Because Jekyll held his desires down for so long, “My devil had been long caged, he came out roaring.”

## Context and Stevenson’s methods

Victorian gentlemen were judged by their **respectability**, and a scandal could ruin them, so many kept their private lives secret. Charles Darwin’s theory of evolution (1859) made some readers fear that people could slip back into a primitive state, and Hyde is described as ape-like and “troglodytic”. Stevenson tells the story like a legal case, with witnesses and documents, and sets it in a foggy London of closed doors and back entrances.

:::mistake Hyde is not a separate villain
Hyde is part of Jekyll. Jekyll enjoyed being Hyde, and that is the real horror: the evil was in the respectable man all along.
:::

:::summary
- A mystery told mostly through **Utterson**, solved by **Lanyon’s narrative** and **Jekyll’s statement** at the end.
- **Duality**: everyone is mixed good and evil; the drug separates Jekyll’s evil out as Hyde.
- **Reputation and secrecy**, **repression**, and **civilisation and the primitive** (ape-like, “troglodytic” Hyde).
- **Context**: Victorian respectability, Darwin and fears of degeneration, and foggy London.
:::
