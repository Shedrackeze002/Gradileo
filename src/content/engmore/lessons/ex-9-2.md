This lesson reads three more poems from the **Love and Relationships** cluster of the AQA anthology: **“Sonnet 29 – ‘I think of thee!’”** by Elizabeth Barrett Browning, a sonnet of longing; **“Neutral Tones”** by Thomas Hardy, the memory of a love that died; and **“The Farmer’s Bride”** by Charlotte Mew, a farmer’s account of the young wife who fears him. All three are in the public domain, so every line quoted here is copied from the editions listed in the references.

In this lesson you will learn what each poem is about, recognise its key lines, and connect each poem to its context and to the other poems of the cluster.

## “Sonnet 29 – ‘I think of thee!’” (1850)

The speaker’s thoughts of her lover grow around him like a plant:

> I think of thee!—my thoughts do twine and bud
> About thee, as wild vines, about a tree,

This **extended metaphor** of vines round a tree runs through the poem. Then her argument turns: “I will not have my thoughts instead of thee”. She wants the man himself, not her thoughts of him, and the final line resolves her longing:

> I do not think of thee—I am too near thee.

The poem is a **Petrarchan sonnet**: fourteen lines, an octave of eight lines and a sestet of six, with a turn, or **volta**, between them. It comes from *Sonnets from the Portuguese*, the sonnets Elizabeth Barrett wrote while Robert Browning was courting her. They married secretly in 1846, against her father’s wishes.

## “Neutral Tones” (1898)

A speaker remembers standing with his lover by a pond on a grey winter day, when he realised their love had died. Even the sun seems drained of colour: “And the sun was white, as though chidden of God”. She looks at him with boredom:

> Your eyes on me were as eyes that rove
> Over tedious riddles solved years ago;

Her smile is a **paradox**, “the deadest thing / Alive enough to have strength to die”. In the last stanza, the speaker has learned since then “keen lessons that love deceives, / And wrings with wrong”, and every such lesson brings back the pond, the sun, the tree and the leaves.

The poem has four quatrains, and it ends with the images it began with. This **cyclical structure** traps the speaker in the memory. Hardy wrote it in 1867, but published it over thirty years later, in his first collection of poems.

:::example Reading a simile
The speaker remembers: “Your eyes on me were as eyes that rove / Over tedious riddles solved years ago”. What does the simile show?
@step A riddle is interesting only until it is solved; one solved years ago is dull.
@step Her eyes wander over him in the same bored way, as if he has nothing new to offer.
@answer The simile shows that her love has turned to boredom. He has become, for her, a puzzle that is no longer worth looking at.
:::

## “The Farmer’s Bride” (1916)

A farmer tells how he married a young woman without courting her, because there was too much to do:

> Three Summers since I chose a maid,
> Too young maybe—but more’s to do
> At harvest-time than bide and woo.

After the wedding she became afraid of him and ran away. The villagers hunted her down, and he locked her in:

> We caught her, fetched her home at last
> And turned the key upon her, fast.

He sees her as a wild creature, “Shy as a leveret, swift as he” (a leveret is a young hare). She now sleeps alone in the attic, and the poem ends with his longing breaking out of control: “The brown of her—her eyes, her hair, her hair!”

The poem is a **dramatic monologue** in the farmer’s rural **dialect**. Mew, a woman, writes in the voice of a man; we never hear the bride speak, so her fear speaks through his account.

## Themes and comparisons

Barrett Browning writes of **desire and longing** that ends in fulfilment. Hardy writes of **loss and heartbreak** that has stayed in his **memory**. Mew writes of **possession and control**: the farmer loves his wife, but he treats her as his property. All three use **nature** to express love: the vines and tree, the bleak pond, and the hare and young larch tree of the farm.

“The Farmer’s Bride” and “Porphyria’s Lover” are both monologues by men who want to possess a woman. “Neutral Tones” and “When We Two Parted” both look back on the end of a relationship, and both end where they began. “Sonnet 29” and “Love’s Philosophy” both use images of nature to express longing.

## Context

These poems span the Victorian age and the early twentieth century. Victorian women had few rights in marriage, which shapes both Barrett Browning’s defiance of her father and the farmer’s control over his young wife.

:::mistake The farmer is not simply a villain
He has locked his wife away, and he is wrong to treat her as property. But the poem also shows his loneliness and longing, and lets us feel for him as well as judge him.
:::

:::summary
- **“Sonnet 29”** (Barrett Browning, 1850): a Petrarchan sonnet; thoughts that twine like vines give way to the lover himself.
- **“Neutral Tones”** (Hardy, 1898): the memory of a dying love, in a cyclical structure of bleak images.
- **“The Farmer’s Bride”** (Mew, 1916): a dramatic monologue in dialect about a farmer who controls, and longs for, his frightened wife.
:::
