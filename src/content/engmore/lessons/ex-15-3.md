Many great works are built from older ones. Shakespeare took almost all his plots from earlier books, and writers ever since have retold his plays in turn. What makes such a work original is what the writer does with the source: what they keep, what they change, and what the changes make us see.

In this lesson you will recognize how a writer draws on a source and analyse how the writer transforms it.

## Writers and their sources

:::definition Source material
A writer’s **source** is an earlier work that supplies a plot, characters, a theme or an image. An **adaptation** retells a source in a new form or setting; an **allusion** refers to it briefly.
:::

| Source | Later work | What the writer does |
|---|---|---|
| Ovid, *Metamorphoses*: Pyramus and Thisbe, lovers divided by a wall, each dying in the belief that the other is dead | *Romeo and Juliet* | the same shape, as tragedy |
| the same tale | *A Midsummer Night’s Dream* | a clumsy play performed by tradesmen, as comedy |
| Arthur Brooke, *The Tragicall Historye of Romeus and Juliet* (1562), told over about nine months | *Romeo and Juliet* | compressed into a few days; Juliet made younger |
| the myth of Prometheus, the Titan punished for giving fire to humans | Mary Shelley, *Frankenstein, or The Modern Prometheus* | a scientist who oversteps and suffers |
| Montaigne, “Of Cannibals” | Gonzalo’s ideal commonwealth in *The Tempest* | a utopian dream voiced by a kindly but mocked courtier |

## Analysing a transformation

Noting that a writer used a source is only the first step. Analysis explains the **effect** of the changes.

:::example From noting to analysing
Brooke’s Juliet is sixteen; Shakespeare’s is thirteen. Turn this fact into analysis.
@step Note the change: Shakespeare makes Juliet younger.
@step Ask what it does: a thirteen-year-old’s choices seem more impulsive and her fate more pitiful.
@answer By making Juliet thirteen instead of sixteen, Shakespeare sharpens our sense of her youth and haste.
:::

:::tip Three questions
1. What did the writer **keep** from the source?
2. What did they **change**: the form, the setting, the point of view, the pace, the ending?
3. **Why**, and what does the change make us see?
:::

Retelling from a new point of view, such as giving a voice to a woman who is silent in a Greek myth, is one of the most common transformations. A borrowed echo can also add meaning a character does not state: Prospero’s farewell to his magic echoes a spell spoken by the sorceress Medea in Ovid, which hints that his power is darker than he admits.

:::summary
- Writers draw on **sources** through adaptation and allusion.
- The same source can become tragedy or comedy, depending on its treatment.
- Changes of **form**, **setting**, **point of view** and **pace** transform a source.
- Analyse by asking what was kept, what was changed, and **why**.
:::
