Commas help a reader hear a sentence the way you meant it. You already use them in lists, after an introduction and in dialogue. This lesson adds the commas that come with speaking to someone, and shows how to set out the title of a book, a poem or a song.

In this lesson you will use commas with *yes* and *no*, with the name of the person spoken to and before a tag question, decide when two adjectives need a comma between them, and punctuate the titles of works.

## Yes, no and direct address

A comma sets off *yes* or *no* at the start of a reply, and it sets off the name of the person you are speaking to, which is called **direct address**.

- *Yes, you can borrow my pencil.*
- *No, thank you.*
- *Grace, please close the door.*
- *Is that your coat, Daniel?*
- *I think, Amara, that you should go first.*

A name in the middle of a sentence needs a comma on each side.

:::note Talking to someone, or about them?
*Come and see this, Noah* speaks to Noah, so it needs a comma. *Noah came to see this* talks about him, so it does not.
:::

## Tag questions

:::definition Tag question
A **tag question** is a short question added to the end of a statement: *isn’t it?*, *didn’t you?*, *can’t we?* A comma comes before it.
:::

- *It’s a lovely day, isn’t it?*
- *You finished your homework, didn’t you?*

## Commas between adjectives

When two adjectives each describe the same noun equally, they are **coordinate adjectives**, and a comma goes between them. Try two tests:

1. Can you put *and* between them? *a long and exhausting journey*
2. Can you swap them? *an exhausting, long journey*

If both work, use a comma: *a long, exhausting journey*. If they do not, leave the comma out: *an old green jacket* (not *an old and green jacket*).

:::example A comma or not?
Does this need a comma? *“We swam in the cold clear water.”*
@step Try *and*: *the cold and clear water* sounds natural.
@step Try swapping: *the clear, cold water* also works.
@step Both tests work, so the adjectives are coordinate.
@answer We swam in the cold, clear water.
:::

## The titles of works

| Long works, in *italics* (underlined when handwritten) | Short works, in “quotation marks” |
|---|---|
| books and novels: *Treasure Island* | poems: “The Raven” |
| films: *The Lost Valley* | short stories: “The Gift of the Magi” |
| newspapers and magazines: *Daily Star* | articles: “Our Library Reopens” |
| plays and albums | songs: “Home on the Range” |
| | chapters: “The Secret Door” |

The rule of thumb: the whole work goes in italics, and a part of something bigger (a chapter, an article, an episode) goes in quotation marks.

:::summary
- Use a comma after **yes** and **no**, and to set off the name of the person spoken to.
- Put a comma before a **tag question**: *isn’t it?*
- Put a comma between **coordinate adjectives**, which pass the *and* test and the swap test.
- Put the titles of **long works** in italics and of **short works** in quotation marks.
:::
