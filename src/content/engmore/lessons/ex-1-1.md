A friend says, "That film was terrifying." You saw it too. Do you agree completely, partly, or not at all? To answer well you would point to particular scenes, say what made them work, and admit the moments that were not frightening at all. An exam question that gives you a statement about a text and asks **how far do you agree?** wants exactly this.

In this lesson you will choose the evidence that supports a statement and the evidence that challenges it, explain how the writer's methods create the effect, and reach a judgement.

## What evaluating means

:::definition Evaluate
To **evaluate** is to judge how well something works. When you evaluate a statement about a text, you decide how far the statement is true, and you prove your judgement with evidence from the text and with the writer's methods.
:::

An evaluation has three parts, and a strong answer has all of them:

- **A judgement:** how far you agree with the statement.
- **Evidence:** short quotations or details from the text.
- **Methods and effect:** what the writer has done, and what it makes the reader think or feel.

Read this short extract.

> The corridor was longer than Asha remembered. Her new shoes squeaked on every step, announcing her to rows of closed doors. Somewhere a bell rang, and the sound seemed to chase her. At the far end, a girl with paint on her sleeves looked up and smiled.

A student says: *"The writer makes the reader feel that Asha is nervous and alone."*

## Evidence for the statement

Start with the details that support the statement. Not every detail does: choose the ones that most clearly make a reader think or feel what the statement says.

:::example Choosing the strongest evidence
Which detail best supports the statement that Asha is nervous and alone?
@step "The corridor was longer than Asha remembered" hints at her unease, but only gently.
@step "the sound seemed to chase her" turns an ordinary bell into something that hunts her. This is stronger: it shows her fear.
@step "rows of closed doors" shows that nobody is there to welcome her.
@answer "the sound seemed to chase her" and "rows of closed doors" are the strongest evidence.
:::

## Evidence against the statement

A statement is rarely the whole truth. Look for a detail that pulls the other way, because weighing it is what turns agreement into evaluation. Here the last sentence does it: "a girl with paint on her sleeves looked up and smiled." Asha is not completely alone after all.

:::tip Words for how far you agree
*I agree completely*, *I agree to a large extent*, *I partly agree* and *I agree only to a small extent* all make a clear judgement. Choose the one your evidence supports.
:::

## Explaining methods and their effect

A quotation proves nothing until you explain it. Name what the writer has done, then say what effect it has on the reader, and link that effect to the statement.

| Kind of comment | Example | Is it evaluation? |
|---|---|---|
| Repeats the quotation | *This shows that the bell rang and Asha heard it.* | No |
| Could be about any text | *This is effective and makes the reader want to read on.* | No |
| Method and effect, linked to the statement | *The personification makes the bell seem to hunt her, so the reader feels her fear.* | Yes |

:::mistake Retelling is not evaluating
The commonest weak answer tells the story again: *Asha walks down the corridor, a bell rings and a girl smiles.* Every word is true, and none of it judges the statement. If your sentence would still make sense with the statement deleted from the question, it is retelling.
:::

:::

## Writing the evaluation

**Open with your judgement.** The first sentence should say how far you agree, and why: *I agree to a large extent, because the writer makes the school seem empty and hostile until the final sentence.* An opening that describes the extract, or says that you enjoyed it, has not started to answer.

:::

**Build each paragraph from a judgement, a quotation and an explanation.** Check your own work for the three weaknesses that cost the most marks: retelling, opinion with no evidence, and methods with no effect.

:::example What is missing?
A student writes: *"I agree because the extract is really tense, and anyone would feel sorry for Asha."* What is the weakness?
@step There is a judgement: the student agrees.
@step There is no quotation, and no detail from the text at all.
@answer It is an opinion with no evidence. It needs a quotation and an explanation of how the writer creates the tension.
:::

:::check ex-evaluate-response 2
:::

**End with a balanced conclusion.** Weigh the evidence on both sides and still decide: *I mostly agree: the closed doors and the chasing bell make Asha seem alone and afraid, although the girl's smile suggests that this is about to change.* Be wary of "every detail" and "nothing in the text", which are almost never true, and of "it depends on the reader", which refuses to judge.

:::check ex-evaluate-response 3
:::

:::summary
- To **evaluate** a statement is to judge how far it is true and to prove the judgement from the text.
- A strong evaluation has a **judgement**, **evidence** and an explanation of the writer's **methods and their effect**.
- Choose the strongest evidence for the statement, and look for a detail that pulls **against** it.
- Explain each quotation: name the method, say what it makes the reader think or feel, and link it to the statement. Retelling the story earns nothing.
- Open with how far you agree, and end with a conclusion that weighs both sides and still decides.
:::
