Two friends go to the same concert. One tells you it was crowded, loud and wonderful; the other says it was crowded, loud and unbearable. If someone asked you afterwards how their evenings differed, you would not repeat everything they said. You would pick out the points that matter, set them side by side, and say what the difference shows. That is a summary of differences.

In this lesson you will state a difference between two texts, support it with a quotation from each, and add an inference.

## What the question asks for

:::definition Summary
A **summary** gives the main points of a text briefly, in your own words. A summary of two texts brings together details from both: this is also called **synthesis**.
:::

A summary question names a focus: *the differences between the two schools*, or *the differences between the two journeys*. Only details about that focus belong in the answer. A good answer does three things for each point:

1. **States the difference** clearly, with both sources in one sentence.
2. **Gives evidence** from each source: a short quotation.
3. **Makes an inference** about what the difference suggests.

Here are two short sources about a walk to school.

> **Source A** (2024): I walk to school with my headphones in. It takes eleven minutes, and I could not tell you the name of one street.

> **Source B** (1880): We walked four miles to the schoolhouse, and I knew every gate, every dog and every farmer by name along the way.

## Stating a difference

Begin with what is true in each source, joined by a comparing word such as *while*, *whereas* or *but*.

:::example A clear statement
State one difference between the two walks.
@step In Source A the walk is short: "eleven minutes".
@step In Source B the walk is long: "four miles".
@answer In Source A the walk to school is short, while in Source B it is long.
:::

:::mistake Getting the sources the wrong way round
Under time pressure it is easy to give Source A's detail to Source B. Check each half of your sentence against the right text before you move on.
:::

## Choosing the evidence

Each half of the statement needs its own quotation, and both quotations must be about the same focus. Keep them short: a few words are enough.

:::tip Select, do not collect
A summary leaves most of the text out. If the question asks about the walks, a detail about the lessons does not belong, however interesting it is.
:::

## Making an inference

:::definition Inference
An **inference** is a conclusion that the text does not state but that its details support. It is what you can work out from what you are told.
:::

In Source A the writer "could not tell you the name of one street"; in Source B the writer "knew every gate, every dog and every farmer". Neither writer says so, but we can infer that the modern walker is cut off from the places he passes, while the earlier walker belongs to them.

An inference must follow from the text. "Children in 1880 were fitter than children today" may or may not be true, but nothing in these two sources tells us, so it is not an inference from them.

:::example Testing an inference
Which is a fair inference: (a) the writer of Source A dislikes music, or (b) the writer of Source A pays little attention to his surroundings?
@step Source A says he walks "with my headphones in". Nothing suggests that he dislikes music.
@step He cannot name one street on a walk he makes every day.
@answer (b). It follows from the details. (a) goes against them.
:::

## Putting it together

A full point has all three parts:

> In Source A the writer notices nothing on his walk ("could not tell you the name of one street"), while in Source B the writer knows everything along the way ("every gate, every dog and every farmer"). This suggests that the modern walker is cut off from his surroundings, while the earlier walker belonged to his.

:::note A summary is not a language answer
Do not analyse the writer's words in a summary. "The list of three is effective" belongs in a question about language. A summary deals with **what** the texts say and what can be inferred, not **how** they say it.
:::

:::summary
- A **summary** of two texts selects the details that answer the question and brings both sources together.
- For each point: **state the difference**, **quote each source** briefly, and **infer** what the difference suggests.
- Join the two halves with a comparing word: *while*, *whereas*, *but*.
- An **inference** goes beyond what is stated, but the text must support it.
- Leave out details about other things, and do not analyse language.
:::
