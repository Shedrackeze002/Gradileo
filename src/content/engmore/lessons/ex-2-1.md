You have a strong opinion and the reasons to back it. Now the task adds one more demand: write it as a letter to your headteacher, or as an article for the local paper, or as a speech to your year group. The argument may be the same in all three, but a letter that begins "Good morning, everyone" has gone wrong before its first point.

In this lesson you will use the features and structure of an article, a formal letter and a speech, and match your language to the audience.

## Form, audience and purpose

:::definition Form
The **form** of a piece of writing is the kind of text it is: an article, a letter, a speech, a review. Each form has features that readers expect.
:::

Every writing task tells you three things. Find them before you plan.

| | Question to ask | In "Write a letter to your headteacher arguing that homework should be optional" |
|---|---|---|
| **Form** | What kind of text? | A letter |
| **Audience** | Who will read or hear it? | The headteacher |
| **Purpose** | What is it for? | To argue |

## The article

An article is written to be read on a page or a screen, by readers who can stop at any moment. Its features help to catch them and keep them.

- A **headline** that catches the eye: *Homework: Time to Close the Book?*
- A **strapline** under the headline that sums up the piece in a sentence.
- An **opening paragraph** that hooks the reader.
- **Subheadings** that divide the text into sections.
- A **final paragraph** that leaves a strong last thought.

An article has no greeting and no sign-off, and it does not thank anyone.

## The formal letter

A letter is written to one reader, who is named if you know the name.

- The **sender's address and the date** at the top.
- A **greeting**: *Dear Mrs Castellano,* or *Dear Sir or Madam,*
- An **opening paragraph** that says why you are writing.
- **Paragraphs** that each give one reason, with evidence.
- A **final paragraph** that says what you want to happen.
- A **sign-off** and your name.

:::definition Sign-off
The **sign-off** is the polite phrase that ends a letter, above the writer's name. In a formal letter it depends on the greeting.
:::

| The letter begins | It ends |
|---|---|
| *Dear Mrs Castellano,* (a name) | *Yours sincerely,* |
| *Dear Sir or Madam,* (no name) | *Yours faithfully,* |

## The speech

A speech is written to be heard. Listeners cannot look back up the page, so a speech guides them.

- A **greeting** to the audience: *Good morning, everyone.*
- An **opening** that grabs attention: a question, a surprising fact, a picture in words.
- **Signposts** so that listeners can follow: *My second point is this.*
- **Direct address**: *you*, *we*, *all of us in this hall*.
- A **call to action**, then a **thank-you** to the audience.

## Register

:::definition Register
**Register** is how formal the language is. It should fit the audience: a letter to a councillor needs a formal register, while a message to a friend does not.
:::

All three forms in this lesson are written for the public or for someone in authority, so all three need standard English. A formal register avoids:

- **slang and chatty phrases**: *cost a bomb*, *to be honest*, *a bit of a joke*;
- **insults**: *only a fool would support this*.

Formal does not mean harsh, and it does not mean long-winded. It means precise, polite and measured.

:::example Raising the register
Rewrite formally: *"The buses round here are a total joke and nobody can be bothered to fix them."*
@step Replace the slang: "a total joke" becomes "unreliable".
@step Replace the chatty complaint with a measured one: "nobody can be bothered to fix them" becomes "the problem has not been addressed".
@answer The local bus service is unreliable, and the problem has not been addressed.
:::

:::mistake Formal is not the same as rude
A strong argument criticises the plan, not the person. *The plan is short-sighted* persuades a councillor. *Only an idiot would support the plan* makes the councillor stop reading. Keep the meaning, too: a formal version must still say what you meant.
:::

## The order of the parts

Whatever the form, the argument sits in the middle. What changes is how the piece begins and ends.

| | Begins with | Ends with |
|---|---|---|
| Article | Headline, strapline, a hook | A strong final thought |
| Letter | Address, date, greeting, why you are writing | What you want to happen, sign-off, name |
| Speech | A greeting, an opening that grabs attention | A call to action, a thank-you |

:::

:::summary
- Before you plan, find the **form**, the **audience** and the **purpose** in the task.
- An **article** has a headline, a strapline, subheadings and a strong final thought, with no greeting or sign-off.
- A **formal letter** has the address and date, a greeting, a clear reason for writing and a sign-off: *Yours sincerely* with a name, *Yours faithfully* with *Dear Sir or Madam*.
- A **speech** greets its listeners, signposts its points, addresses the audience directly, and ends with a call to action and a thank-you.
- Use a formal **register**: standard English, no slang and no insults, with the meaning unchanged.
:::
