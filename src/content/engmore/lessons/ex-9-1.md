The **Love and Relationships** cluster of the AQA poetry anthology has fifteen poems about romantic love, longing and heartbreak, and the love between parents and children. This lesson reads three poems from the nineteenth century: **“When We Two Parted”** by Lord Byron, **“Love’s Philosophy”** by Percy Bysshe Shelley and **“Porphyria’s Lover”** by Robert Browning. All three are in the public domain, so every line quoted here is copied from the editions listed in the references.

In this lesson you will learn what each poem is about, recognise its key lines, and connect each poem to its context and to the other poems of the cluster.

## “When We Two Parted” (1816)

A speaker remembers the end of a secret love affair. The poem opens with the moment they parted:

> When we two parted
> In silence and tears,
> Half broken-hearted
> To sever for years,

“Sever” makes the parting sound like a cut. Even then her love seemed to be fading: “Pale grew thy cheek and cold, / Colder thy kiss”. Now he hears her name spoken in gossip, “And share in its shame”, but because the affair was secret he must grieve alone: “In secret we met— / In silence I grieve”. The last lines return to the beginning:

> How should I greet thee?—
> With silence and tears.

The poem has four eight-line stanzas with a regular rhyme scheme, and its **cyclical structure** shows that his pain has not changed. Byron is thought to have written about a real affair with a married woman, and he gave the poem a false date, perhaps to protect her.

## “Love’s Philosophy” (1819)

A speaker tries to persuade someone to kiss him, using nature as evidence. Everything in the world joins with something else:

> The fountains mingle with the river
> And the rivers with the Ocean,

“Nothing in the world is single”, he argues, so it would be unnatural for her to refuse. In the second stanza, nature is **personified** as lovers: “See the mountains kiss high Heaven / And the waves clasp one another”. Each stanza ends with a question, and the final one is never answered:

> What is all this sweet work worth
> If thou kiss not me?

The poem has two eight-line stanzas. The unanswered question suggests that his longing is not returned. Like the other Romantic poets, Shelley draws on the beauty of nature to explore feeling.

## “Porphyria’s Lover” (1836)

A man describes a stormy night: “The rain set early in to-night, / The sullen wind was soon awake”. The weather is **pathetic fallacy**, matching his dark mood. Porphyria arrives at his cottage, warms the room and tells him she loves him. He decides to keep that moment for ever:

> That moment she was mine, mine, fair,
> Perfectly pure and good:

Then, in the same calm voice, he strangles her with her own hair (“In one long yellow string I wound / Three times her little throat around, / And strangled her”). He sits with her body all night, and ends: “And yet God has not said a word!”

The poem is a **dramatic monologue**: only the killer speaks, so we must judge him by his own words. His regular rhyme scheme and reasonable tone make his madness more disturbing.

:::example Reading the speaker’s words
The speaker says: “That moment she was mine, mine, fair, / Perfectly pure and good”. What does it show about him?
@step The word “mine” is repeated: what matters to him is owning her.
@step He wants to keep her as she is at “That moment”, perfect and unchanging.
@answer His love is really a wish to **possess** her completely, and it leads straight to her murder.
:::

## Themes and comparisons

Byron writes of **loss and heartbreak**, Shelley of **desire and longing**, and Browning of **possession and control**, the dark side of desire. Nature appears in two of them: Shelley uses nature to argue for love, and Browning uses the storm to reflect the speaker’s mind.

“Porphyria’s Lover” pairs closely with “The Farmer’s Bride”: both are monologues by men who want to possess a woman. “When We Two Parted” and “Neutral Tones” both remember the end of a love affair, and “Love’s Philosophy” and “Sonnet 29” both use images of nature to express longing.

## Context

Byron and Shelley were Romantic poets who valued strong feeling and nature, and both lived scandalous lives. Browning was a Victorian, and the dramatic monologue was his favourite way of letting a speaker reveal more than he means to.

:::mistake The speaker of “Porphyria’s Lover” is not a romantic hero
The poem sounds tender and is full of love words, but the speaker murders the woman who loves him. The calm voice is what makes him frightening.
:::

:::summary
- **“When We Two Parted”** (Byron, 1816): the pain of a secret affair’s end; cyclical, beginning and ending in “silence and tears”.
- **“Love’s Philosophy”** (Shelley, 1819): an argument for love from nature, ending in an unanswered question.
- **“Porphyria’s Lover”** (Browning, 1836): a dramatic monologue by a man who kills to possess the woman he loves.
:::
