*The Sign of Four* is Arthur Conan Doyle’s second Sherlock Holmes novel, published in 1890. Mary Morstan asks Holmes for help: her father vanished ten years ago, and every year since, someone has sent her a pearl. The trail leads to a murder in a locked room, a chase down the Thames, and a treasure stolen in India decades before. Dr Watson, Holmes’s friend, tells the story, and falls in love with Mary along the way.

In this lesson you will learn who the characters are and the order of the story, read its key quotations closely, and connect the novel to detective fiction and the British Empire.

## The story

| Part | What happens |
|---|---|
| The case begins | Watson objects to Holmes taking cocaine. Mary Morstan arrives with her mystery, and Thaddeus Sholto tells them of the Agra treasure. |
| The murder | Bartholomew Sholto is found dead at Pondicherry Lodge, and the treasure is gone. Holmes and Watson follow the dog Toby along a trail of creosote. |
| The chase | The Baker Street Irregulars find the launch Aurora. Holmes and the police chase it down the Thames and capture Jonathan Small. |
| The ending | The treasure box is empty. Watson tells Mary he loves her, and Small tells the story of the four men and the treasure. |

## Key quotations

Holmes needs puzzles: his mind “rebels at stagnation. Give me problems, give me work”. Without a case, he turns to cocaine. He sees detection as pure logic: “Detection is, or ought to be, an exact science, and should be treated in the same cold and unemotional manner.” Watson calls him “an automaton,—a calculating-machine!”

:::example Reading a quotation
Holmes asks: *“How often have I said to you that when you have eliminated the impossible whatever remains, however improbable, must be the truth?”* How does he reason?
@step He rules out every way the killer could not have entered.
@step Whatever way is left must be the answer, however strange it seems.
@answer Holmes reasons by elimination, so he accepts an unlikely answer when nothing else is possible.
:::

When the treasure box is found empty, Watson admits that “a great shadow seemed to pass from my soul”: Mary is no longer far richer than he is. Small, who suffered for “Twenty long years in that fever-ridden swamp”, would rather the treasure were lost than given to anyone else. At the end, Watson has a wife and Jones the credit; for Holmes, “there still remains the cocaine-bottle.”

## Context and Doyle’s methods

The treasure was stolen during the **Indian Rebellion of 1857**, when Indian soldiers rose against British rule. The novel reflects the attitudes of the Empire: its description of Tonga, Small’s companion from the Andaman Islands, is racist, and readers today recognise it as a colonial stereotype. Cocaine was legal in 1890. The story follows the pattern of detective fiction, a crime, clues, a chase and a final explanation, and Watson’s admiring narration makes Holmes seem brilliant and mysterious.

:::mistake The detective does not narrate
Watson tells the story, so we are as puzzled as he is until Holmes explains. That is what makes each solution a surprise.
:::

:::summary
- A mystery of pearls, a locked room, a river chase and an empty treasure box.
- **Reason and detection**: Holmes eliminates the impossible; Watson brings feeling.
- **Greed and treasure**: the Agra treasure ruins everyone who touches it.
- **Empire and justice**: the treasure comes from British India, and the novel shares the Empire’s prejudices.
:::
