Historical fiction sets made-up characters in a real time and place. It can make the past feel close and alive, but it is still fiction, so a careful reader sets it beside history to see which details are true, which are invented, and why the author made those choices.

In this lesson you will tell the history in a historical novel from what the author invented, and compare a fictional portrayal of a time with a historical account of it.

## History and invention

:::definition Historical fiction
**Historical fiction** is a story set in a real period of the past. Its background, such as real events and ways of life, comes from history, while many of its characters, scenes and conversations are invented.
:::

Here is a passage from a novel and a passage from a history book, both written for this lesson.

**From a novel:**

> Tom’s fingers were raw from the icy river. He had sold his father’s watch to reach California, and every morning he swore that today his pan would shine. “Fortune favours the stubborn,” old Hal told him, and Tom believed it.

**From a history book:**

> Gold was found at Sutter’s Mill, California, in January 1848. By 1849 about 90,000 people had arrived to search for it. Most miners found little gold, and the merchants who sold them food and tools often grew richer than the miners did.

:::example Setting the two side by side
What does the novel share with the history book, what has the novelist invented, and what does the history tell us that Tom does not know?
@step Both show people coming to California in hope of finding gold and panning in rivers.
@step Tom, old Hal, the watch and their conversation are the novelist’s inventions.
@step The history book says most miners found little gold, so we fear that Tom’s hope is in vain.
@answer The Gold Rush is history and Tom’s story is invented, and setting them side by side lets us know more than Tom does.
:::

## Why authors use and alter history

A history book gives the wider facts; a novel shows what one person might have felt. Novelists imagine what the record leaves out, such as a captain’s face as his ship sinks, and they invent dialogue for real people, ideally in keeping with what is known of them. Sometimes they **alter** history, such as moving a battle by a year, to make the plot work, and many explain these changes in an **author’s note**.

:::mistake Anachronisms
An **anachronism** is a detail that does not belong to the period, such as a word or an invention from a later time. Comparing fiction with history helps us spot one.
:::
