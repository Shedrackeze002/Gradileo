Give two people the same rainy afternoon and one will write about ruined plans, the other about the smell of wet earth. The rain is the same. What differs is how each person sees it, and the words each chooses to make you see it that way too. Comparing two writers means comparing both: what they think, and how they make you feel it.

In this lesson you will identify each writer's viewpoint and attitude, name the methods that convey them, and write a sentence that compares the two.

## Viewpoint and attitude

:::definition Viewpoint
A writer's **viewpoint** is what the writer thinks and feels about the subject: an opinion, and the attitude that goes with it.
:::

A viewpoint is rarely stated in one sentence. You work it out from the whole text: what the writer chooses to mention, and the feeling in the words. Here are two short sources about winter.

> **Source A** (2023): Winter is four months of scraping ice off a car in the dark. Who decided this was acceptable?

> **Source B** (1866): The frost has made a cathedral of our lane. Every twig is silvered, and the very air rings like a bell.

The writer of Source A believes that winter is a miserable inconvenience. The writer of Source B believes that winter is a time of beauty.

:::check ex-compare-viewpoints 1
:::

## Comparing the viewpoints

A comparison sets one viewpoint against the other in a single sentence, with a comparing word.

| To show a difference | To show a similarity |
|---|---|
| whereas, while, but, however, in contrast | both, similarly, likewise, in the same way |

:::example A comparing sentence
Compare the two writers' viewpoints on winter.
@step Source A: winter is a miserable inconvenience.
@step Source B: winter is a time of beauty.
@answer The writer of Source A sees winter as a miserable inconvenience, whereas the writer of Source B sees it as a time of beauty.
:::

:::

## Tone

:::definition Tone
**Tone** is the attitude that the writing carries, as a tone of voice does in speech: angry, amused, admiring, bitter, hopeful.
:::

Two adjectives are usually more exact than one. Source A is *irritable and sarcastic*; Source B is *admiring and joyful*. Find the words that carry the feeling: "scraping", "in the dark" and the sharp question in Source A; "cathedral", "silvered" and "rings" in Source B.

:::check ex-compare-viewpoints 3
:::

## Methods

:::definition Method
A **method** is anything a writer does on purpose to affect the reader: a choice of word, an image, a kind of sentence, the way the text is built.
:::

| Method | What it is | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Simile | A comparison using *like* or *as* | "the very air rings like a bell" |
| Metaphor | Saying one thing is another | "The frost has made a cathedral of our lane" |
| Personification | Giving a thing human feelings or actions | "the wind complained at the door" |
| Rhetorical question | A question asked for effect, not for an answer | "Who decided this was acceptable?" |
| List of three | Three items in a row, for emphasis | "cold, dark and endless" |
| Direct address | Speaking to the reader as *you* | "You know the feeling." |
| Humorous exaggeration | Overstating for comic effect | "I have not been warm since October." |

:::check ex-compare-methods 1
:::

Naming a method is only the start. Say what it makes the reader picture or feel, and how that conveys the writer's viewpoint.

:::example From method to viewpoint
How does the metaphor "The frost has made a cathedral of our lane" convey the writer's viewpoint?
@step A cathedral is vast, beautiful and a place of wonder.
@step Calling the frozen lane a cathedral makes an ordinary place seem grand and almost holy.
@answer It shows that the writer looks at winter with awe, which conveys the view that winter is beautiful.
:::

:::mistake "It makes the reader want to read on"
A comment that could be written about any text explains nothing. Always say what **this** method makes the reader think or feel about **this** subject.
:::

:::check ex-compare-methods 2
:::

## Comparing the methods

The strongest comparison does four things in one sentence: it names each writer's method, quotes it, says what it is used to do, and joins the two with a comparing word.

> The writer of Source A uses a rhetorical question ("Who decided this was acceptable?") to invite the reader to share his irritation, whereas the writer of Source B uses a metaphor ("a cathedral of our lane") to present winter as a thing of wonder.

A sentence that only compares what the writers think has left out the methods. A sentence that lists methods without their purpose has left out the comparison of viewpoints. You need both.

:::check ex-compare-methods 3
:::

:::summary
- A writer's **viewpoint** is what the writer thinks and feels about the subject; the **tone** is the attitude the writing carries.
- Compare viewpoints in one sentence with a comparing word: *whereas*, *while*, *both*, *similarly*.
- A **method** is a deliberate choice: a simile, a metaphor, personification, a rhetorical question, a list of three, direct address, exaggeration.
- Explain each method: what it makes the reader picture or feel, and how that conveys the viewpoint.
- The best comparison names each method, quotes it, gives its purpose and sets the two writers side by side.
:::
