This lesson reads three more poems from the **Power and Conflict** cluster of the AQA anthology, all from the nineteenth and early twentieth centuries: **“My Last Duchess”** by Robert Browning, a duke’s chilling account of his first wife; **“The Charge of the Light Brigade”** by Alfred, Lord Tennyson, about a disastrous cavalry charge; and **“Exposure”** by Wilfred Owen, about soldiers freezing in the trenches of the First World War. All three are in the public domain, so every line quoted here is copied from the editions listed in the references.

In this lesson you will learn what each poem is about, recognise its key lines, and connect each poem to its context and to the other poems of the cluster.

## “My Last Duchess” (1842)

A duke shows a visitor a portrait of his late wife. Only the duke speaks: the poem is a **dramatic monologue**. His first words already sound like an owner showing off a possession:

> That’s my last Duchess painted on the wall,
> Looking as if she were alive.

He keeps the portrait behind a curtain that no one draws but him (“none puts by / The curtain I have drawn for you, but I”). As he talks, his complaint comes out: she smiled at everyone, and thanked people for small kindnesses as warmly as for “My gift of a nine-hundred-years-old name”. Then, calmly:

> I gave commands;
> Then all smiles stopped together.

He has almost certainly had her killed. At the end we learn why the visitor is there: the duke is arranging his next marriage. The poem is written in **rhyming couplets**, but the sentences run on across the lines, so the neat rhyme is half hidden, like the menace beneath the duke’s polished manners. The duke is usually linked to Alfonso II, Duke of Ferrara in Renaissance Italy, whose young wife died in suspicious circumstances.

:::example Reading the duke’s words
The duke says: “I gave commands; / Then all smiles stopped together.” What does he mean, and how does he say it?
@step He has just complained that the duchess smiled at everyone, so the smiles that stopped are hers.
@step He gave orders, and then she stopped smiling for ever: he is hinting that he had her killed.
@answer The duke admits to a murder in two short, calm sentences. His lack of emotion shows how easily he uses his **power**, and how little he valued her.
:::

## “The Charge of the Light Brigade” (1854)

In October 1854, during the Crimean War, a misunderstood order sent about six hundred British light cavalry charging down a valley at the Russian guns, at the Battle of Balaclava, and many were killed. Tennyson, the **Poet Laureate**, wrote the poem weeks later, after reading newspaper reports. Its galloping **dactylic** rhythm (one stressed beat and two unstressed) imitates the horses:

> Half a league, half a league,
> Half a league onward,

The soldiers know that a commander has made a mistake, yet none of them falters: “Was there a man dismay’d? / Not tho’ the soldier knew”. Their duty is simply to obey:

> Their’s not to reason why,
> Their’s but to do and die,

They ride “Into the jaws of Death, / Into the mouth of Hell”, where death is a monster that swallows them. The last stanza is a string of commands to the reader: “Honour the charge they made! / Honour the Light Brigade,”. The poem admits the blunder, but its purpose is to celebrate the soldiers’ courage.

## “Exposure” (1920)

Wilfred Owen served as an officer in the trenches of the First World War, and wrote this poem from experience in the winter of 1917. He was killed in November 1918, a week before the war ended, and his poems were published after his death. In “Exposure”, the soldiers’ real enemy is the weather:

> Our brains ache, in the merciless iced east winds that knife us

The wind is personified as an attacker with a knife. Each morning brings no hope: “Dawn massing in the east her melancholy army / Attacks once more in ranks on shivering ranks of gray”. Half asleep, the men imagine going home as ghosts (“Slowly our ghosts drag home”), and at the end a burial party finds men frozen to death, and “All their eyes are ice”. Several stanzas end with the same short line, “But nothing happens.” The **half-rhymes**, words that nearly but not quite rhyme, sound uneasy and unfinished, like the waiting itself.

## Themes and comparisons

The duke’s calm voice shows **pride** and the **abuse of power** over another person. Tennyson presents war through **patriotism**, honouring obedience; Owen shows the **effects of conflict** with no glory at all, and makes the **power of nature** more deadly than the enemy.

## Context

These poems span the Victorian age to the First World War. Browning used the dramatic monologue to let villains reveal themselves. Tennyson wrote as the nation’s poet, celebrating its soldiers. Owen wrote against the idea that war is glorious, to show readers at home what the soldiers suffered.

:::mistake “The Charge of the Light Brigade” is not an anti-war poem
Tennyson admits that someone blundered, but the poem’s purpose is to honour the soldiers’ courage and obedience. Owen’s “Exposure” is the poem that shows war without glory.
:::

:::summary
- **“My Last Duchess”** (Browning, 1842): a dramatic monologue in rhyming couplets; a proud duke reveals that he had his wife killed.
- **“The Charge of the Light Brigade”** (Tennyson, 1854): a galloping, patriotic tribute to soldiers who obeyed a mistaken order.
- **“Exposure”** (Owen, written 1917, published 1920): soldiers freeze in the trenches; nature is the enemy, and “nothing happens”.
:::
