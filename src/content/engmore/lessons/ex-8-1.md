The **Power and Conflict** cluster of the AQA poetry anthology has fifteen poems, written over more than two hundred years, about the power of rulers, of nature and of war, and what conflict does to people. This lesson reads three poems from the Romantic period: **“Ozymandias”** by Percy Bysshe Shelley, **“London”** by William Blake and an **extract from *The Prelude*** by William Wordsworth. All three are in the public domain, so every line quoted here is copied from the editions listed in the references.

In this lesson you will learn what each poem is about, recognise its key lines, and connect each poem to the time it was written and to the other poems of the cluster.

## “Ozymandias” (1818)

A traveller describes the ruins of a huge statue in the desert: two legs of stone, and a shattered face half sunk in the sand. The sculptor captured the king’s cruelty perfectly:

> Half sunk, a shattered visage lies, whose frown,
> And wrinkled lip, and sneer of cold command,

On the pedestal, the king’s words survive:

> ‘My name is Ozymandias, king of kings:
> Look on my works, ye Mighty, and despair!’

Then comes the blunt reply of the poem: “Nothing beside remains.” The last line leaves only the desert: “The lone and level sands stretch far away.”

The poem is a **sonnet**, but its rhyme scheme is irregular, as if the form itself is breaking down like the statue. It is also told at a distance: the speaker reports what a traveller said about what a sculptor carved, so the once-mighty king seems far away and small. Ozymandias was the Greek name for Ramesses II of Egypt.

:::example Reading the irony
The inscription says: *‘Look on my works, ye Mighty, and despair!’* What does it mean now?
@step The king meant: other rulers should despair, because they can never match his greatness.
@step But “Nothing beside remains”: there are no works left to look at.
@answer The boast has become **ironic**. The mighty should still despair, but because even the greatest power is destroyed by time.
:::

## “London” (1794)

From William Blake’s *Songs of Experience*. A speaker walks through London and sees suffering in every face. The opening repeats one word:

> I wander through each chartered street,
> Near where the chartered Thames does flow,

“Chartered” means mapped out, owned and controlled: even the streets and the river belong to the powerful. The people are trapped by “The mind-forged manacles”, chains made of ideas they have been taught to accept. Blake then blames the institutions of power directly:

> How the chimney-sweeper’s cry
> Every blackening church appals,
> And the hapless soldier’s sigh
> Runs in blood down palace-walls.

The church is blackened by soot and by guilt, because it lets child chimney sweeps suffer; the soldier’s blood stains the palace, blaming the king for his death. The poem’s four regular quatrains have a steady beat, like the speaker’s trudging footsteps through misery that never ends.

## Extract from *The Prelude* (1850)

*The Prelude* is Wordsworth’s long poem about the growth of his own mind, published after his death. In this extract the speaker remembers taking a boat out on a lake one summer evening as a boy. He knows it is wrong, “an act of stealth / And troubled pleasure”, and he rows out “Proud of his skill”. Then a mountain seems to rise up behind the ridge:

> a huge peak, black and huge,
> As if with voluntary power instinct
> Upreared its head.

Terrified, he rows back “With trembling oars”. For days afterwards, “huge and mighty forms, that do not live / Like living men” move through his mind and trouble his dreams. The poem is in **blank verse**, unrhymed lines of iambic pentameter that follow the rhythm of thought and memory. The mood moves from confident pleasure, to fear, to a lasting unease: nature has humbled him.

## Themes and comparisons

All three poems are about **power**. In “Ozymandias”, human **pride** and the **abuse of power** are defeated by the **power of nature** and time. In “London”, the abuse of power is in the present, and the poem is a **protest** against it. In *The Prelude*, the power belongs to nature, which fills a proud boy with **fear** and stays in his **memory**.

## Context

Shelley, Blake and Wordsworth were **Romantic** poets. The Romantics valued nature, imagination and strong feeling, and many sympathised with the French Revolution of 1789, which had overthrown a king. Shelley was a radical who hated tyranny; Blake attacked the church and the monarchy for the misery of London’s poor; Wordsworth believed that nature shapes who we become.

:::mistake “London” is not a description of a tourist’s walk
The speaker is not admiring the city. Every image of London is of suffering, and the poem blames the church, the monarchy and the people with power for it.
:::

:::summary
- **“Ozymandias”** (Shelley, 1818): a ruined statue shows that a tyrant’s pride and power are destroyed by time; an irregular sonnet, told at a distance.
- **“London”** (Blake, 1794): a protest against the abuse of power, in four regular quatrains, with the repeated word “chartered”.
- **Extract from *The Prelude*** (Wordsworth, 1850): a boy’s pride turns to fear before the power of nature, which stays in his memory; blank verse.
:::
