*Great Expectations* is Charles Dickens’s novel of 1861, first published in weekly parts. Pip, an orphan raised by his harsh sister and her gentle husband Joe, the village blacksmith, is suddenly told that he has “great expectations”: an unknown benefactor will make him a gentleman. The older Pip tells the story, looking back with regret at how money and ambition changed him.

In this lesson you will learn who the characters are and the order of the story, read its key quotations closely, and connect the novel to Victorian ideas about class, money and being a gentleman.

## The story in three stages

| Stage | What happens |
|---|---|
| Pip’s childhood | An escaped convict terrifies Pip in the churchyard, and Pip brings him food. Pip visits Miss Havisham’s Satis House, where Estella makes him ashamed of being common. He is apprenticed to Joe. |
| Pip’s expectations | Mr Jaggers announces that Pip will become a gentleman. Pip moves to London, grows ashamed of Joe, and loves the cold Estella. The convict, Magwitch, returns and reveals that he is the benefactor. |
| Pip’s ruin and growth | Miss Havisham begs forgiveness and is burned. The escape with Magwitch fails, and Magwitch dies. Joe nurses Pip and pays his debts. Years later Pip meets Estella in the ruins of Satis House. |

## The characters

**Pip** narrates. **Joe Gargery** is loyal and good, though he has no money or polish. **Magwitch**, the convict, turns out to love Pip. **Miss Havisham**, jilted on her wedding day, has stopped every clock and raised **Estella** to break men’s hearts. **Mr Jaggers** is the powerful lawyer, **Herbert Pocket** Pip’s friend, and **Biddy** the kind girl who later marries Joe.

## Key quotations

At Satis House Estella sneers: “And what coarse hands he has! And what thick boots!” For the first time Pip is ashamed of being working class, and soon he tells Biddy, “I want to be a gentleman.” His ambition grows from shame, not from any wish to be better.

:::example Reading a quotation
Magwitch tells Pip: *“Yes, Pip, dear boy, I’ve made a gentleman on you! It’s me wot has done it!”* What does this reveal?
@step Pip had believed that Miss Havisham was his benefactor.
@step The money came from a transported convict, the man Pip thinks least of.
@answer Pip’s gentility was bought with a convict’s money, so being a gentleman is shown to be a matter of money, not birth or worth.
:::

Joe, leaving Pip’s rooms after an awkward visit, says: “Pip, dear old chap, life is made of ever so many partings welded together”. He uses the language of the forge, with quiet dignity. Estella tells Miss Havisham, “I am what you have made me,” and at the end she says that suffering has bent and broken her, “but—I hope—into a better shape.”

## Context and Dickens’s methods

In Victorian England a “gentleman” was supposed to be a man of good birth and manners who did not work with his hands. Dickens questions this: Joe, a blacksmith, is the truest gentleman in the novel. **Expectations** meant an inheritance you expected to receive. The novel came out in weekly parts, so many chapters end on a hook. Because the older Pip narrates, he can judge his younger self’s snobbery.

:::mistake Pip is not the hero he thinks he is
The older narrator often criticises his younger self, for example for being ashamed of Joe. The novel is about learning what really matters.
:::

:::summary
- Three stages: Pip’s childhood, his **expectations** in London, and his ruin and growth.
- **Social class and ambition**: Estella’s scorn makes Pip ashamed of his home.
- **Wealth and gentility**: Magwitch, a convict, paid for Pip to be a gentleman; Joe is the true one.
- **Revenge and its cost**: Miss Havisham’s revenge damages Estella and herself.
:::
