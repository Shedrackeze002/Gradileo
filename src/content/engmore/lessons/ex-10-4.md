*Animal Farm* is a short novel, or novella, by George Orwell, first published in 1945. The animals of Manor Farm rise up against their drunken farmer and run the farm themselves, promising that all animals will be equal. But the pigs, led by Napoleon, slowly take all the power and become as cruel as the humans they replaced. Orwell called it a fairy story, but it is a political **allegory** of the Russian Revolution. The book is still in copyright in some countries, so this lesson tells its story and discusses its key moments in our own words, without quoting it.

In this lesson you will learn who the characters are, the order of the main events and the chapter each happens in, read its key moments closely, and connect it to the history Orwell was writing about.

## The story in ten chapters

| Chapters | What happens |
|---|---|
| 1 to 2 | Old Major tells the animals of his dream of a world without humans. After his death, the animals drive out Mr Jones, rename the farm and paint the Seven Commandments on the barn wall. |
| 3 to 5 | The pigs keep the milk and apples for themselves. The animals beat off Mr Jones at the Battle of the Cowshed. Napoleon’s dogs chase Snowball off the farm, and Napoleon ends the animals’ meetings. |
| 6 to 8 | The windmill falls in a storm, and Napoleon blames Snowball. Animals who confess to plotting with Snowball are killed. The commandments are quietly changed. |
| 9 to 10 | Boxer collapses and is sold to a horse slaughterer. Years later the pigs walk on two legs, and at a dinner with the farmers the animals cannot tell the pigs from the men. |

## The characters

**Old Major**, a prize boar, inspires the rebellion. **Napoleon**, a fierce boar, takes power and rules as a tyrant. **Snowball**, clever and lively, plans the windmill and is driven out. **Squealer**, a persuasive pig, explains away every decision Napoleon makes. **Boxer**, the strong cart-horse, works harder and harder for the farm. **Benjamin**, an old donkey, understands what is happening but rarely speaks. **Mr Jones** is the farmer the animals drive out.

## Key moments

When the milk and apples go missing, Squealer explains that the pigs need them to keep their brains working for the good of everyone. He twists the truth so that the pigs’ greed sounds like a sacrifice: this is **propaganda**. Napoleon then seizes power with force, not argument, using the dogs he raised in secret to chase Snowball away.

The cruellest moment comes when Boxer, worn out from work, is taken away in a slaughterer’s van, while Squealer tells the animals that he died peacefully in hospital. At the end, the animals watching through a window can no longer see any difference between the pigs and the human farmers. The revolution has come full circle.

## Context and Orwell’s methods

Orwell based the story on Russia after the revolution of 1917. Old Major suggests Marx and Lenin, Napoleon suggests Stalin, and Snowball suggests Trotsky, whom Stalin drove into exile. Orwell supported socialism, but he believed Stalin had betrayed it. Telling the story as a simple animal **fable** makes the pattern of power, lies and betrayal clear to any reader, in any country. As each rule is broken, the pigs rewrite the Seven Commandments, until a single commandment is left that openly places the pigs above everyone else.

:::mistake The book is not against all revolutions
Orwell was not arguing that the animals were wrong to rebel against Mr Jones. He shows how a revolution is betrayed when power falls into the hands of a few, and the others are too ignorant or too afraid to stop them.
:::

:::summary
- **Power and corruption**: Napoleon becomes the tyrant the animals rebelled against.
- **Propaganda**: Squealer’s lies and the rewritten commandments.
- **Class and equality**: the pigs exploit the workers they claim to serve.
- **Revolution** and **ignorance**: the animals cannot read or remember well enough to resist.
:::
