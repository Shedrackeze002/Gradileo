*An Inspector Calls* is a play by J. B. Priestley, first performed in 1945 and set in 1912. On the night the Birlings celebrate their daughter’s engagement, a police inspector arrives to tell them that a young woman has died. One by one, he shows that each member of the family played a part in her ruin. The play is still in copyright, so this lesson tells its story and discusses its key moments in our own words, without quoting it.

In this lesson you will learn who the characters are and the order of the play, read its key moments closely, and connect the play to the time Priestley wrote it.

## The play in three acts

The whole play takes place in one evening, in the Birlings’ dining room.

| Act | What happens |
|---|---|
| Act 1 | The Birlings celebrate Sheila’s engagement to Gerald. Mr Birling boasts about the future. The Inspector arrives: Mr Birling sacked Eva Smith for leading a strike, and Sheila had her dismissed from a shop. |
| Act 2 | Gerald admits that he kept the young woman, then calling herself Daisy Renton, as his mistress. Mrs Birling admits that her charity committee refused the pregnant girl help, and blames the child’s father. |
| Act 3 | Eric admits that he was the father, and that he stole money to help her. The Inspector warns them all and leaves. The family find that he may not be a real inspector, but then the telephone rings. |

## The characters

**Arthur Birling** is a wealthy factory owner who believes a man should look after himself and his own family. **Sybil Birling**, his wife, is cold and snobbish. Their children, **Sheila** and **Eric**, are the first to accept their guilt. **Gerald Croft** is Sheila’s fiancé, the son of a rival businessman. **Inspector Goole** questions each of them in turn, and **Eva Smith**, the young woman, is never seen on stage.

## Key moments

At dinner, Mr Birling confidently tells the young men that there will be no war, and that the new liner *Titanic* cannot sink. The 1945 audience knew that both claims were wrong, so this **dramatic irony** shows that his views about society are just as mistaken.

Mrs Birling insists that the father of the girl’s child should be made to pay, without realising that she is condemning her own son. Before he leaves, the Inspector warns the family that people are responsible for each other, and that if they do not learn it, they will learn it through suffering. After he has gone, the parents are relieved to find he may have been a hoax, but Sheila and Eric are still ashamed. Priestley places his hope in the young, who can change.

## Context and Priestley’s methods

Priestley was a **socialist**. He set the play in 1912, before two world wars, but wrote it in 1945, when Britain was deciding what kind of country to build after the Second World War. Mr Birling speaks for selfish **capitalism**; the Inspector speaks for **social responsibility**. The Inspector’s name, Goole, sounds like “ghoul”, and with his knowledge of everything he seems more like a conscience than a policeman. The play keeps strictly to one room and one evening, and the final telephone call starts the investigation again, so the family, and the audience, cannot escape its message.

:::mistake The play is not just a murder mystery
No one is accused of murder. The question is not who killed Eva Smith, but how a whole family, and a whole society, helped to destroy her.
:::

:::summary
- **Social responsibility**: we are all responsible for each other.
- **Class**: the Birlings use their power over a working-class woman.
- **Age and youth**: Sheila and Eric change; their parents refuse to.
- **Dramatic irony** and a **cyclical ending** drive Priestley’s message home.
:::
