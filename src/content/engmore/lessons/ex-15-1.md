A seminar is a discussion in which a group works together to understand a text more deeply than any one member could alone. There is no winner, and the teacher often says very little. The quality of the seminar depends on how well each student prepares, listens, questions and draws the threads together.

In this lesson you will prepare for a seminar, set norms and roles with your group, and lead and take part in the discussion by asking questions that move it forward, probing reasoning and synthesizing what has been said.

## Preparing for a seminar

:::definition Seminar
A **seminar** (sometimes called a Socratic seminar) is a structured discussion of a text or an idea, in which participants ask open questions, support their claims with evidence and build on one another’s thinking.
:::

Come with the text annotated, two or three passages marked with page references, and at least one **open question**: a question with more than one defensible answer, such as *Why does the narrator never tell us what she decided?* A closed question, such as *In what year was the novel published?*, has one answer and ends the conversation.

:::tip Norms, goals and roles
Before the seminar, the group agrees on **norms** (everyone points to the text; nobody decides alone), a **goal** (for example, one shared claim by Friday) and **roles**, such as a facilitator who tracks time and steers back to the goal.
:::

## Leading and taking part

| Move | Example |
|---|---|
| propel the discussion toward a larger idea | *What does the ending suggest about whether people can change?* |
| probe reasoning and evidence | *Which passage supports that? I read the letter scene differently.* |
| draw in a quiet voice | *Sam, you marked this page. What did you notice?* |
| synthesize | *We agree the narrator is unreliable, but we are split on whether she knows it.* |

:::example Moving a stalled seminar
A seminar has spent ten minutes on plot details. Write a question that moves it toward a theme.
@step Plot questions have single answers, so the talk has nowhere to go.
@step A question about what the events suggest invites several readings, each backed by evidence.
@answer What does the ending suggest about whether people can change?
:::

Respond to perspectives unlike your own by asking what the speaker noticed, and when the evidence persuades you, **qualify** your claim aloud and say what changed your mind. That is a sign of a strong participant, not a weak one.

:::summary
- Prepare with an annotated text, marked passages and **open questions**.
- Agree on **norms**, a **goal** and **roles** before you begin.
- **Propel** the discussion, **probe** for evidence and draw in every voice.
- **Synthesize** at the end, and **qualify** your views when the evidence warrants it.
:::
