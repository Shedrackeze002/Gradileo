Some of the most important arguments in American history were written to persuade: newspaper essays urging New York to ratify the Constitution, and Supreme Court opinions deciding what that Constitution means. Reading them closely means finding each argument’s premises, following its steps and judging whether its conclusions follow.

In this lesson you will trace and evaluate the reasoning of *The Federalist* on faction, checks and balances and the courts, and of Supreme Court opinions and dissents on equality.

## The Federalist

:::definition The Federalist
*The Federalist* is a series of 85 essays written in 1787 and 1788 by Alexander Hamilton, James Madison and John Jay under the shared pen name *Publius*, to persuade New Yorkers to ratify the Constitution.
:::

In No. 10, Madison takes on the danger of **faction**: a group of citizens united by a passion or interest against the rights of others or the good of the community [1]. His argument moves in steps.

:::example Tracing Madison’s argument in No. 10
How does Madison reason from the causes of faction to the case for a large republic?
@step Faction could be ended by destroying liberty, but “Liberty is to faction what air is to fire” [1], and abolishing liberty would be a cure worse than the disease.
@step Faction is sown in human nature, so “the CAUSES of faction cannot be removed, and … relief is only to be sought in the means of controlling its EFFECTS” [1].
@step A large republic controls the effects: “Extend the sphere, and you take in a greater variety of parties and interests” [1], so no single faction easily becomes an oppressive majority.
@answer Since faction cannot be removed without destroying liberty, it must be controlled, and a large republic controls it best by diluting any one faction among many.
:::

In No. 51, Madison reasons from a realistic view of human nature to checks and balances [2]:

> If men were angels, no government would be necessary. If angels were to govern men, neither external nor internal controls on government would be necessary.

Since people are not angels, government must control the governed and also control itself, and so “Ambition must be made to counteract ambition” [2]: each branch guards its own powers against the others. In No. 78, Hamilton argues that the judiciary is the “least dangerous” branch, because it has “neither FORCE nor WILL, but merely judgment” [3].

## Opinions and dissents of the Supreme Court

:::definition Kinds of opinion
The **majority opinion** decides a case and explains why. A **concurring opinion** agrees with the decision for different reasons. A **dissenting opinion** disagrees, and although it decides nothing, it can shape later law.
:::

In *Plessy v. Ferguson* (1896), the Court upheld a law requiring racial segregation on trains, under the doctrine of “separate but equal.” Justice John Marshall Harlan dissented alone [4]:

> Our Constitution is color-blind, and neither knows nor tolerates classes among citizens.

Fifty-eight years later, in *Brown v. Board of Education* (1954), a unanimous Court held [5]:

> We conclude that, in the field of public education, the doctrine of "separate but equal" has no place. Separate educational facilities are inherently unequal.

To call segregated schools *inherently* unequal is to say that separation is unequal in itself, however equal the buildings may be, and in reaching that conclusion the Court came round, in public education, to a principle close to the one Harlan had stood for alone.

:::tip Evaluating legal reasoning
Ask whether the premises hold, whether the conclusion follows from them, what purpose the opinion serves, and how it answers the strongest argument on the other side.
:::

:::summary
- *The Federalist* argued for ratification; trace each essay from its **premises** to its **conclusion**.
- No. 10: faction cannot be removed, so control its effects with a **large republic**.
- No. 51: people are not angels, so **ambition must counteract ambition**; No. 78: the judiciary has only **judgment**.
- A **dissent** can anticipate later law, as Harlan’s did before *Brown*.
:::
