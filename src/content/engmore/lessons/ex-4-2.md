Many English courses ask you to speak, not only to write: to give a short presentation on a subject you care about, and then to answer questions from your audience. A good talk has the same backbone as a good essay, but it is built for listeners, who cannot look back up a page.

In this lesson you will plan and deliver a presentation that holds an audience, use standard English, and answer questions politely and directly.

## Planning a presentation

:::definition Presentation
A **presentation** is a prepared talk to an audience on a chosen subject, usually followed by questions.
:::

A clear structure helps listeners follow.

1. **Grab attention**: a question, a short story or a striking picture in words.
2. **Say what you will cover**: “I’m going to talk about three things.”
3. **Make each point in turn**, with **signposts**: “First…”, “My second point is…”, “Finally…”.
4. **Sum up** the main points and end with a clear final message.
5. **Thank the audience**, and invite their questions.

:::example A strong opening
The topic is *why everyone should learn to swim*. Which opening is better: *“Hello. My presentation is about swimming.”* or *“Hands up if you can swim. Now imagine falling into deep water and not being able to.”*?
@step The first names the topic but gives the audience no reason to listen.
@step The second involves the audience at once and makes them feel why the topic matters.
@answer The second.
:::

## Delivering it

- **Use prompt cards**, not a full script, so that you speak to the audience rather than read at them.
- **Make eye contact** with different parts of the room.
- **Vary your pace and tone**, and **pause** after an important point.
- **Signpost** so that listeners always know where they are.

## Standard English

:::definition Standard English
**Standard English** is the form of English used in formal speech and writing, with standard grammar and vocabulary. It can be spoken in any accent.
:::

In a formal presentation, avoid non-standard forms.

| Non-standard | Standard |
|---|---|
| *We was surprised.* | *We were surprised.* |
| *I done the research.* | *I did the research.* |
| *There ain’t no easy answer.* | *There isn’t an easy answer.* |
| *Them figures show…* | *Those figures show…* |

## Answering questions

A good answer is **polite**, **honest** and **direct**: it answers the actual question, gives a reason, and links back to your argument where it can.

:::example Answering a challenge
After a talk on why the school day should start later, someone asks: *“Wouldn’t a later finish clash with after-school clubs?”*
@step Accept the point fairly: it could.
@step Answer it with a reason or a solution.
@answer “It could, yes, so clubs might run at lunchtime or start a little later.”
:::

:::mistake Don’t dismiss the questioner
“That isn’t what my talk was about” or “You weren’t listening” loses the audience at once. If you do not know the answer, say so honestly and offer to find out.
:::

:::summary
- Structure a presentation: grab attention, say what you will cover, make signposted points, sum up, thank the audience.
- Deliver it with prompt cards, eye contact, varied pace and pauses.
- Use **standard English** in a formal talk; it can be spoken in any accent.
- Answer questions politely, honestly and directly, with a reason, and never dismiss the questioner.
:::
