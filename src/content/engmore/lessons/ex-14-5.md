Some stories are so old and so widely known that writers can call them up in a single word. Calling a weakness an *Achilles’ heel*, or a contest a *David and Goliath* match, borrows the meaning of a whole story. Many modern novels and films go further, and retell an ancient myth or an older work in a new setting.

In this lesson you will explain allusions to myths, the Bible and literature, and show how a modern story draws on an older one and makes it new.

## Allusions

:::definition Allusion
An **allusion** is a brief reference to a well-known story, person, place or event. It borrows meaning from what the reader already knows.
:::

| Allusion | The story behind it | What it means |
|---|---|---|
| Achilles’ heel | the Greek hero Achilles could be wounded only in his heel | a small weakness in someone strong |
| Pandora’s box | Pandora opened a jar that released every evil into the world | a source of many unexpected troubles |
| the Midas touch | King Midas turned everything he touched into gold | the knack of making everything succeed |
| an odyssey | Odysseus took ten years to sail home in Homer’s *Odyssey* | a long, eventful journey |
| David and Goliath | in the Bible, young David defeats the giant Goliath with a sling | a weak side beating a far stronger one |

:::example Explaining an allusion
A reporter writes: *“Opening the old files was like opening Pandora’s box.”* Explain the allusion.
@step In the Greek myth, Pandora opened a jar, later called a box, and let all the evils out into the world.
@step So the reporter means that opening the files let loose a stream of problems nobody expected.
@answer It means that opening the files released a whole series of unexpected troubles.
:::

## Myths in modern stories

Modern writers draw on myths and traditional stories because their patterns already carry meaning. A story about a young inventor who ignores warnings, flies too high and crashes calls up the myth of **Icarus**, whose wax wings melted when he flew too close to the sun.

:::definition Archetype
An **archetype** is a pattern of events or a type of character that recurs across myths and modern stories. The **hero’s journey**, in which a hero leaves home, faces trials with a wise guide’s help and returns changed, is one of the best known.
:::

The interesting question is what the new story **changes**. If the modern Icarus survives and learns from her fall, the writer has turned a story about punishment into one about learning from failure. Names can allude too: a character called Cassandra, whose warnings nobody believes, echoes the Greek prophet who foresaw the truth and was never believed.

:::summary
- An **allusion** borrows meaning from a myth, the Bible, history or literature.
- To explain one, recall the original story and the quality it is famous for.
- Modern stories draw on myths and **archetypes** such as the hero’s journey.
- Ask what the new story **changes**, because the change often reveals its theme.
:::
