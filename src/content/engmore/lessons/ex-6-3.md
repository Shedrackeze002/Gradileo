*Pride and Prejudice* is Jane Austen’s novel of 1813. The five Bennet sisters must marry well, because their father’s house will pass to a male cousin when he dies. The second daughter, Elizabeth, takes an instant dislike to the proud Mr Darcy, and only slowly learns that her first impressions were wrong. It is a love story, and also a sharp, comic look at marriage, money and class.

In this lesson you will learn who the characters are and the order of the story, read its key quotations closely, and connect the novel to the world of the Regency gentry.

## The story

| Part | What happens |
|---|---|
| Meryton | Darcy calls Elizabeth only “tolerable”. Bingley falls for Jane. Wickham tells Elizabeth that Darcy wronged him. Elizabeth refuses Mr Collins, who marries Charlotte Lucas instead. |
| Hunsford | Darcy proposes, and Elizabeth angrily refuses. His letter tells her the truth about Wickham, and she sees how she misjudged him. |
| Pemberley | Elizabeth visits Darcy’s estate and finds him changed. Lydia runs away with Wickham, and Darcy secretly pays for them to marry. |
| Longbourn | Bingley proposes to Jane. Lady Catherine tries to stop Elizabeth marrying Darcy. Darcy proposes again and is accepted. |

## Key quotations

The first sentence is famous for its **irony**: “It is a truth universally acknowledged, that a single man in possession of a good fortune must be in want of a wife.” Really it is the poor families who want the rich man.

:::example Reading a quotation
At the ball, Darcy says of Elizabeth: *“She is tolerable: but not handsome enough to tempt me”*. Why does this matter so much?
@step Darcy says it where Elizabeth can hear.
@step It shows his pride, and it wounds hers.
@answer This is where Elizabeth’s prejudice begins; as she admits, she could forgive his pride “if he had not mortified mine”.
:::

Charlotte Lucas is practical: “Happiness in marriage is entirely a matter of chance.” and, on accepting Mr Collins, “I am not romantic, you know. I never was. I ask only a comfortable home”. After reading Darcy’s letter, Elizabeth realises: “Till this moment, I never knew myself.” Facing Lady Catherine, she stands up for herself: “He is a gentleman; I am a gentleman’s daughter; so far we are equal.”

## Context and Austen’s methods

The Bennets’ house is **entailed**: it can only pass to a male heir, Mr Collins. Daughters of the gentry could not easily earn a living, so a good marriage was their security. A sister’s scandal, like Lydia’s elopement, could ruin all their chances. Austen first called the novel *First Impressions*. Her narrator often says one thing and means another, so the reader must notice the irony.

:::mistake Pride is not only Darcy’s fault
Both are proud and both are prejudiced. Elizabeth prides herself on judging people, and her prejudice is as strong as his pride.
:::

:::summary
- Four places: **Meryton, Hunsford, Pemberley, Longbourn**, from first impressions to a second proposal.
- **Pride and prejudice**: both Darcy and Elizabeth must change.
- **Marriage and money**: the entail, Charlotte’s practical choice, Lydia’s disgrace.
- **Irony**: Austen’s narrator, and comic characters like Mr Collins, mock snobbery and greed.
:::
