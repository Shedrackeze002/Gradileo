Some writing tasks give you a picture and ask you to describe it. Nothing has to happen: there is no plot to invent. That sounds easy, but it means every sentence has to earn its place through what it lets the reader see, hear and feel.

In this lesson you will choose and describe details that fit a picture, plan a description from the wide view to a small detail, and use imagery and the senses to show rather than tell.

## Starting from the picture

:::definition Description
A **description** recreates a place, person or moment in the reader’s mind through precise detail, so that the reader experiences it rather than being told about it.
:::

Begin by looking closely. Note the time of day, the weather, the light, the people and the small details at the edges. You may add sounds, smells and textures that the picture suggests, but your description must not contradict it: a picture of a winter beach is not a description of a crowded summer one.

## Precise and sensory detail

Vague words such as *nice*, *very*, *a lot* and *things* tell the reader nothing. Precise nouns and verbs do the work.

| Vague | Precise |
|---|---|
| The wind was very strong. | The wind tore at the shutters. |
| There were lots of nice smells. | Warm sugar and cinnamon hung in the air. |
| He walked slowly. | He shuffled, dragging one foot. |

Use the senses beyond sight: sound, smell, touch and even taste.

## Show, not tell

:::definition Show, not tell
**Telling** names a feeling or quality (*she was nervous*). **Showing** gives the details that reveal it (*she twisted the strap of her bag*), so the reader works it out.
:::

:::example Showing a feeling
Rewrite *“The old house was creepy.”* so that it shows rather than tells.
@step Ask what makes a house creepy: neglect, darkness, odd sounds.
@step Give those details instead of the word “creepy”.
@answer The gate hung from one hinge, and somewhere inside a door creaked slowly open.
:::

## Imagery and sound

| Method | What it does | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Simile** | Compares, using *like* or *as* | The frost was as sharp as broken glass. |
| **Metaphor** | Says one thing *is* another | The city was a hive. |
| **Personification** | Gives a living thing’s action to something that is not alive | The waves gnawed at the cliff. |
| **Onomatopoeia** | A word that sounds like its meaning | Rain pattered on the roof. |
| **Alliteration** | Repeats a sound at the start of nearby words | Silent snow settled softly. |

## Structuring a description

A description needs a shape, just as a story does. One clear plan works like a camera:

1. **The wide view**: the whole scene, its light and weather.
2. **Closer**: one part of the scene, with movement and sound.
3. **A small detail**: one object or face, close up.
4. **A change**: the light fades, the tide turns, the crowd thins. Returning to the opening image, changed, gives a **cyclical** ending.

:::check ex-describe-detail 3
:::

:::mistake Clichés and lists
“It was a dark and stormy night” has been written too many times to make a reader see anything. Avoid clichés, avoid starting every sentence the same way, and do not simply list everything in the picture: choose the details that matter.
:::

:::check ex-describe-craft 3
:::

:::summary
- Look closely at the picture, and never contradict it; add sounds, smells and textures it suggests.
- Use **precise** nouns and verbs and the **senses**, not vague words.
- **Show, don’t tell**: give the details that reveal a feeling.
- Use **imagery** (simile, metaphor, personification) and **sound** (onomatopoeia, alliteration) with purpose.
- Structure it like a camera: wide view, closer, a small detail, then a change.
:::
