The other nine poems of the **Power and Conflict** cluster were written between the 1950s and the 2010s, and they are still in copyright. In this lesson you will learn who wrote each poem and what it is about, its form, the key moments to know, and the context that helps to explain it.

## Nature and war

**“Storm on the Island”** by Seamus Heaney (1966). Islanders who have built their houses low and strong describe a storm that batters them. The speaker begins confidently, sure that they are prepared, but ends by admitting that what they really fear is something they cannot even see. It is a single stanza of blank verse. Heaney grew up in Northern Ireland, and many readers link the unseen threat to the tension that led to the Troubles; the title has been read as a pun on Stormont, Northern Ireland’s parliament.

**“Bayonet Charge”** by Ted Hughes (1957). A soldier in the First World War is suddenly running at the enemy across a field, before he has had time to think. In the middle of the charge he stops, bewildered, and wonders why he is running at all; a hare caught in the gunfire thrashes in agony in front of him. Ideas such as honour and country fall away in the terror of the moment. Hughes did not fight, but his father survived the war, including Gallipoli.

**“Exposure”** and **“The Charge of the Light Brigade”** are older poems on the same subject (Lesson 8.2): compare how each writer presents the soldier’s experience.

## Soldiers who come home

**“Remains”** by Simon Armitage (2008). A soldier tells, in casual everyday speech, how he and two others shot a looter during a war in the Middle East. Back home, the memory will not leave him: he sees the dead man whenever he shuts his eyes. Armitage wrote it from interviews with real soldiers, for a documentary called *The Not Dead* about the mental effects of war.

**“Poppies”** by Jane Weir (2009). A mother remembers the day her son left home to join the army: she fixed a poppy on him and straightened his collar, holding back the urge to hug him. Afterwards she walks to a war memorial and waits, hoping to hear his voice. The ending is left open, so her fear and grief are never resolved. The poem was written for a collection of new war poems commissioned by Carol Ann Duffy.

**“War Photographer”** by Carol Ann Duffy (1985). A photographer develops his pictures of war in a darkroom at home. His hands were steady at the scenes of war, but now they tremble as the faces appear. He knows that readers of the Sunday papers will feel sorry for a moment, then forget. The poem has four six-line stanzas with a regular rhyme scheme, an orderly form for the chaos it remembers.

Which two poems show the lasting effect of war on someone who has left the fighting behind?
@step In “Remains”, the soldier is home, but sees the man he killed whenever he closes his eyes.
@step In “War Photographer”, the photographer is home, but the memories return as he develops his photographs.
@answer Both show that conflict follows people home, as **memory**. Armitage gives the soldier’s own voice; Duffy writes about the photographer in the third person.

## Power, identity and history

**“Tissue”** by Imtiaz Dharker (2006). The speaker reflects on paper, from maps and receipts to the thin pages of a family Koran, and compares it with the things people build and with human skin. Borders and buildings seem powerful, yet they are as fragile as paper. The title means both tissue paper and the tissue of the body.

**“The Émigrée”** by Carol Rumens (1993). A woman remembers the city she left as a child. The news says it is at war and ruled by tyrants, but in her memory it is always full of sunlight. By the end, she feels watched and threatened where she now lives. An *émigrée* is a woman who has left her country; Rumens never names the city, so the poem speaks for many exiles.

**“Kamikaze”** by Beatrice Garland (2013). A daughter tells her children how their grandfather, a Japanese pilot sent on a suicide mission in the Second World War, saw the fishing boats and the sea below him, remembered his childhood, and turned back. When he came home, his wife and neighbours acted as if he were invisible, and in time his own children stopped playing with him.

**“Checking Out Me History”** by John Agard. A speaker from the Caribbean complains that his British education taught him nursery rhymes and British heroes, while hiding the Black and Caribbean figures of his own history. He ends by taking charge of who he is. Agard, who was born in Guyana and moved to Britain, writes in Caribbean dialect, which is itself a protest against the standard English of that education.

## Form and methods

A poem’s **form** is how it is built: its stanzas, line lengths and rhyme, and whose voice speaks. Several of these poems use **free verse**, with no regular rhyme or metre, which suits speech and thought. “Remains” and “Checking Out Me History” are **dramatic monologues** in a speaker’s own voice; “Kamikaze” is told mostly in the third person, as a story passed down a family; “Storm on the Island” is one unbroken stanza, like the storm itself.
