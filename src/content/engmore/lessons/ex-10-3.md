*Lord of the Flies* is a novel by William Golding, first published in 1954. During a war, a plane carrying a group of British schoolboys crashes on a desert island, and no adults survive. The boys try to govern themselves, but their society falls apart into fear, violence and murder. The novel is still in copyright, so this lesson tells its story and discusses its key moments in our own words, without quoting it.

In this lesson you will learn who the characters are, the order of the main events and the chapter each happens in, read the novel’s key moments closely, and connect it to Golding’s view of human nature.

## The story in twelve chapters

| Chapters | What happens |
|---|---|
| 1 to 3 | Ralph and Piggy find a conch and call the boys together. Ralph is elected chief, and Jack’s choir become hunters. A signal fire, lit with Piggy’s glasses, burns out of control, and a small boy is lost. |
| 4 to 6 | Jack lets the signal fire go out to go hunting, just as a ship passes. Fear of a “beast” grows, and the dead body of a parachutist on the mountain is taken for it. |
| 7 to 9 | Jack breaks away and forms his own tribe. The hunters put a pig’s head on a stick as a gift for the beast. Simon discovers that the beast is not real, but is killed by the boys in a frenzied dance. |
| 10 to 12 | Jack’s tribe steals Piggy’s glasses. Roger sends a boulder down on Piggy, killing him and smashing the conch. Ralph is hunted across a burning island, and reaches the beach to find a naval officer. |

## The characters

**Ralph** is the elected chief, who wants to keep the signal fire burning and be rescued. **Piggy**, clever and asthmatic, values rules and is mocked by the others. **Jack**, leader of the choir, becomes obsessed with hunting and power. **Simon** is quiet and thoughtful, and the only one to discover the truth about the beast. **Roger** grows crueller until he kills without remorse. **Samneric**, the twins Sam and Eric, stay loyal to Ralph until they are captured.

## Key moments

At their first meeting, the boys agree that whoever holds the conch may speak. The conch stands for order and democracy, so when it is smashed with Piggy’s death, civilisation on the island ends.

When Jack lets the fire go out to go hunting, he puts the thrill of the kill before the hope of rescue. Later, alone in the forest, Simon faces the pig’s head on its stick, covered in flies, and seems to hear it tell him that the beast is part of the boys themselves. At the end, rescued at last, Ralph breaks down in tears for the innocence the boys have lost and the evil he has seen in them, while the officer turns away.

## Context and Golding’s methods

Golding served in the Royal Navy in the Second World War, and the war convinced him that cruelty lies within ordinary people. Earlier adventure stories showed shipwrecked British boys behaving well; Golding overturns that idea. The novel is an **allegory**: the island is a small version of the world, or a **microcosm**, and the conch, the fire and the beast are **symbols**. The ending is ironic: the officer is shocked by the boys’ war, but he is fighting a war of his own.

:::mistake The beast is not a creature on the island
The boys fear a monster, but the only “beast” they find is a dead parachutist. Simon understands that the real beast is the evil inside the boys themselves.
:::

:::summary
- **Civilisation vs savagery**: the conch and the fire against the hunt and the painted faces.
- **Power and leadership**: Ralph’s elected order against Jack’s rule by fear.
- **Fear**: the beast grows from the boys’ own fears.
- **Evil in human nature** and **loss of innocence**: without rules, even children turn to violence.
:::
