A good discussion is more than taking turns to talk. Each speaker comes ready with something to say, listens closely to the others, and builds on what they hear, so the group ends up understanding more than any one member did at the start.

In this lesson you will prepare for a discussion, agree on rules and roles, build on and paraphrase what others say, summarize a speaker’s points and judge the reasons they give.

## Coming prepared

:::definition A collaborative discussion
A **collaborative discussion** is a conversation in which a group works together to understand a text or a topic, or to reach a decision. Everyone is expected to take part.
:::

Before the discussion, read or study the material and bring evidence with you: two or three passages you can point to, a fact from your research, and a question you would like the group to think about.

:::tip Agree on rules and roles
Groups work best when they agree on a few rules, such as *each person speaks once before anyone speaks twice*, and share out roles:

| Role | What it involves |
|---|---|
| Leader | keeps the talk on topic and invites quiet members to speak |
| Note taker | records the main points and any decisions |
| Timekeeper | watches the clock and the group’s deadline |
:::

## Building on what others say

The best responses connect to what has just been said. You can **ask** about a remark (*What makes you think so?*), **link** it to evidence or to another speaker (*Maya said the ending was sad; could it also be hopeful?*), or **paraphrase** it before replying (*So you’re saying the rule is unfair to younger pupils*).

:::example Building on an idea
A classmate says, “I think the narrator is lying.” Write a response that builds on the idea.
@step Show that you listened, then ask for the reason or offer evidence that tests it.
@step Point to a specific part of the text, so the group can check it.
@answer What makes you think so? In chapter two he changes his story about where he was.
:::

## Summarizing and judging a speaker

To **summarize** a speaker, give their claim and the main reasons they offer for it, in your own words. To **evaluate** what they said, ask whether each reason is true, relevant to the claim and enough to support it.

:::mistake Taking a big word as proof
A speaker who says *everyone hates homework* has not given evidence. *Everyone* is rarely true, and disliking something does not show that it is harmful.
:::

Good speakers also change their views when someone shares new evidence, and say so: *That’s new to me; I now think the plan might work.* At the end, review what the group agreed on and where people still disagree.

:::summary
- Come **prepared**, with evidence and a question, and agree on rules and roles.
- **Build on** others’ remarks: ask about them, link them to evidence, or paraphrase them.
- A **summary** gives a speaker’s claim and reasons; an **evaluation** judges whether the reasons are sound.
- Change your view when new evidence warrants it, and review the discussion at the end.
:::
