*The Merchant of Venice* is a Shakespeare play first performed about 1597. Antonio, a Christian merchant, borrows money from Shylock, a Jewish moneylender he has publicly insulted, so that his friend Bassanio can woo the rich heiress Portia. Shylock asks, as a “merry” bond, for a pound of Antonio’s flesh if the loan is not repaid. When Antonio’s ships are lost, Shylock demands his bond, and Portia, disguised as a lawyer, comes to court.

In this lesson you will learn who the characters are and the order of the play, read its key speeches closely, and connect it to the prejudice of Shakespeare’s time and how audiences see it now.

## The play in five acts

| Act | What happens |
|---|---|
| Act 1 | Bassanio asks Antonio for money; Portia complains of her father’s casket test; Shylock lends three thousand ducats on the bond of a pound of flesh. |
| Act 2 | Shylock’s daughter Jessica elopes with Lorenzo, taking his money; the Prince of Morocco chooses the gold casket and loses. |
| Act 3 | Shylock vows revenge on Antonio; Bassanio chooses the lead casket and wins Portia; news comes that Antonio’s ships are lost. |
| Act 4 | In court, Portia shows the bond allows no blood; Shylock loses his wealth and is forced to become a Christian. |
| Act 5 | At Belmont, Portia and Nerissa tease their husbands about the rings, and the lovers are reunited. |

## Key speeches

Shylock reminds Antonio of how he has been treated: “You call me misbeliever, cut-throat dog, / And spet upon my Jewish gaberdine,”. Antonio shows his own contempt: “The devil can cite Scripture for his purpose.”

:::example Reading a speech
Shylock asks: *“If you prick us, do we not bleed? If you tickle us, do we not laugh? If you poison us, do we not die? And if you wrong us, shall we not revenge?”* What is he arguing?
@step The first three questions show that Jews are as human as Christians.
@step The last question turns that shared humanity into a reason for revenge.
@answer Shylock claims equal humanity, and then says that revenge is what Christians have taught him: “The villainy you teach me I will execute”.
:::

In court Portia pleads: “The quality of mercy is not strain’d, / It droppeth as the gentle rain from heaven / Upon the place beneath.” When Shylock refuses mercy, she beats him with the wording of his own bond: “This bond doth give thee here no jot of blood.” Stripped of his wealth, Shylock says: “you take my life / When you do take the means whereby I live.”

## Context and Shakespeare’s methods

Jewish people had been expelled from England in 1290, so few in Shakespeare’s audience had met a Jew, and **antisemitic** stereotypes were common. Christians thought lending at interest (**usury**) was sinful, and Jews were barred from many trades, so moneylending was one of the few open to them. The play is a **comedy**, ending in marriages, but Shylock’s ruin and forced conversion make it uncomfortable. Since the Holocaust, productions have focused on the abuse Shylock suffers. The caskets show the theme of appearance and reality: “All that glisters is not gold”.

:::mistake Shylock is not simply the villain
He is wronged as well as wronging. Shakespeare gives him the play’s most powerful speech about shared humanity, and the Christians who preach mercy show him little.
:::

:::summary
- Two plots: the **bond** in Venice and the **caskets** in Belmont, joined by Bassanio and Portia.
- **Prejudice and religion**: the Christians’ contempt for Shylock, and his answer: “Hath not a Jew eyes?”
- **Mercy and justice**: Portia’s plea for mercy, and the strict law turned against Shylock.
- **Appearance and reality**: the caskets, and Portia disguised as a lawyer.
:::
