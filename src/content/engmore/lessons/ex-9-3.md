The other nine poems of the **Love and Relationships** cluster were written between the 1960s and the 2000s, and they are still in copyright. So this lesson does not quote them: it describes each poem in our own words, and the references say where each was first published. Read the full poems in the AQA anthology alongside this lesson.

In this lesson you will learn who wrote each poem and what it is about, its form, the key moments to know, and the context that helps to explain it.

## Parents and children

**“Walking Away”** by Cecil Day-Lewis (1962). A father remembers watching his young son walk away from him at a school football match, eighteen years before, small and uncertain, like something pulled away into space. He reflects that a child becomes their own person by leaving, and that a parent shows love by allowing it. The poem has four five-line stanzas with a regular rhyme scheme, and it is addressed to the poet’s son.

**“Follower”** by Seamus Heaney (1966). The speaker remembers following his father’s plough as a boy, tripping and falling behind him, admiring his skill and wanting to grow up to be just like him. At the end the roles are reversed: now it is the ageing father who stumbles along behind the son. Heaney uses the precise words of ploughing to show his father’s expertise, in six regular quatrains.

**“Mother, any distance”** by Simon Armitage (1993). A young man measures the rooms of his first home, with his mother holding the end of the tape at the bottom of the stairs. The tape is like an umbilical cord, the bond he must stretch to leave. At the top of the house he reaches up towards open space, where he might either fail or soar. It is a loose, stretched **sonnet**, a love-poem form for a son’s love for his mother.

**“Before You Were Mine”** by Carol Ann Duffy (1993). A daughter imagines her mother as a carefree young woman, laughing with friends on a street corner and dancing in the city at night, in the years before the daughter was born. The daughter could not have seen any of this, so she builds it from photographs and stories. The word “mine” in the title is possessive, and she feels some guilt that her birth ended her mother’s glamorous life.

## Grandparents and memory

**“Eden Rock”** by Charles Causley (1988). The speaker pictures his dead parents as a young couple having a picnic beside a stream, described in loving, ordinary detail. They beckon him to cross the stream and join them, and he finds it easier than he expected. Crossing the stream suggests death and reunion. The last line stands apart from the rest, like the moment before he crosses.

**“Climbing My Grandfather”** by Andrew Waterhouse (2000). The speaker climbs his grandfather as if he were a mountain, with no safety equipment, from his shoes up past the folds of his clothes and the lines of his skin, until he lies on the summit and feels his heart beating calmly. The whole poem is one **extended metaphor**, in a single stanza of free verse.

## Romantic love

**“Winter Swans”** by Owen Sheers (2005). After a long spell of rain, a couple who have argued walk along the flooded edge of a lake, apart and silent. Two swans appear, turning together on the water; swans are said to pair for life. By the end, the couple find that their hands have joined again. The poem is in three-line stanzas with a final couplet, a pair of lines for the reunited pair.

**“Singh Song!”** by Daljit Nagra (2007). A young Sikh shopkeeper keeps slipping away from his father’s shop to be with his new bride, while the customers complain. Late at night the couple sit together in the empty shop and tease each other about how much the moon is worth. Nagra writes in a playful Punjabi-English voice that celebrates the speaker, his humour and his love.

**“Letters from Yorkshire”** by Maura Dooley. A speaker in a city receives letters from a man in Yorkshire who works outdoors: he has seen lapwings come back for the spring while he was working in his garden. She works indoors at a screen, and wonders whose life is more truly lived, yet their letters link the two lives across the distance.

Which two poems show a parent letting a child go?
@step In “Walking Away”, a father watches his son walk off across a field, and decides that a loving parent must allow him to leave.
@step In “Mother, any distance”, a son leaves home while his mother still holds the end of the tape.
@answer Both show **distance and separation** in a family. Day-Lewis writes from the parent’s side; Armitage writes from the child’s.

## Form and methods

The form of a poem often mirrors its meaning. Armitage stretches the sonnet as the son stretches the tape; Sheers ends with a couplet as the couple come together; Causley sets his last line apart, like the stream between the speaker and his parents. Several poems use **free verse**, which follows the rhythm of speech and memory.
