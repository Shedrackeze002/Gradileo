Literature from other countries lets us see the world through other eyes: a young official posted from the city to a remote village, a lawyer locked in a room for fifteen years to win a bet. Reading it well means paying attention to its setting and history, to its point of view, and to the cultural experience it shows, without assuming that one story speaks for a whole people.

In this lesson you will analyse a point of view and a cultural experience in two stories from beyond the United States, by Rabindranath Tagore of Bengal and Anton Chekhov of Russia, and read world literature in its context.

## “The Postmaster”, by Rabindranath Tagore

Tagore (1861–1941) wrote in Bengali, and in 1913 he became the first writer from outside Europe to win the Nobel Prize in Literature. “The Postmaster” opens like this [1]:

> The postmaster first took up his duties in the village of Ulapur. Though the village was a small one, there was an indigo factory near by, and the proprietor, an Englishman, had managed to get a post office established. Our postmaster belonged to Calcutta. He felt like a fish out of water in this remote village.

The details set up the story’s world: India under British rule, a colonial business beside a village, and a man from the great city who feels lost there. The postmaster comes to depend on Ratan, an orphan girl who keeps house for him and calls him *Dada*, a Bengali word for an elder brother that the translation keeps. When he leaves, he consoles himself with thoughts about life’s meetings and partings, but, as the narrator says, “Ratan had no philosophy.”

What does the contrast between the postmaster and Ratan at the end reveal?
@step He is educated and from the city, and can return to a wider world.
@step She is a poor village orphan with nowhere else to go.
@answer The same parting means something quite different to each, because of their class, age and place.

## “The Bet”, by Anton Chekhov

At a party in Russia, a rich banker and a young lawyer argue over whether the death penalty or life imprisonment is more humane. The banker bets two million that the lawyer could not bear solitary confinement for five years; the lawyer offers fifteen. Near the end of the fifteen years, the prisoner leaves a letter [2]:

> “To prove to you in action how I despise all that you live by, I renounce the two million of which I once dreamed as of paradise and which now I despise. To deprive myself of the right to the money I shall go out from here five hours before the time fixed, and so break the compact ...”

Years of reading alone have changed him so deeply that the money no longer means anything, while the banker, ruined by his own speculations, is left with “so great a contempt for himself” [2].

## Reading in context

:::tip Questions for world literature
- Where and when is the story set, and what history lies behind it?
- Whose point of view does it give, and whose does it leave out?
- What words or customs has the translator kept, and why?
:::

Some writers tell their culture’s story from within, partly to answer outsiders who described it. Chinua Achebe’s *Things Fall Apart* (1958), which is still in copyright and so is described here rather than quoted, follows an Igbo leader in Nigeria as missionaries and colonial rule arrive, told from inside the community. When you write about any of these stories, back each claim with evidence and avoid sweeping statements about a whole people.

:::summary
- World literature gives **points of view** and **cultural experiences** from beyond the United States.
- Read the **setting** and its history, such as colonial Bengal or Russia in the 1880s.
- Notice what a **translator** keeps, and whose voice a story gives.
- Support every claim with evidence, and avoid stereotypes.
:::
