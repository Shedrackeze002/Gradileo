A verb tells us when an action happens, but its **mood** tells us how the speaker presents it: as a fact, a command, a question, a possibility or a wish. Choosing the mood, and the voice, on purpose lets a writer be exact about what is real and what is only imagined.

In this lesson you will name the five moods of English verbs, form the conditional and the subjunctive, and correct sentences that shift voice or mood for no reason.

## The five moods

| Mood | What it does | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Indicative** | states a fact or an opinion | *The library opens at nine.* |
| **Imperative** | gives a command or an instruction | *Close the window before you leave.* |
| **Interrogative** | asks a question | *Did you feed the fish?* |
| **Conditional** | says what would or might happen | *If it snowed, we **would build** a snowman.* |
| **Subjunctive** | presents a wish, a demand or something not real | *I wish she **were** here.* |

## The conditional and the subjunctive

The **conditional** uses *would*, *could* or *might* with the base form of the verb: *we would miss the start*, *the plan might work*. It is useful for expressing uncertainty.

The **subjunctive** has two common forms:

1. *Were* for a wish or a condition that is not true, whatever the subject: *If I **were** taller, I could reach the shelf.* (I am not taller.)
2. The base form after a demand or a suggestion: *The coach insists that every player **be** on time.* *I suggest that she **take** the early train.*

:::example A condition that is not true
Complete the sentence: *“If I ___ the captain, I ___ choose you first.”* (You are not the captain.)
@step The condition is not true, so it takes the subjunctive: *were*.
@step The result of an imagined condition takes the conditional: *would choose*.
@answer If I were the captain, I would choose you first.
:::

## Shifts in voice and mood

Recall that in the **active voice** the subject does the action (*Zainab painted the walls*), and in the **passive voice** the subject receives it (*The walls were painted by Zainab*). Changing voice or mood halfway through a sentence for no reason confuses the reader.

| Shift | Fixed |
|---|---|
| *Zainab painted the walls, and the doors were painted by her too.* (active to passive) | *Zainab painted the walls, and she painted the doors too.* |
| *First, mix the flour, and then you should add the eggs.* (imperative to indicative) | *First, mix the flour, and then add the eggs.* |
| *If I were the captain, I will choose you.* (subjunctive with the wrong result) | *If I were the captain, I would choose you.* |

:::tip Choose the passive for a reason
The passive is not wrong. Use it when the actor is unknown or unimportant and you want to keep the focus on the action: *The window was broken during the night.*

:::summary
- The **indicative** states, the **imperative** commands, the **interrogative** asks, the **conditional** says what would happen, and the **subjunctive** presents a wish or something not real.
- Use *were* for a condition contrary to fact, and the base form after *insist that* or *suggest that*.
- Keep the same **voice** and **mood** through a sentence unless you have a reason to change.
:::
