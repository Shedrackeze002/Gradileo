A story can live in many forms. A myth becomes a painting and then a poem about the painting; a play becomes a short story, a film and a hundred different stage productions. Each new version makes choices about what to show, what to stress and what to leave out, so comparing them shows how each one interprets its source.

In this lesson you will analyse a subject in two artistic mediums, and compare and evaluate productions of a play by Shakespeare and by an American dramatist.

## One subject in two mediums

In Ovid’s myth, Icarus flies too close to the sun on wings of wax and falls into the sea while his father, Daedalus, watches in grief. The painting *Landscape with the Fall of Icarus*, attributed to Pieter Bruegel the Elder, tells it very differently: a ploughman, a shepherd and a ship fill the scene, and Icarus is just a pair of legs splashing into the water in a corner.

:::example Comparing the myth and the painting
What does the painting emphasize that the myth does not?
@step The myth centres on Icarus and his father.
@step The painting makes Icarus tiny and fills the foreground with people who carry on working.
@answer The painting emphasizes that the world goes on, barely noticing the boy’s fall.
:::

W. H. Auden’s poem “Musée des Beaux Arts” (1938), still in copyright and so described here rather than quoted, reflects on this painting and draws from it a general idea: that suffering happens while other people go about their ordinary business. Each medium has its own tools: the painter has composition, colour and scale; the poet has the sound, rhythm and order of words.

Susan Glaspell turned her play *Trifles* (1916) into a short story, “A Jury of Her Peers” (1917). The story’s narrator can tell us what the women are thinking; the play has to show it through what they say and do.

## One play in many productions

In *Trifles*, the men search a farmhouse for evidence that a wife killed her husband and find nothing, while the two women notice the “trifles” of the kitchen and quietly hide what they find. The play ends [1]:

> COUNTY ATTORNEY: (*Facetiously*) Well, Henry, at least we found out that she was not going to quilt it. She was going to—what is it you call it, ladies?
>
> MRS. HALE: (*Hand against her pocket*) We call it—knot it, Mr. Henderson.

Every production must decide how to play this moment. Spoken quietly, with a glance at Mrs. Peters, the line stresses the women’s secret bond; spoken boldly to the attorney, it becomes defiance. The stage direction lets the audience see where the evidence is hidden while the men do not, and a production decides how visible to make that gesture.

:::definition Interpreting a source text
Every **production** or **recording** of a play interprets its script through casting, delivery, staging, setting and cuts. To evaluate a production, ask whether its choices illuminate the play.
:::

Shakespeare is interpreted the same way. Laurence Olivier’s 1948 film of *Hamlet* cuts Rosencrantz, Guildenstern and Fortinbras and focuses on Hamlet’s mind, while Kenneth Branagh’s 1996 film uses nearly the full text and keeps the play’s political world.

- The same subject is treated differently in each **medium**; ask what each emphasizes or leaves out.
- Prose can enter a character’s mind; drama must show it.
- Every **production** interprets its play through delivery, staging and cuts.
- Evaluate a production by whether its choices **illuminate** the text.
