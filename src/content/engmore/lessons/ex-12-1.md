Nobody speaks the same way to a best friend, a teacher and a job interviewer, and nobody should. English comes in many varieties, from the casual chat of friends to the careful language of a formal report, and from one region’s dialect to another’s. Good writers and speakers choose the variety that suits the moment.

In this lesson you will match register to an audience and purpose, explain why writers give characters a dialect, and recognize variations from standard English and revise them for formal writing.

## Register

:::definition Register
**Register** is how formal or informal language is. We choose it to suit the audience (who will read or hear it) and the purpose (why we are writing or speaking).
:::

| Informal | Formal |
|---|---|
| *Hey, can we swing by in May?* | *I am writing to ask whether our class may visit in May.* |
| contractions and slang: *gonna*, *stuff*, *guys* | complete sentences and precise words |
| texts and chats with friends | letters, reports, essays, speeches to an audience |

:::

:::example Choosing the register
You are giving a speech to the school board. Which opening suits it: *“Hi guys, thanks for letting us chat about stuff”* or *“Good evening, members of the board. Thank you for hearing us”*?
@step The audience is a formal body, and the purpose is to persuade it.
@step A formal occasion needs formal English: a polite greeting and complete, precise sentences.
@answer Good evening, members of the board. Thank you for hearing us.
:::

## Dialect in stories and plays

:::definition Dialect
A **dialect** is a form of a language with its own words, grammar and pronunciation, shared by the people of a region or a community.
:::

Writers often let characters speak in dialect while the narrator uses standard English. It shows where a character comes from, gives each voice its own sound, and makes the speech feel real.

> “I ain’t never seen a storm like this un,” said Old Tom, squinting at the sky.

The writer has not made a mistake: Old Tom’s words tell us about him, just as the narrator’s standard English tells us that we are hearing a different voice.

## Standard English

:::definition Standard English
**Standard English** is the variety of English that most readers share and that is expected in formal writing and speech, such as essays, reports and presentations.
:::

A dialect is not wrong, but in formal writing we revise variations from standard English:

| Variation | Standard English |
|---|---|
| *We was surprised.* | *We were surprised.* |
| *I seen the film.* | *I saw the film.* |
| *There isn’t no milk.* / *There ain’t any milk.* | *There isn’t any milk.* |
| *The results was better.* | *The results were better.* |

:::summary
- **Register** is the level of formality; match it to your audience and purpose.
- A **dialect** is a variety of a language; writers use it in dialogue to show who a character is.
- **Standard English** is the shared variety for formal writing; revise variations such as *we was* and *I seen* when the situation is formal.
:::
