*Blood Brothers* is a musical play by Willy Russell, first performed as a musical in 1983. In Liverpool, a poor mother of a large family learns that she is expecting twins, and gives one of them to the wealthy woman she cleans for. The twins grow up on opposite sides of the class divide, become best friends without knowing the truth, and die on the same day. The play is still in copyright, so this lesson tells its story and discusses its key moments in our own words, without quoting it.

In this lesson you will learn who the characters are and the order of the play, read its key moments closely, and connect the play to the time Russell wrote it.

## The play in two acts

The play opens at the end, with the twins’ deaths, and then tells how it happened.

| Act | What happens |
|---|---|
| Act 1 | Mrs Johnstone gives one of her twins to Mrs Lyons, who makes her swear on the Bible to keep the secret. At seven, Mickey and Eddie meet and become blood brothers. The Johnstones move out of the city. |
| Act 2 | As teenagers the twins meet again. Eddie goes to university; Mickey marries Linda, loses his job and goes to prison after a robbery. Mickey learns that Linda has been meeting Eddie, confronts him with a gun, and both twins are shot. |

## The characters

**Mrs Johnstone** is a warm, struggling mother. **Mrs Lyons**, her employer, longs for a child and grows more and more afraid of losing Eddie. **Mickey**, raised by Mrs Johnstone, grows up poor; **Eddie**, raised by Mrs Lyons, goes to university and becomes a councillor. **Linda** grows up with both of them and marries Mickey. **Sammy**, Mickey’s older brother, leads him into crime. The **Narrator** comments on the action and reminds the audience that a price must be paid.

## Key moments

To keep the secret, Mrs Lyons makes Mrs Johnstone swear on the Bible, and later plays on her superstition by warning that twins who learn they were parted will die. At seven, Mickey and Eddie cut their hands, mix their blood and promise to be brothers for ever, not knowing that they already are.

The twins then take very different paths. Eddie goes to university, while Mickey is laid off, turns to crime and comes out of prison depressed. At the end, Mrs Johnstone tells Mickey that Eddie is his twin, and Mickey asks why he was not the one given away. He sees that he could have had Eddie’s life.

## Context and Russell’s methods

Russell grew up in Liverpool. In the early 1980s, many factories in the city and across the North closed, and unemployment rose sharply; Mickey’s loss of his job reflects what happened to many working-class families. Twins with the same nature end up with very different lives, so the play argues that **class** and money, not fate, shape who we become. The **Narrator**, like the chorus of a Greek tragedy, keeps the threat of the ending in the audience’s mind, and because the play opens with the deaths, the audience watches to see why it happens, not what happens.

:::mistake Superstition is not the real cause of the tragedy
The characters blame fate and superstition, and the Narrator talks of a price to be paid. But the play shows that the twins’ different chances in life, decided by class, are what lead to their deaths.
:::

:::summary
- **Class**: the twins’ lives divide because one is brought up rich and one poor.
- **Fate and superstition**: the oath, the Narrator and the shoes on the table create a sense of doom.
- **Friendship**: the blood brothers choose each other, unaware that they are twins.
- **Nature and nurture**: the same nature, different upbringings, different lives.
:::
