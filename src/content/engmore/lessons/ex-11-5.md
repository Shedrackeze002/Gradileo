People argue about English. Can you split an infinitive? End a sentence with a preposition? Use *they* for one person? These arguments matter because the rules of usage are not laws of nature: they are conventions that careful writers agree on, and conventions change.

In this lesson you will treat usage as convention, recognize the rules that usage guides call myths, settle a disputed point with a reference, and follow the conventions for hyphens.

## Usage is convention

:::definition Usage
**Usage** is the way words and constructions are customarily used. It rests on what careful speakers and writers agree on, and that agreement shifts over time, which is why dictionaries and usage guides are revised.
:::

Some “rules” that many people learned are rejected by modern usage guides:

| The old rule | What most modern guides say |
|---|---|
| Never end a sentence with a preposition. | A myth: *What are you looking at?* is natural; *At what are you looking?* is stiff. |
| Never split an infinitive (*to boldly go*). | Acceptable, especially when it reads better; the rule was borrowed from Latin, where an infinitive is one word. |
| Never begin a sentence with *and* or *but*. | Acceptable, and common in good writing. |

## Points still in dispute

Other questions are genuinely disputed, and the right answer depends on how formal the writing is and which style it follows.

- **Fewer and less.** Formal writing keeps *fewer* for things you count (*fewer cars*) and *less* for amounts (*less noise*), although *less* with count nouns is common in speech.
- **Singular *they*.** It has been used for centuries, and many guides accept it, especially for a person whose gender is unknown or who uses *they*; some formal styles still prefer to reword.
- **Hopefully.** Its use for “it is to be hoped that” (*Hopefully, it will stop raining*) was long criticized and is now widely accepted.
- ***Data is* or *data are*.** Both are used; scientific writing often keeps *data* plural.

:::tip Consult a usage guide
A **usage guide**, such as a dictionary of English usage, explains disputed points, gives their history and records what careful writers actually do. When the writing must follow a style guide (a school’s, a journal’s or a newspaper’s), follow it.
:::

## Hyphens

A hyphen (-) joins words. It is shorter than a dash and has no spaces around it.

| Convention | Examples |
|---|---|
| Two words acting as one adjective **before** a noun | *a well-known writer*, *a two-hour workshop* |
| No hyphen when the compound comes **after** the noun | *The writer is well known.* |
| No hyphen after an adverb ending in *-ly* | *a highly rated film* |
| Compound numbers from 21 to 99 | *thirty-seven*, *ninety-nine* |
| The prefixes *self-*, *ex-* and *all-* | *self-taught*, *ex-champion*, *all-important* |
| To prevent a misreading | *re-cover* the sofa (cover again), not *recover* |
| A suspended hyphen shares the last word | *first- and second-year students* |

:::example Hyphen or not?
Which is right: *“a two hour workshop”* or *“a two-hour workshop”*? And *“The workshop lasted two hours”*?
@step *Two hour* comes before the noun *workshop* and works as one adjective, so it takes a hyphen.
@step In the second sentence, *two hours* is not describing a noun that follows, so no hyphen is needed.
@answer a two-hour workshop; The workshop lasted two hours.
:::

:::note Hyphens change too
New compounds often start as two words, gain a hyphen and end as one word: *e mail*, *e-mail*, *email*. Guides disagree about where a word has got to, so choose one guide and be consistent.

:::summary
- **Usage** is convention, and conventions change; some old rules are myths.
- Formal writing keeps distinctions such as *fewer* and *less*; for a disputed point, consult a **usage guide** or follow your style guide.
- Hyphenate compound adjectives before a noun, compound numbers, and *self-*, *ex-* and *all-*; not after an *-ly* adverb.
- Be consistent: follow one guide.
:::
