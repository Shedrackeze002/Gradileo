No two people tell the same event in quite the same way. A person who lived through it remembers how it felt; a reporter collects the facts; a historian looks for causes. Reading several accounts together, and noticing where they agree and disagree, gives us a fuller and fairer picture.

In this lesson you will tell firsthand from secondhand accounts, compare what each one focuses on, combine information from several texts, and weigh texts that disagree.

## Firsthand and secondhand accounts

:::definition Firsthand and secondhand
A **firsthand** account is by someone who took part in or saw the event, such as a diary or a letter. A **secondhand** account is by someone who was not there, such as a newspaper report or a history book.
:::

Here are two accounts of one flood, written for this lesson.

**Account A**, from the diary of Maria, aged twelve:

> The water came up the steps faster than I could believe. Dad carried Grandma, and I carried the cat, who scratched me the whole way to the hill. I could hear our neighbour shouting for her dog. I have never been so frightened.

**Account B**, from the town newspaper the next morning:

> The River Hale burst its banks at 4 a.m. yesterday after three days of heavy rain. Forty homes on Mill Street were flooded. No one was hurt. The council says the flood wall, built in 1950, was two feet too low.

:::example Comparing the two accounts
What does each account focus on, and why?
@step Maria was there, so her diary gives feelings and personal details: her fear, the cat, the neighbour’s shouts.
@step The newspaper reports for the whole town, so it gives the time, the number of homes, and the cause.
@answer Account A focuses on what the flood felt like, and Account B on the facts, the scale and the cause, because each writer had a different point of view and purpose.
:::

## When texts disagree

Texts on the same topic do not always agree. Sometimes the authors **stress different evidence**: two articles can quote one survey and pick different numbers to suit their views. Sometimes they **interpret the same facts differently**, as when two historians explain one battle in two ways. And sometimes they simply give **conflicting information**.

:::tip Weighing texts that conflict
1. Find exactly where the texts disagree.
2. Check who wrote each one, when, and where their evidence comes from.
3. Prefer recent, tested evidence over old opinion, and look for a third source if you can.
:::

:::summary
- A **firsthand** account comes from someone who was there; a **secondhand** account reports it.
- Each writer’s **point of view** and **purpose** shape what they include.
- Combine several accounts to get a fuller picture.
- When texts **disagree**, find the disagreement and weigh each text’s evidence and source.
:::
