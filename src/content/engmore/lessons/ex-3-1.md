A letter from 1847 can feel like a message in a foreign language. The sentences run on for lines, the words are old-fashioned, and the writer takes for granted a world without cars, phones or clean rivers. But the writer is still a person telling you what they saw and what they thought of it, and with a few habits you can read them as clearly as a writer today.

In this lesson you will work out the meaning of older words and long sentences from their context, find information, infer the writer’s attitude, and use the text as evidence about its time.

## Reading older words in context

:::definition Context
The **context** of a word is the sentence and passage around it. When a word is unfamiliar, the context usually narrows down what it can mean.
:::

Nineteenth-century writers use some words that are rare today, and some everyday words in older senses.

| Word | In the text | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| *whither* | “the inn at Derby, whither the railway carried us” | to which place |
| *seldom* | “will seldom acquire the habit of industry” | rarely |
| *industry* | “the habit of industry” | hard work |
| *hands* | “the hands were perfectly contented” | workers |
| *obliged* | “I was obliged to cover my mouth” | forced |

:::example Working out a word
*“I confess I was much alarmed at first by the shrieking of the engine and the prodigious speed, for the hedges flew past the window so swiftly that I could not fix my eye upon a single leaf.”* What does *prodigious* mean?
@step The writer is alarmed by the speed.
@step The hedges fly past so fast she cannot see a single leaf.
@answer Enormous: the speed is very great.
:::

## Breaking down long sentences

Long sentences are built from shorter ideas joined by words such as *for* (meaning “because”), *yet*, *however*, *whither* and *which*, and by semicolons. Find the joins, and put each part into your own words.

:::example A long sentence
*“For years the poorer districts upon the banks have endured this nuisance without remedy; it is a curious circumstance that the evil should only now be thought worthy of attention, when it has reached the windows of Parliament itself.”*
@step Before the semicolon: the poor people by the river have put up with the smell for years, with no cure.
@step After it: only now is anyone paying attention, now that it has reached Parliament.
@answer The poor suffered for years, but only now that MPs suffer is anything being done.
:::

## Attitude and tone

:::definition Attitude
A writer’s **attitude** is what they think and feel about their subject: approving, critical, sympathetic, alarmed, amused. It shows in the words they choose.
:::

Nineteenth-century writers are often polite on the surface and sharp underneath. Calling something “a curious circumstance” sounds mild, but it can be **irony**: the writer means it is shameful.

:::mistake Polite is not the same as approving
A formal, courteous style can carry strong criticism. Read what the writer says happened, and ask what they want the reader to think of it.
:::

## Reading the text as evidence about its time

A nineteenth-century text shows what its writer took for granted. A visitor who reports girls of fifteen working from six in the morning until seven at night tells us that such hours were normal enough to be shown to visitors. An advice book written only about boys tells us how differently boys and girls were expected to live.

:::example What the text takes for granted
A governess in 1847 writes that she was alarmed by the speed of the train, but expects that “in a few years nobody will think it remarkable at all”. What does this suggest about the time?
@step She is frightened by something a modern reader takes for granted.
@step She predicts that people will get used to it.
@answer Railways were still new, and many people had never travelled so fast.
:::

:::summary
- Use the **context** to work out older words: read the whole sentence and try each meaning in place of the word.
- Break a long sentence at its joins (*for*, *yet*, *however*, semicolons) and put each part into your own words.
- A writer’s **attitude** shows in their word choices; polite language can carry sharp criticism, sometimes through irony.
- Read the text as **evidence about its time**: notice what the writer takes for granted.
:::
