A coffee shop manager wants to know which drinks sell best. A coach wants to know which drills the team likes. They both collect **data** and put it in a chart or graph, so the answer is easy to see.

## Tally charts

:::definition Data and tally
**Data** is information you collect, such as counts or measurements. A **tally** is a quick way to count. You make one mark for each person or thing. Every fifth mark goes across the four marks before it, so you can count by fives.
:::

On paper, a tally for $7$ looks like the picture above. In the charts on this site, each group of $5$ is shown as `|||||`, with a space between groups.

:::example Counting a tally
A warehouse asked its staff how they get to work. How many staff answered?
@step Car: two groups of $5$ and $2$ more: $5 + 5 + 2 = 12$
@step Bus: one group of $5$ and $3$ more: $5 + 3 = 8$
@step Bike: $3$
@step Add the rows: $12 + 8 + 3 = 23$
@answer $23$ staff answered.
:::

## Bar graphs

:::definition Bar graph
A **bar graph** uses bars to compare groups. The height of each bar shows how many. The numbers on the side are the **scale**.
:::

:::figure
{"type":"barChart","categories":["Mon","Tue","Wed","Thu","Fri"],"values":[25,40,35,50,65],"valueLabel":"Lunches sold","categoryLabel":"Day","max":70,"step":10}
:::

This bar graph shows the lunches a food truck sold each day. Look at Wednesday. The bar stops **halfway** between $30$ and $40$, so Wednesday's value is $35$.

:::example How many more?
How many more lunches did the food truck sell on Friday than on Tuesday?
@step Friday's bar reaches $65$. It is halfway between $60$ and $70$.
@step Tuesday's bar reaches $40$.
@step Subtract: $65 - 40 = 25$
@answer The truck sold $25$ more lunches on Friday.
:::

:::mistake Not checking the scale
The grid lines do not always go up by $1$. On this graph they go up by $10$. Always read the numbers on the side before you read a bar.
:::

## Circle graphs

:::definition Circle graph
A **circle graph** (or pie chart) shows how one whole is split into parts. The whole circle is $100\%$. Bigger slices are bigger parts.
:::

:::figure
{"type":"circleGraph","slices":[{"label":"Pizza","value":35},{"label":"Burgers","value":25},{"label":"Tacos","value":20},{"label":"Salad","value":10},{"label":"Soup","value":10}],"show":"percent"}
:::

:::example Reading percents
Students at a high school voted for their favourite lunch. What percent did **not** choose pizza?
@step The whole circle is $100\%$. Pizza is $35\%$.
@step $100\% - 35\% = 65\%$
@answer $65\%$ of the students did not choose pizza.
:::

:::tip Check the total
All the parts of a circle graph add to $100\%$: $35 + 25 + 20 + 10 + 10 = 100$. If they do not, something is wrong.
:::

## Fair surveys and the best graph

:::definition Survey, population and sample
A **survey** asks people questions to collect data. The **population** is the whole group you want to learn about. A **sample** is the part of the population you actually ask. A survey is **biased** if it pushes the results one way.
:::

A fair survey question does not push people toward an answer. "Which snack would you like: fruit, chips or cookies?" is fair. "Don't you agree chips are the best?" is biased.

A fair sample gives everyone in the population the same chance to be picked. Asking people at a hockey game if the town needs a new arena is **not** fair. They probably like hockey more than most people.

:::mistake Asking only people you know
Your friends often think like you. Pick people **at random** from the whole group instead.
:::

| Graph | Best for | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Bar graph | comparing groups | votes for each pizza topping |
| Line graph | change over time | the temperature every hour |
| Circle graph | parts of one whole | how a paycheque is spent |

:::example Choosing a graph
Maya weighs her puppy every week for $10$ weeks. Which graph should she use?
@step The data is the same thing measured again and again over time.
@answer A **line graph**, because it shows how something changes over time.
:::

:::summary
- A tally counts in groups of $5$. Count by fives, then add the extra marks.
- On a bar graph, check the scale first. A bar halfway between two lines is halfway between the two numbers.
- A circle graph shows parts of one whole. The parts add to $100\%$.
- A fair survey uses fair questions and a sample picked at random.
- Bar graph: compare groups. Line graph: change over time. Circle graph: parts of a whole.
:::
