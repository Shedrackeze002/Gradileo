Your first full-time pay cheque can feel huge, until rent, a phone bill and groceries each take their share. A **budget** is a plan that tells your money where to go before it is gone. It takes nothing more than adding, subtracting and finding percents.

## Gross pay, deductions and net pay

Before you plan your spending, you need to know how much money you actually get. Your employer takes some money off every pay cheque before it reaches you.

:::definition Gross pay, deductions and net pay
**Gross pay** is everything you earn before anything is taken off. **Deductions** are amounts taken off, such as income tax, Canada Pension Plan (CPP) and Employment Insurance (EI) contributions. **Net pay** (take-home pay) is what is left:
$$\text{net pay} = \text{gross pay} - \text{deductions}$$
:::

The real deduction rates change every year and depend on how much you earn, so in this course every question tells you the deductions to use.

:::example Net pay for a month
Jordan works at a warehouse for \$18.40 an hour and worked 150 hours in March. For this example, deductions take 19% of gross pay. Find Jordan’s net pay for March.
@step Gross pay: $18.40 \times 150 = 2{,}760.00$
@step Deductions: $19\% = 0.19$, so $2{,}760.00 \times 0.19 = 524.40$
@step Net pay: $2{,}760.00 - 524.40 = 2{,}235.60$
@answer Jordan’s net pay for March is \$2,235.60.
:::

:::mistake Budgeting with gross pay
Plan with **net** pay, the money that actually lands in your account. If Jordan planned to spend \$2,760.00, the budget would be short by \$524.40 every month.
:::

## Fixed and variable expenses

Every budget lists **expenses**: the things you spend money on each month. It helps to sort them into two kinds.

:::definition Fixed and variable expenses
A **fixed expense** costs the same amount every month, such as rent, a phone plan, a bus pass or a truck payment. A **variable expense** changes from month to month, depending on how much you buy or use, such as groceries, gas, utilities, eating out and entertainment.
:::

| Fixed (same each month) | Variable (changes each month) |
|---|---|
| Rent | Groceries |
| Phone plan | Gas for a vehicle |
| Vehicle insurance | Utilities |
| Gym membership | Eating out and entertainment |

Fixed expenses are easy to plan for. For a variable expense, look at what you spent over the last few months and use the average.

:::tip Do not forget yearly costs
Some bills come once a year, such as vehicle registration or a hunting licence. Divide a yearly cost by 12 and set that much aside every month, so the bill does not catch you short.
:::

## Balancing a budget: surplus or deficit

To balance a budget, add up all the expenses and compare the total with your net income.

:::definition Surplus and deficit
If net income is **more** than total expenses, the difference is a **surplus**: money you can save. If total expenses are **more** than net income, the difference is a **deficit**: you are spending money you do not have.
:::

Amara’s net income is \$2,150.00 a month. Is there a surplus or a deficit, and how much?
@step Add the expenses: $725 + 68.40 + 22 + 55 + 105 + 40 + 380 + 90 + 75 + 60 + 35 = 1{,}655.40$
@step Compare: the income is bigger, so there is a surplus.

## Percent of income and circle graphs

A percent shows how big each expense is compared with the whole income:
$$\text{percent of income} = \dfrac{\text{expense}}{\text{net income}} \times 100$$

Amara’s rent is $725 \div 2{,}150 \times 100 \approx 33.7\%$ of her net income. Rent, utilities and tenant insurance together are $815.40 \div 2{,}150 \times 100 \approx 37.9\%$.

Many budgeting guides suggest a target percent for each category, such as keeping total housing costs to about a third of net income. Targets differ, so a question will always tell you the guideline to use. A circle graph shows a planned budget at a glance.

:::example From a percent to dollars
Mateo’s net income is \$2,400 a month. How much does his plan set aside for food?
@step The graph shows food is $20\%$ of net income, and $20\% = 0.20$.
@step $0.20 \times 2{,}400 = 480.00$
@answer Mateo’s plan sets aside \$480.00 a month for food.

## Adjusting a budget to reach a goal

A savings goal turns a budget into a plan. Find how much you need each month, then compare it with your surplus. If the surplus is too small, cut spending (usually a variable expense) or earn more.

:::example Saving for a damage deposit
It is the start of March. Mateo wants \$1,800 for a damage deposit and first month’s rent on an apartment by the start of September. He already has \$300 saved, and his budget has a surplus of \$180 a month. How much must he save each month, and how much must he cut?
@step Months of saving: March, April, May, June, July and August, which is 6 months.
@step Still needed: $1{,}800 - 300 = 1{,}500$
@step Each month: $1{,}500 \div 6 = 250$
@step Cut: $250 - 180 = 70$
@answer Mateo must save \$250.00 a month, so he needs to spend \$70.00 less each month, for example by cutting eating out from \$120 to \$70 and entertainment by \$20.
:::

:::example How many months?
Kiona can save \$275 a month toward \$2,000 for a set of winter tires and a remote starter. How many months will it take?
@step $2{,}000 \div 275 \approx 7.27$
@step After 7 months Kiona has only $7 \times 275 = 1{,}925$, which is not enough.
@answer It will take 8 months.
:::

:::mistake Rounding the months down
When you find the number of months, always round **up**. Rounding $7.27$ down to 7 months leaves Kiona \$75 short.
:::

:::summary
- Net pay = gross pay − deductions. Budget with net pay.
- Fixed expenses stay the same each month; variable expenses change.
- Surplus: income is more than expenses. Deficit: expenses are more than income.
- Percent of income = expense ÷ net income × 100; an amount = percent (as a decimal) × income.
- For a savings goal: (goal − money already saved) ÷ months = savings each month. Cut spending by the gap between that and your surplus.
- When counting months to reach a goal, round up.
:::
