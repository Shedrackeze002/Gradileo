A farm supply store tracks its seed sales, a towing company tracks its calls and a sporting goods store tracks the ages of its customers. Graphs turn these numbers into pictures you can read at a glance. In this lesson you will read bar graphs, circle graphs and histograms, and turn what you read into amounts, percents and angles.

## Bar graphs

:::definition Bar graph
A **bar graph** compares amounts in separate categories. Each category gets its own bar, and the height of the bar is read from the scale on the vertical axis.
:::

:::figure
{"type":"barChart","categories":["Cars","Pickups","SUVs","Vans","Semis"],"values":[60,85,70,25,15],"valueLabel":"Vehicles serviced","categoryLabel":"Type of vehicle","max":100,"step":10}
:::

The bar graph shows the vehicles an auto shop serviced last month. The grid lines go up by $10$, so a bar that stops halfway between two lines is halfway between their numbers.

:::example Reading two bars
How many more pickups than vans did the shop service?
@step The Pickups bar stops halfway between $80$ and $90$, so it shows $85$.
@step The Vans bar stops halfway between $20$ and $30$, so it shows $25$.
@step $85 - 25 = 60$
@answer The shop serviced $60$ more pickups than vans.
:::

:::mistake Misreading the scale
Check how much each grid line is worth before you read a bar. Here each line is worth $10$, not $1$, so the Vans bar is $25$, not $2.5$.
:::

## Circle graphs

:::definition Circle graph
A **circle graph** shows how one whole is split into parts. Each part is a **sector** of the circle, and the percents of all the sectors add to $100\%$.
:::

:::figure
{"type":"circleGraph","slices":[{"label":"Drive alone","value":45},{"label":"Carpool","value":25},{"label":"Bus","value":15},{"label":"Bike","value":10},{"label":"Walk","value":5}],"show":"percent"}
:::

A gas plant outside the city asked its $400$ workers how they get to work. The circle graph shows the results.

:::example From a percent to an amount
How many workers carpool?
@step Carpool is $25\%$ of the $400$ workers.
@step $25\% = 0.25$, and $0.25 \times 400 = 100$
@answer $100$ workers carpool.
:::

To draw a circle graph, split the $360^\circ$ around the centre. Each sector gets the same percent of $360^\circ$ as its category has of the whole.

:::example The angle of a sector
What is the angle of the Bus sector?
@step Bus is $15\%$ of the circle.
@step $0.15 \times 360 = 54$
@answer The Bus sector has an angle of $54^\circ$.
:::

:::check m203-graph-read 2
:::

When you start from counts, find the percent or the angle by comparing the part with the total:
$$\text{percent} = \dfrac{\text{part}}{\text{total}} \times 100 \qquad \text{angle} = \dfrac{\text{part}}{\text{total}} \times 360^\circ$$

:::example From a count to a percent
A towing company answered $80$ calls, and $28$ were for dead batteries. What percent of the calls were for dead batteries?
@step $\dfrac{28}{80} \times 100 = 0.35 \times 100 = 35$
@answer $35\%$ of the calls were for dead batteries. Its sector would be $0.35 \times 360 = 126^\circ$.
:::

:::check m203-graph-read 3
:::

## Histograms

:::definition Histogram
A **histogram** is a bar graph for numerical data grouped into equal **intervals**, such as ages $10$–$19$, $20$–$29$ and $30$–$39$. The height of each bar is the **frequency**: how many values fall in that interval.
:::

For whole-number data, the interval $30$–$39$ includes $30$, $39$ and every whole number between them. The intervals all have the same width, they do not overlap, and there are **no gaps** between them: $39$ is followed right away by $40$. That is why the bars of a hand-drawn histogram touch each other. (The graphs in this course leave a thin space between bars, but the intervals still have no gaps.)

:::figure
{"type":"barChart","categories":["10–19","20–29","30–39","40–49","50–59","60–69"],"values":[4,8,12,10,6,2],"valueLabel":"Number of customers","categoryLabel":"Age (years)","max":14,"step":2}
:::

:::example Reading a histogram
The histogram shows the ages of the customers at a sporting goods store one Saturday morning. How many customers were $40$ or older?
@step The intervals $40$–$49$, $50$–$59$ and $60$–$69$ hold ages of $40$ or more.
@step Their bars are $10$, $6$ and $2$.
@step $10 + 6 + 2 = 18$
@answer $18$ customers were $40$ or older.
:::

:::example Sorting data into intervals
Here are the ages of $16$ customers: $23$, $35$, $41$, $30$, $19$, $38$, $52$, $29$, $33$, $47$, $39$, $61$, $30$, $27$, $44$, $36$. What is the frequency of the $30$–$39$ interval?
@step Go through the list and pick out every age from $30$ to $39$.
@step In order: $30,\ 30,\ 33,\ 35,\ 36,\ 38,\ 39$
@answer The frequency of $30$–$39$ is $7$.
:::

:::mistake Values on a boundary
An age of $30$ belongs in $30$–$39$, not in $20$–$29$. Each interval starts at its first number, so a value that equals a lower boundary goes in the interval that starts with it.
:::

## Using a histogram

A histogram also answers questions about parts of the data. Add the bars you need, and compare with the total.

:::example A percent from a histogram
Use the histogram of customer ages above. What percent of the customers were younger than $30$, to the nearest tenth of a percent?
@step Total: $4 + 8 + 12 + 10 + 6 + 2 = 42$ customers.
@step Younger than $30$: $4 + 8 = 12$
@step $\dfrac{12}{42} \times 100 \approx 28.571$
@answer About $28.6\%$ of the customers were younger than $30$.
:::

:::tip The median interval
With $42$ values, the median is halfway between value $21$ and value $22$ in order. Add the bars from the left: $4$, then $4 + 8 = 12$, then $12 + 12 = 24$. Values $13$ to $24$ are in $30$–$39$, so the median is in that interval.
:::

:::check m203-histogram 3
:::

:::summary
- A bar graph compares separate categories. Check the scale before you read a bar.
- A circle graph shows parts of one whole. Amount $=$ percent $\times$ total, and sector angle $=$ percent $\times\ 360^\circ$.
- From counts: percent $= \dfrac{\text{part}}{\text{total}} \times 100$ and angle $= \dfrac{\text{part}}{\text{total}} \times 360^\circ$.
- A histogram groups numerical data into equal intervals with no gaps. Each bar's height is a frequency.
- A value on a lower boundary goes in the interval that starts with it. Add bars to answer "how many" or "what percent" questions.
:::
