A rancher watches the water in a dugout drop through a dry summer, and a site supervisor watches the diesel in a generator go down hour by hour. A line graph shows these changes at a glance, and lets you predict what comes next. In this lesson you will also learn to pick the right graph, and to spot a graph that tells a misleading story.

## Reading a line graph

:::definition Line graph
A **line graph** shows how one quantity changes over time. Time goes along the bottom axis. Each point is one measurement, and the line joins the points in order.
:::

:::definition Trend
The **trend** is the general direction of the line: going up, going down, staying about the same, or changing direction.
:::

The line graph shows the temperature in Riverton on a summer day. The times use the 24-hour clock, and the grid lines go up by $4^\circ\text{C}$.

:::example Reading and describing
What was the temperature at 10:00, and what is the trend?
@step Find 10 on the bottom axis, go straight up to the point, then straight across to the scale: the point is on the $20$ line.
@step From 6:00 to 14:00 the line goes up, to a high of $28^\circ\text{C}$. After 14:00 it goes down.
@answer At 10:00 it was $20^\circ\text{C}$. The temperature went up until 14:00, then went down.
:::

## Rate of change on a line graph

The steeper the line, the faster the quantity is changing. The **rate of change** between two points is the change in the value divided by the change in time:
$$\text{rate of change} = \dfrac{\text{change in value}}{\text{change in time}}$$

:::example How fast did it warm up?
At what average rate did the temperature rise from 8:00 to 14:00?
@step At 8:00: $16^\circ\text{C}$. At 14:00: $28^\circ\text{C}$.
@step Change in temperature: $28 - 16 = 12^\circ\text{C}$. Change in time: $14 - 8 = 6$ hours.
@step $12 \div 6 = 2$
@answer The temperature rose at an average of $2^\circ\text{C}$ per hour.
:::

:::mistake Counting points instead of time
From 8:00 to 14:00 there are $3$ steps between points, but $6$ hours. Divide by the **time**, read from the bottom axis, not by the number of steps.
:::

:::

## Predicting from a steady trend

When the points fall on a straight line, the quantity changes by the same amount each hour (or week, or month). If the trend continues, you can extend the pattern to predict.

:::figure
{"type":"plane","grid":{"xMin":0,"xMax":5,"yMin":0,"yMax":260,"xStep":1,"yStep":20,"xLabel":"Time (h)","yLabel":"Diesel in tank (L)"},"polylines":[{"points":[[0,240],[1,220],[2,200],[3,180],[4,160],[5,140]]}],"points":[{"x":0,"y":240},{"x":1,"y":220},{"x":2,"y":200},{"x":3,"y":180},{"x":4,"y":160},{"x":5,"y":140}]}
:::

:::example Diesel for a generator
A generator at a remote well site burns diesel at a steady rate. The graph shows the diesel left in its tank. If the trend continues, how much will be left after $9$ hours, and when will the tank be empty?
@step Rate: $240\ \text{L}$ at the start and $220\ \text{L}$ after $1$ hour, so it uses $20\ \text{L}$ each hour.
@step After $9$ hours: $240 - 9 \times 20 = 240 - 180 = 60$
@step Empty: $240 \div 20 = 12$
@answer About $60\ \text{L}$ will be left after $9$ hours, and the tank will be empty after $12$ hours.
:::

:::tip Predictions are only as good as the trend
Extending a line assumes nothing changes. A generator that is turned down, or a calf that stops gaining weight, breaks the pattern. Predictions close to the data are more reliable than predictions far beyond it.
:::

## Choosing the best graph

Each type of graph suits a different kind of data.

| Graph | Best for | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Bar graph | comparing separate categories | trucks of each brand in a fleet |
| Circle graph | parts of one whole (percents add to $100\%$) | how a monthly budget is spent |
| Line graph | change over time | a calf's mass each week |
| Histogram | numerical data in equal intervals | ages of customers: $10$–$19$, $20$–$29$, ... |

:::example Which graph?
Harpreet recorded how many minutes each of $150$ workers drives to work, and grouped the times as $0$–$9$, $10$–$19$, $20$–$29$ and so on. Which graph is best?
@step The data are numbers (minutes), not categories, and they are grouped into equal intervals.
@step A histogram shows how many values fall in each interval.
@answer A histogram is best.
:::

## Misleading graphs

A graph can show true numbers and still give the wrong idea. Two common tricks:
- the vertical axis **does not start at $0$**, so small differences look huge
- the times on the horizontal axis are **not evenly spaced**, but the bars or points are

:::figure
{"type":"plane","grid":{"xMin":2020.4,"xMax":2024.6,"yMin":90,"yMax":110,"xStep":1,"yStep":2,"xLabel":"Year","yLabel":"Pickups sold"},"polylines":[{"points":[[2020.7,90],[2020.7,94],[2021.3,94],[2021.3,90]]},{"points":[[2021.7,90],[2021.7,96],[2022.3,96],[2022.3,90]]},{"points":[[2022.7,90],[2022.7,100],[2023.3,100],[2023.3,90]]},{"points":[[2023.7,90],[2023.7,106],[2024.3,106],[2024.3,90]]}]}
:::

:::example A cut-off axis
A truck dealership made this graph of the pickups it sold. The 2024 bar looks $4$ times as tall as the 2021 bar. What really happened?
@step The axis starts at $90$, not $0$. The bars are cut off at the bottom.
@step 2021: $94$ pickups. 2024: $106$ pickups. Increase: $106 - 94 = 12$
@step Percent increase: $\dfrac{12}{94} \times 100 \approx 12.8\%$
@answer Sales went up by $12$ pickups, about $13\%$, not to $4$ times as many.
:::

:::figure
{"type":"barChart","categories":["2000","2010","2020","2022","2024"],"values":[2400,2900,3400,3500,3600],"valueLabel":"Population","categoryLabel":"Year"}
:::

:::example Uneven years
A town council made this bar graph of the town's population. Growth seems to slow down after 2020. Is that true?
@step 2000 to 2010: up $2900 - 2400 = 500$ in $10$ years, or $50$ people a year.
@step 2020 to 2022: up $3500 - 3400 = 100$ in $2$ years, or $50$ people a year.
@answer No. The town grew by $50$ people every year. The gaps of $10$ years and $2$ years are drawn the same width, which makes the growth look slower.
:::

:::summary
- A line graph shows change over time; the trend is its general direction.
- Rate of change $= \dfrac{\text{change in value}}{\text{change in time}}$. Read the time from the axis.
- For a steady trend, extend the pattern to predict, and be careful far beyond the data.
- Bar graph: categories. Circle graph: parts of a whole. Line graph: change over time. Histogram: numerical data in equal intervals.
- Watch for misleading graphs: an axis that does not start at $0$, or unevenly spaced times.
:::
