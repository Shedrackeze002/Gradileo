An argument is more than an opinion said loudly. A strong one is built from parts that fit together, and it takes the other side seriously. In this lesson you will take an argument apart to find its **claim**, **reasons**, **evidence** and **warrant**, and you will see how a writer answers a **counterargument**.

## The parts of an argument

| Part | What it is | Question it answers |
|---|---|---|
| **Claim** | the main position the writer argues for | What do you want me to believe? |
| **Reason** | a statement that supports the claim | Why? |
| **Evidence** | facts, statistics, examples or expert views | How do you know? |
| **Warrant** | the link that explains why the evidence supports the claim | Why does that evidence prove it? |

Read this short argument written for this lesson.

> Schools should ban phones during lessons. In a trial at one school, test scores rose by 8% in the year after phones were banned. Phones distract students, and a student who checks a message loses focus for several minutes afterwards. Some argue that phones help in emergencies; however, students could still use them at break and lunch, and the school office can always reach parents.

:::example Finding the parts
@step **Claim**: “Schools should ban phones during lessons.”
@step **Reason**: phones distract students.
@step **Evidence**: test scores rose by 8% after the ban, and a student loses focus for several minutes after checking a message.
@step **Warrant** (not written down, but assumed): if scores rose after the ban, the phones must have been hurting learning.
@answer The warrant is the hidden step that connects the 8% rise to the claim.
:::

Testing the warrant is how you find an argument’s weak spots. Here, the evidence comes from a single school, which may not be typical, so the result may not apply everywhere. Something else might also have changed that year. A stronger argument would use several studies.

:::mistake Evidence is not the claim
“Test scores rose by 8%” is evidence, not the claim. The claim is what the writer wants you to believe or do. Ask: what is all this evidence *for*?
:::

## Counterarguments and rebuttals

:::definition Counterargument, concession and rebuttal
A **counterargument** is an argument against the writer’s claim. A **concession** admits that part of the other side has a point. A **rebuttal** shows why the counterargument is weak or does not change the conclusion.
:::

In the passage, the counterargument is “Some argue that phones help in emergencies.” The writer rebuts it by showing that emergencies can still be handled without phones in lessons: students can use them at break and lunch, and the office can reach parents.

Writers use signal phrases to move through these steps:

- counterargument: *Some people argue…*, *Critics claim…*, *It might be said that…*
- concession: *It is true that…*, *Admittedly…*
- rebuttal: *However…*, *Even so…*, *But this overlooks…*

:::example Concede, then rebut
@step Counterargument: uniforms cost families money.
@step Concession: “It is true that uniforms cost money…”
@step Rebuttal: “…but they are cheaper than buying many outfits.”
@answer Admitting the fair point first makes the writer seem reasonable, so the rebuttal is more convincing.
:::

Answering the other side makes an argument stronger, not weaker. It shows the writer is fair and has thought about objections, and it leaves the reader with fewer doubts.

:::summary
- An argument has a **claim**, **reasons**, **evidence** and a **warrant** that links the evidence to the claim.
- Test the warrant to find weak spots, such as evidence from only one case.
- A **counterargument** opposes the claim; a **concession** admits a fair point; a **rebuttal** answers it.
:::
