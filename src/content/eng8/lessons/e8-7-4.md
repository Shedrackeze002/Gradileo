Research makes writing stronger, but only if you use it honestly and well. In this lesson you will learn to bring a source into your writing by **quoting** and **paraphrasing**, and to **cite** it so that readers know where every borrowed idea came from.

## Quoting and paraphrasing

| Method | What it is | When to use it |
|---|---|---|
| **Direct quotation** | the source’s exact words, in quotation marks | when the exact words are powerful or important |
| **Paraphrase** | the source’s idea in your own words and sentence structure | most of the time, to keep your own voice |

:::example A good paraphrase
@step Original: “Regular exercise significantly reduces the risk of heart disease.”
@step Copying it word for word without quotation marks is not a paraphrase.
@step Changing only one or two words is not enough either.
@answer A good paraphrase: “Exercising often can greatly lower a person’s chance of heart problems.” It keeps the meaning but uses new words and a new structure.
:::

Introduce a source with a **signal phrase** so readers know where the information comes from:

> According to a 2024 survey by the town council, most residents want more parks.

Other signal phrases: *As Dr. Okafor explains…*, *A report by the Riverbend Water Trust found that…*, *In her book on sleep, Ruth Adeyemi argues that…* (The sources named in this lesson are invented examples.)

After quoting or paraphrasing, always explain how the evidence supports your point. Research evidence, like literary evidence, needs explanation.

:::definition Plagiarism
**Plagiarism** is presenting someone else’s words or ideas as your own. A paraphrase still needs a citation, because the idea belongs to the source.
:::

## Citing sources

Citations do two jobs: they credit the original author, and they let readers check the information. A citation has two parts.

1. An **in-text citation**, in brackets after the borrowed idea, usually giving the author’s surname and the year:

> Teenagers need about nine hours of sleep (Okafor, 2023).

2. A **bibliography** (or **works cited** list): a list of all the sources used, at the end of the writing, in **alphabetical order by the author’s surname**.

A website entry usually needs the author or organisation, the title of the page, the site name, the date and the web address, so a reader can find the same page again.

| Source | Example entry (invented for this lesson) |
|---|---|
| Book | Okafor, Grace. *Why Teenagers Sleep Late*. Hilltop Books, 2023. |
| Website | Riverbend Water Trust. “Saving Water at Home.” *Riverbend Water Trust*, 12 May 2024, www.riverbend.example. |

Different subjects and schools use different citation styles, with small differences in order and punctuation. Follow the style your teacher asks for, and use it consistently.

:::mistake Paraphrases need citations too
Changing the words does not make an idea yours. If a book gave you the idea, cite the book, whether you quoted it or not.
:::

:::summary
- **Quote** when the exact words matter; **paraphrase** in your own words and structure the rest of the time.
- Introduce sources with **signal phrases**, and explain the evidence afterwards.
- **Plagiarism** is passing off others’ words or ideas as your own.
- Cite in the text (author, year) and list every source alphabetically in a **works cited** list.
:::
