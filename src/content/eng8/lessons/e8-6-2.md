“I saw a deer driving home from school.” Was the deer driving? A **modifier** is a word or phrase that describes something, and when it is in the wrong place, or has nothing to describe, a sentence can say something silly or confusing. In this lesson you will find and fix **misplaced** and **dangling** modifiers.

## Misplaced modifiers

:::definition Modifier
A **modifier** is a word or phrase that describes or limits another word: *in paper cups*, *with a leather wallet*, *only*, *almost*. A modifier should sit next to the word it describes.
:::

A **misplaced modifier** is placed next to the wrong word, so it seems to describe the wrong thing.

> The man sold a dog with a leather wallet to my neighbour.

*With a leather wallet* sits next to *dog*, so it sounds as though the dog has the wallet. Move it: *The man with a leather wallet sold a dog to my neighbour.*

:::example Fixing a misplaced phrase
@step “Leo served the guests in paper cups cold drinks.”
@step What is in paper cups? The drinks, not the guests.
@step Move the phrase next to *drinks*.
@answer “Leo served the guests cold drinks in paper cups.” Now *in paper cups* comes right after *drinks*, the word it describes.
:::

Small words like **only**, **almost**, **just** and **nearly** change the meaning depending on where they go.

| Sentence | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Only** Leo finished the essay. | nobody else finished it |
| Leo finished **only** the essay. | he finished nothing else |
| She **almost** failed every test. | she came close to failing, but passed every test |
| She failed **almost** every test. | she failed most of them |

## Dangling modifiers

A **dangling modifier** is usually an opening phrase that has no clear word in the sentence to describe. The person doing the action is never named, so the phrase attaches itself to whatever comes after the comma.

> Running late, the bus left without me.

The phrase *running late* should describe the person who was late, but the word right after the comma is *the bus*. So it sounds as though the bus was running late.

:::example Fixing a dangling modifier
@step “Walking to school, the rain started.”
@step Who was walking? Not the rain. The walker is never named.
@step Put the walker straight after the comma.
@answer “Walking to school, I felt the rain start.” (Or rewrite: “As I was walking to school, the rain started.”)
:::

:::mistake Check the word after the comma
Whenever a sentence opens with a phrase like *Tired after the match,* or *To win the prize,*, look at the very next word. It must be the person or thing the phrase describes. “Tired after the match, Leo fell asleep on the bus” works. “Tired after the match, the bus took Leo home” does not.
:::

:::summary
- A **modifier** should sit next to the word it describes.
- A **misplaced modifier** is next to the wrong word; move it.
- Watch where **only**, **almost** and **just** go: they change the meaning.
- A **dangling modifier** has nothing to describe; name the doer straight after the comma.
:::
