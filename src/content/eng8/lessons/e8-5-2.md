The news shapes how we see the world, so it matters how it is made. In this lesson you will learn to tell a **news report** from texts that give opinions, and to explain how **framing** (the choices about words, sources and images) can steer readers towards a view, even in a report that sounds neutral.

## News and opinion

| Kind of text | Its purpose | Clues |
|---|---|---|
| **News report** | to inform | facts: who, what, when, where; no “I think”; several sources |
| **Opinion column** | to argue a personal view | a named writer, “I believe”, persuasive language |
| **Editorial** | to give the newspaper’s own view | “this newspaper believes”, “we urge”; often unsigned |
| **Advertisement** | to sell or promote | a product or brand, a call to buy or join |

:::example Telling them apart
@step “The city council voted 7–2 on Tuesday to build a new bridge over the river. Work will begin in March.”
@step It gives facts (who voted, how, when and what happens next) and no one’s view.
@answer This is a **news report**. Compare: “I have lived in this city for forty years, and I believe this bridge is a waste of our money”, which is an **opinion column**.
:::

Clear labels such as *Opinion* or *Comment* help readers know when they are reading a view rather than a report. If a website places an opinion piece right beside a news report on the same topic, with no label, readers may mistake opinion for neutral fact.

## Framing

:::definition Framing
**Framing** is how a story is presented, which shapes how readers see it. Every report involves choices: which words to use, which facts to put first, whose voices to include and which image to show.
:::

| Choice | How it frames a story |
|---|---|
| **Headline** | “Workers fight for fair pay” focuses on the workers’ cause; “Strike brings city to standstill” focuses on the disruption |
| **Sources** | quoting three people who oppose a new park and nobody who supports it frames the story against the park |
| **Images** | a photo of the one person shouting at a mostly peaceful protest makes the protest seem angrier than it was |
| **Captions** | “Floodwater covers Main Street on Monday morning” is neutral; “Council’s failure leaves Main Street underwater” blames someone |

:::example Finding the frame
@step Two headlines report the same strike.
@step “Workers fight for fair pay” puts the workers first and uses a positive word, *fair*.
@step “Strike brings city to standstill” puts the effect on everyone else first.
@answer One frames the strike as a story of justice; the other as a story of inconvenience. Both may be accurate, but they lead readers to different feelings.
:::

:::mistake Framing is not the same as lying
A framed story can be entirely true. Framing works through what is chosen and stressed, not through false facts. That is why it is easy to miss. Ask: what does this story stress, and whose voices are heard?

:::summary
- A **news report** informs; an **opinion column** argues a writer’s view; an **editorial** is the newspaper’s own view; an **advertisement** sells.
- **Framing** is how a story is presented: headlines, sources, images and captions.
- A neutral caption states what, when and where, without blame or loaded words.
:::
