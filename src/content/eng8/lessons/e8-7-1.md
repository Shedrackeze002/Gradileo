In Unit 4 you took arguments apart. Now you will build one. An **argument essay** tries to convince readers to accept a position, using reasons and evidence, and it deals fairly with the other side. In this lesson you will write a strong **thesis** and plan a **counterargument** paragraph.

## The thesis

:::definition Thesis
The **thesis** is one sentence that states the writer’s position on the issue. In an argument essay, it usually comes at the end of the introduction, after a hook and some background.
:::

A strong thesis is:

| Quality | Weak | Strong |
|---|---|---|
| **Arguable**: people could disagree | Many schools start at about eight in the morning. | Schools should start an hour later to help students sleep. |
| **Specific**: says exactly what, where or when | Cars are bad and something should be done. | Our town should ban cars from the high street on Saturdays. |
| **Confident**: takes a clear side | I think homework is kind of bad, maybe. | Homework should be limited to thirty minutes a night. |

A thesis can also **preview** the main reasons, which shows readers how the essay will be organised:

> Uniforms should stay because they save money, reduce bullying and build pride.

Each reason then becomes a body paragraph.

:::mistake Don’t announce
“This essay is going to talk about school start times” tells the reader the topic but not your position. A thesis says what you think, not what you will discuss.
:::

## The counterargument paragraph

A strong argument essay includes a paragraph that faces the other side. It shows the writer is fair and makes the argument stronger, because it answers the reader’s doubts before they can grow.

The best order is:

1. **State** the opposing view fairly: *Some people argue that…*
2. **Concede** any fair point: *It is true that…*
3. **Rebut** it: *However…*, *But this overlooks…*

:::example Building the paragraph
@step Thesis: “Schools should start an hour later.”
@step Counterargument: “Some people argue that a later start would disrupt parents’ work.”
@step Concession: “It is true that some parents leave for work early.”
@step Rebuttal: “However, schools could open their doors at the usual time for a supervised breakfast club, so parents’ routines would not need to change.”
@answer The writer admits a real problem, then answers it with a practical solution, which is far more convincing than ignoring it.
:::

A rebuttal must answer the worry directly. Calling the other side silly, or simply repeating your thesis, is not a rebuttal. “Teachers can set clear rules for when phones are used for learning” answers the claim that phones distract students; “That is a silly idea” does not.

:::summary
- A **thesis** is one arguable, specific, confident sentence, usually at the end of the introduction.
- A thesis can preview the essay’s main reasons.
- In a counterargument paragraph, **state** the other view, **concede** what is fair, then **rebut** it.
:::
