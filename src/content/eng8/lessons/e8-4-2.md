Over two thousand years ago, the Greek thinker Aristotle described three ways a speaker can persuade an audience. We still use his names for them: **ethos**, **pathos** and **logos**. In this lesson you will identify each appeal and explain why a writer chooses it for a particular audience.

## The three appeals

| Appeal | Appeals to | How it works | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Ethos** | credibility and trust | shows the speaker is expert, experienced or fair | “As a nurse for twenty years, I have seen what poor diets do to children.” |
| **Pathos** | emotion | makes the audience feel pity, fear, anger, hope or pride | “Imagine a child going to bed hungry, night after night.” |
| **Logos** | logic and reason | uses facts, statistics and step-by-step reasoning | “Cycling to school takes 15 minutes and costs nothing; driving takes 10 minutes and costs £3 a day.” |

Ethos can also come from other people’s expertise. “Scientists at three universities reached the same conclusion independently” builds trust by pointing to experts who agree.

:::example Naming the appeal
@step “If we do nothing, our grandchildren will ask us why we stood by as the forests burned.”
@step There are no facts or figures, and the speaker does not mention their own expertise.
@step The sentence makes us imagine our grandchildren blaming us, which stirs guilt and fear.
@answer This is **pathos**, an appeal to emotion.
:::

:::mistake Numbers can hide in pathos
A single upsetting statistic (“one child every minute”) can be used for its emotional punch. Ask what the sentence mainly does: does it reason, or does it make you feel?

## Choosing an appeal for an audience

Good persuaders think about who is listening.

- A group of **scientists** will be most persuaded by **logos**: clear data and reasoning.
- A **charity advert** with a shivering dog and soft music uses **pathos** to make viewers feel pity, so they donate.
- A speaker who mentions their own experience first uses **ethos** to show they can be trusted on the subject.

Each appeal has a risk when used alone. Too much pathos, and readers may feel strongly but not be convinced by reasons. Too much logos, and the argument can feel cold. Weak ethos, and the audience may not trust the facts at all.

:::example Combining the appeals
@step Here is a speech for cycle helmets, written for this lesson. Ethos: “As a paramedic, I have attended hundreds of cycling accidents.”
@step Logos: “Studies show that helmets cut the risk of serious head injury by more than half.”
@step Pathos: “No family should have to hear the news I have had to give.”
@answer Strong speeches often combine all three, because each reaches the audience in a different way: trust, feeling and reason.
:::

:::summary
- **Ethos** appeals to trust, **pathos** to emotion and **logos** to logic.
- Choose the appeal that suits the audience: logos for experts, pathos to move people to act, ethos to build trust.
- Relying on one appeal alone is risky; strong arguments combine all three.
:::
