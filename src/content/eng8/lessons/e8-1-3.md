Every story is told by someone, and that someone decides what we see, what we know and what stays hidden. Changing the **narrator** can change a story completely. In this lesson you will identify the main kinds of narrator and explain how a narrator’s point of view, and their honesty, shape a story.

## Kinds of narrator

| Point of view | Clues | What it can show |
|---|---|---|
| **First person** | the narrator says *I* and takes part in the story | one character’s thoughts, from the inside |
| **Second person** | the reader is called *you* and does the actions | rare in fiction; it puts the reader in the story |
| **Third-person limited** | *he*, *she*, *they*, following one character | that one character’s thoughts and feelings only |
| **Third-person omniscient** | *he*, *she*, *they*, moving between characters | anyone’s thoughts; *omniscient* means “all-knowing” |

> The town slept. In the bakery, Mr. Diaz worried about the rent; at the station, the night guard dreamed of the sea; and in the tallest house, a girl lay awake, planning her escape.

This narrator moves from one character’s mind to the next, so it is **third-person omniscient**.

:::mistake Limited or omniscient?
Both use *he* and *she*. The test is whose thoughts we are told. If we only ever get inside one character’s head, the narrator is limited. A limited narrator can tell us what that character sees and hears, but not what the other characters are secretly thinking.
:::

## How the narrator shapes a story

A writer chooses a narrator for a reason.

- A **first-person** narrator lets us share one character’s thoughts closely, so we feel what they feel. But we only know what they know.
- An **omniscient** narrator can show readers secrets that the characters do not know, which is a good way to build dramatic irony.
- A young or innocent narrator may not understand what they describe. A child who calls a house fire “like fireworks, really exciting” makes the danger feel more shocking to us, because we understand what the child does not.

:::definition Unreliable narrator
An **unreliable narrator** is one whose account we cannot fully trust. They may be lying, confused, too young to understand, or blind to their own faults.
:::

:::example Catching an unreliable narrator
@step The narrator says: “I stayed perfectly calm. Fine, I shouted a bit, and a plate got broken, but I was calm.”
@step He describes himself as calm.
@step In the same breath he admits that he shouted and broke a plate.
@answer His own details contradict his claim, so we should not trust his view of himself. He is an unreliable narrator.
:::

When you meet an unreliable narrator, read between the lines. The story is often about the gap between what the narrator says and what really happened.

:::summary
- **First person** (*I*), **second person** (*you*), **third-person limited** (one character’s mind) and **third-person omniscient** (any character’s mind).
- First person brings us close to one character; omniscient narration can reveal secrets and build dramatic irony.
- An **unreliable narrator** cannot be fully trusted; look for details that contradict what they say.
:::
