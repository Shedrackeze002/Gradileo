Poets rarely say things plainly. They compare, exaggerate and bring objects to life, because a fresh image can make us feel an idea instead of just understanding it. In this lesson you will review the main kinds of **figurative language** and follow an **extended metaphor** through a whole poem.

:::definition Figurative language
**Figurative language** is language that means more than, or something different from, what the words literally say. A writer uses it to create a vivid image or a feeling. Each particular type, such as a simile or a metaphor, is called a **figure of speech**.
:::

## Figures of speech

| Figure | What it does | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Simile** | compares using *like* or *as* | Her laugh rang out like a bell. |
| **Metaphor** | says one thing *is* another | The classroom was a zoo after lunch. |
| **Personification** | gives human qualities to something that is not human | The wind whispered through the trees. |
| **Hyperbole** | exaggerates for effect | I’m so hungry I could eat a horse. |
| **Symbol** | an object that stands for a bigger idea | a dove for peace |

Emily Dickinson personifies death as a polite gentleman:

> Because I could not stop for Death,\
> He kindly stopped for me;\
> The carriage held but just ourselves\
> And Immortality.
>
> — Emily Dickinson, “Because I could not stop for Death” (1890 printing), first stanza

Death “kindly stopped”, as a courteous caller might. Giving death good manners makes it seem calm rather than frightening, which is strangely unsettling.

:::mistake Metaphor or simile?
Look for *like* or *as*. “Life is a broken-winged bird” has neither, so it is a metaphor. “Life is like a broken-winged bird” would be a simile.
:::

## Extended metaphor

:::definition Extended metaphor
An **extended metaphor** is a metaphor that is developed over several lines or a whole poem. The poet keeps adding details to the same comparison.
:::

In “Mother to Son”, Langston Hughes’s speaker compares her life to a staircase.

> Well, son, I’ll tell you:\
> Life for me ain’t been no crystal stair.\
> It’s had tacks in it,\
> And splinters,\
> And boards torn up,\
> And places with no carpet on the floor—\
> Bare.
>
> — Langston Hughes, “Mother to Son” (1922), first lines

:::example Following the metaphor
@step A **crystal stair** would be beautiful, shining and easy to climb. Her life has not been like that.
@step The **tacks** and **splinters** are things that hurt as you climb: the hardships and pain she has faced.
@step The **boards torn up** and **no carpet** suggest gaps, danger and poverty.
@answer Every detail adds to one image: her life has been a hard, painful climb, but she has kept climbing.
:::

In “Dreams” (1923), Hughes uses two metaphors for a life without dreams: “a broken-winged bird / That cannot fly” and “a barren field / Frozen with snow”. Both show a life that cannot rise or grow.

:::summary
- **Simile** (*like*, *as*), **metaphor** (*is*), **personification** (human qualities), **hyperbole** (exaggeration) and **symbol** (an object for an idea).
- An **extended metaphor** builds one comparison across many lines.
- Explain what each detail of the comparison adds to the poem’s meaning.
:::
