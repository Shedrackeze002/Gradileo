The semicolon and the colon look alike, but they do different jobs. Used well, they let you link ideas with precision. In this lesson you will learn when to use each one.

## Semicolons

:::definition Semicolon
A **semicolon** ( ; ) joins two closely related complete sentences (independent clauses). Each side should be able to stand alone as a sentence.
:::

> It rained all day; the match was cancelled.

A full stop would also be correct, but the semicolon shows that the two ideas are closely linked.

A semicolon is one way to fix a **comma splice**, where two sentences are wrongly joined by only a comma.

| Comma splice (wrong) | Fixed |
|---|---|
| The bell rang, everyone left the room. | The bell rang; everyone left the room. |
| | The bell rang, and everyone left the room. |
| | The bell rang. Everyone left the room. |

Semicolons often come before connecting words such as *however*, *therefore* and *meanwhile*. Put the semicolon before the word and a comma after it.

> Leo studied hard; however, the test was difficult.

Semicolons also separate items in a list when the items already contain commas:

> The tour visited Lagos, Nigeria; Accra, Ghana; and Nairobi, Kenya.

:::mistake Both sides must be sentences
“We bought; apples, pears and plums” is wrong, because *We bought* cannot stand alone. Before you use a semicolon, check that the words on each side would make sense as a sentence on their own.
:::

## Colons

:::definition Colon
A **colon** ( : ) comes after a complete sentence and announces that something follows which explains or completes it: a **list**, an **explanation** or a **quotation**.
:::

| Use | Example |
|---|---|
| a list | Bring three things: a pen, a ruler and a calculator. |
| an explanation | Leo had one reason for leaving: he was exhausted. |
| emphasis | One word sums up the day: chaos. |
| a quotation | Puck sums up the chaos: “Lord, what fools these mortals be!” |

:::example Colon or semicolon?
@step “The sign gave one clear warning \_\_\_ the bridge was closed.”
@step The second part tells us what the warning was. It explains the first part.
@answer Use a **colon**: “The sign gave one clear warning: the bridge was closed.”
:::

:::mistake No colon straight after a verb
“My favourite subjects are: art and music” is wrong, because *My favourite subjects are* is not a complete sentence. Either drop the colon (“My favourite subjects are art and music”) or complete the sentence first (“I have two favourite subjects: art and music”).

:::summary
- A **semicolon** joins two related complete sentences, comes before *however* and similar words, and separates list items that contain commas.
- A **colon** follows a complete sentence and introduces a list, an explanation or a quotation.
- Never put a colon straight after a verb, and check that both sides of a semicolon are sentences.
:::
