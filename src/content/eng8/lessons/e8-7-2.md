A literary paragraph makes a point about a text and proves it with evidence. The difference between a good paragraph and a great one is usually what happens *after* the quotation. In this lesson you will learn to **embed** quotations smoothly and to write **analysis** instead of summary.

## Embedding quotations

:::definition Embedding a quotation
To **embed** a quotation is to blend a short quotation into your own sentence, so that it fits the grammar of what you are writing.
:::

| Floating (weak) | Embedded (strong) |
|---|---|
| “Lord, what fools these mortals be!” This is what Puck says. | Puck mocks the lovers as “fools”, which shows his amusement at human love. |
| Lysander. Quote: the course of true love never did run smooth. | Lysander warns that “the course of true love never did run smooth.” |

A **floating** quotation is dropped into an essay with no introduction or comment. Every quotation needs a lead-in and an explanation of why it matters.

Tips for embedding:

- **Keep it short.** Choose the few key words and embed them, rather than copying a whole speech.
- **Quote exactly.** Only the writer’s exact words go inside the quotation marks: Helena says that Hermia is “little” but “fierce”.
- **Use square brackets** for a small change that makes the quotation fit: “[Love] looks not with the eyes”.

## From summary to analysis

A literary paragraph often follows **PEEL**:

| Step | What you do |
|---|---|
| **Point** | make a clear claim that answers the question |
| **Evidence** | support it with a short, embedded quotation |
| **Explain** | say what the quotation shows, and how its words create that effect |
| **Link** | connect the point back to the essay question |

The **explanation** is where the analysis happens. Summary retells what happens; analysis explains what the writer’s choices reveal.

| Summary | Analysis |
|---|---|
| Puck puts the love potion on the wrong Athenian. | Puck’s word “fools” shows he finds human love ridiculous. |

:::example Zooming in on a word
@step Question: How does Shakespeare present Hermia?
@step Point: Hermia is small but powerful.
@step Evidence: Helena warns that “though she be but little, she is fierce.”
@step Explain: the word “but” turns a weakness into a strength, so Hermia’s size makes her fierceness all the more surprising.
@step Link: Shakespeare presents Hermia as someone who refuses to be underestimated, which matches her defiance of her father.
@answer The paragraph proves its point by zooming in on one word and explaining its effect.
:::

:::mistake Saying where is not saying why
“This is a quote from Act 4” tells the reader where the words come from, not what they show. After every quotation, ask yourself: *so what?*
