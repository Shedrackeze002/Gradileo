A poem’s **form** is its shape, and its **metre** is its beat. Poets choose both on purpose, so a good reader asks not just *what* the form is but *why* the poet chose it. In this lesson you will identify common forms and learn to hear and count metre.

## Forms of poetry

| Form | What to look for |
|---|---|
| **Haiku** | three lines, traditionally of 5, 7 and 5 syllables in English, capturing one moment, often in nature |
| **Rhyming couplets** | lines that rhyme in pairs (AA BB CC…) |
| **Sonnet** | 14 lines, usually in iambic pentameter, with a set rhyme scheme |
| **Blank verse** | unrhymed lines of iambic pentameter |
| **Free verse** | no regular rhyme and no regular beat |

Here is a haiku written for this lesson:

> Old pond in the rain—\
> a frog’s single splash, and then\
> the rain, the rain, rain.

Robert Frost’s “Nothing Gold Can Stay” (1923) is eight short lines in rhyming couplets:

> Nature’s first green is gold,\
> Her hardest hue to hold.\
> Her early leaf’s a flower;\
> But only so an hour.\
> Then leaf subsides to leaf.\
> So Eden sank to grief,\
> So dawn goes down to day.\
> Nothing gold can stay.
>
> — Robert Frost, “Nothing Gold Can Stay” (1923)

:::example Why this form?
@step The poem is about beauty that lasts only a moment.
@step It is very short, and its quick, neat couplets snap shut one after another.
@answer The brief, tight form matches its subject: the poem is over almost as soon as it begins, like the gold of early spring.
:::

:::mistake Blank verse is not free verse
Both are unrhymed. But **blank verse** keeps a regular beat (iambic pentameter), while **free verse** has no regular beat at all. Shakespeare wrote most of his plays in blank verse.
:::

## Metre

Metre is built from **feet**: small groups of stressed (DUM) and unstressed (da) syllables.

| Foot | Pattern | Example word |
|---|---|---|
| **Iamb** | da-DUM | to-DAY |
| **Trochee** | DUM-da | GAR-den |

The number of feet in a line gives the second part of the name: **tetrameter** has four feet, and **pentameter** (*penta* means five) has five.

:::example Counting feet
@step Take Lysander’s line from *A Midsummer Night’s Dream*: “The course of true love never did run smooth.”
@step Mark the beats: the COURSE | of TRUE | love NEV | er DID | run SMOOTH.
@step Each foot is da-DUM, and there are five of them.
@answer The line is **iambic pentameter**.
:::

William Blake’s “The Tyger” (1794) opens with a different beat: “TY-ger TY-ger, BURN-ing BRIGHT”. That is DUM-da DUM-da DUM-da DUM, so it is **trochaic**, with the final weak syllable dropped. The heavy first beats sound like a hammer striking.

Iambic pentameter is often said to sound like natural speech, because English words and phrases tend to fall into da-DUM patterns. When a poet breaks a steady beat with a heavy stress at the start of a line, it makes that word stand out.

- **Haiku** (5-7-5), **rhyming couplets**, **sonnet** (14 lines), **blank verse** (unrhymed iambic pentameter) and **free verse** (no regular rhyme or beat).
- Ask why a poet chose a form: it often matches the subject.
- An **iamb** is da-DUM; a **trochee** is DUM-da. **Tetrameter** has four feet; **pentameter** has five.
