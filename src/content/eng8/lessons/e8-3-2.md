Shakespeare’s English is over four hundred years old, so it can look strange at first. But most of it is the same language we speak today, with a handful of old words and a different word order. In this lesson you will learn the keys that unlock it, and you will see why Shakespeare gives some characters **verse** and others **prose**.

## Old words, new meanings

| Shakespeare | Today |
|---|---|
| thou, thee | you |
| thy, thine | your, yours |
| art | are (*thou art* = you are) |
| hath, doth | has, does |
| wherefore | why (not “where”) |
| ere | before |
| ill | bad, badly |

*Thou* and *thee* were used with friends, family and people of lower rank; *you* was more formal or respectful. So when a character switches between them, it can show a change in feeling.

:::example Decoding a line
@step When Oberon meets his queen in the wood, he says: “Ill met by moonlight, proud Titania.”
@step *Ill* means bad or unlucky, and *met* is “meeting”, so “ill met” means “a bad meeting”.
@answer He means: “It is bad luck to meet you here tonight, proud Titania.” The greeting shows how angry their quarrel has made them.
:::

Some words have changed meaning. When Quince sees Bottom with an ass’s head, he cries: “Bless thee, Bottom! bless thee! thou art translated.” Here *translated* means **changed or transformed**, not put into another language.

Shakespeare also moves words around to fit the rhythm. “The course of true love never did run smooth” uses an extra *did*; in modern word order it means “True love has never been easy.” And when Puck boasts, “I’ll put a girdle round about the earth / In forty minutes”, a *girdle* is a belt: he can fly all the way round the world very fast.

:::mistake “Wherefore” means “why”
Many people think *wherefore* means “where”. It means “why”. Juliet’s famous “Wherefore art thou Romeo?” means “Why are you Romeo?”, not “Where are you?”
:::

## Verse and prose

Shakespeare uses three kinds of speech in the play, and his choice tells us about the characters.

| Kind | What it is | Who speaks it |
|---|---|---|
| **Blank verse** | unrhymed iambic pentameter | the court and the lovers, in serious moments |
| **Rhyming verse** | rhymed lines, often couplets or short sung lines | the lovers in the woods, and the fairies |
| **Prose** | ordinary sentences, with no regular beat | the mechanicals, such as Bottom |

“The course of true love never did run smooth” is **verse**: a line of iambic pentameter (the COURSE | of TRUE | love NEV | er DID | run SMOOTH).

:::example Why prose for Bottom?
@step After his night as an ass, Bottom wakes and says: “I have had a most rare vision. I have had a dream, past the wit of man to say what dream it was.”
@step He speaks in prose, the language of ordinary working people in the play.
@step The words reach for something wonderful but keep stumbling, as if he cannot quite say it.
@answer The prose suggests an ordinary man struggling to describe something magical, which is both funny and touching.
:::

Once the potion takes hold, the lovers often speak in neat rhyming couplets, and the sing-song rhymes suit their silly, magic-made romance. The fairies sometimes speak in short, rhyming, song-like lines, which makes them sound magical and different from humans.

:::summary
- *Thou*, *thee* = you; *thy*, *thine* = your; *art* = are; *wherefore* = why; *ill* = bad.
- Some words have shifted meaning: *translated* = transformed; *girdle* = belt.
- **Blank verse** is unrhymed iambic pentameter; **prose** is ordinary sentences.
- The court and lovers speak verse, the mechanicals speak prose, and the fairies often speak in rhyme.
:::
