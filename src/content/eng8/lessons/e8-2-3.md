The **tone** of a poem is the speaker’s attitude towards the subject: proud, bitter, fond, mocking. Tone is not always steady. Many poems **shift**, changing direction partway through, and the shift is often where the meaning lies. In this lesson you will describe tone precisely and find where and why it changes.

## Naming the tone precisely

“Happy” and “sad” are rarely enough. Many poems are not simply one or the other, so a precise word shows that you have read closely.

| Instead of… | try… |
|---|---|
| happy | joyful, playful, celebratory, content |
| sad | wistful, mournful, bitter, nostalgic |
| angry | defiant, indignant, scornful |
| proud | arrogant, boastful, confident |
| saying the opposite | ironic, sarcastic, mocking |

*Wistful* names a gentle, longing sadness. *Nostalgic* means looking back fondly on happy times that are gone. *Defiant* means refusing to give in.

:::example Finding the tone
@step In Shelley’s “Ozymandias”, the words on a statue’s base read: “My name is Ozymandias, King of Kings; / Look on my Works, ye Mighty, and despair!”
@step The king calls himself the king of all kings, and tells other powerful rulers to despair because they can never match him.
@answer The tone is **arrogant**.
:::

To find the tone, look at word choice (*diction*), images and punctuation. Ask: how does the speaker feel about this?

## Shifts

:::definition Shift
A **shift** (or **turn**) is a change in a poem’s tone, speaker, time or idea. Words like *but*, *yet*, *then* and *now* often signal one.
:::

Here is the whole of “Ozymandias”. A traveller describes a broken statue in the desert.

> I met a traveller from an antique land,\
> Who said—“Two vast and trunkless legs of stone\
> Stand in the desert. . . . Near them, on the sand,\
> Half sunk a shattered visage lies, whose frown,\
> And wrinkled lip, and sneer of cold command,\
> Tell that its sculptor well those passions read\
> Which yet survive, stamped on these lifeless things,\
> The hand that mocked them, and the heart that fed;\
> And on the pedestal, these words appear:\
> My name is Ozymandias, King of Kings;\
> Look on my Works, ye Mighty, and despair!\
> Nothing beside remains. Round the decay\
> Of that colossal Wreck, boundless and bare\
> The lone and level sands stretch far away.”
>
> — Percy Bysshe Shelley, “Ozymandias” (1818)

:::example Tracing the shift
@step Up to “despair!”, we hear the king’s boast: his works will make the mighty despair.
@step Then: “Nothing beside remains.” The great works are gone. Only a broken statue and empty sand are left.
@step The boast now sounds ironic. The mighty should despair, but for a different reason: all power fades.
@answer The tone shifts from arrogant pride to irony, and the “lone and level sands” mock the king’s boast, showing that power does not last.
:::

In “Nothing Gold Can Stay”, Frost’s tone moves from delight in spring’s first gold to sad acceptance: “But only so an hour.” In “Mother to Son”, the mother describes her hard life, then turns: “But all the time / I’se been a-climbin’ on”. The shift shows her determination to keep going.

:::mistake Don’t just say where; say why
“The tone shifts in line 12” is only half an answer. Say what it shifts *from* and *to*, and what the change makes us understand.
:::

:::summary
- **Tone** is the speaker’s attitude; describe it with precise words such as *wistful*, *defiant*, *arrogant* or *ironic*.
- A **shift** is a change in tone, speaker, time or idea, often signalled by *but*, *yet* or *then*.
- Always say what the tone shifts from and to, and why it matters.
:::
