Two writers can tell the same story and make it feel completely different. The difference is **voice**. In this lesson you will create a narrator with a distinctive voice, and you will write **dialogue** that sounds real and says more than its words.

## Narrative voice

:::definition Narrative voice
**Narrative voice** is the personality and attitude that comes through in how a story is told. It is built from word choice, sentence rhythm and what the narrator notices and thinks.
:::

Compare these two openings.

> The gym was flooded. It happened on a Tuesday.

> Look, I never meant to flood the gym. Nobody believes that, but it’s true.

The first gives facts. The second has a strong voice: the narrator speaks straight to the reader, sounds defensive, and is funny without trying to be. We already want to know what happened.

Voice comes from:

| Tool | Example |
|---|---|
| **Word choice** | “My knees had turned to custard” instead of “I was scared” |
| **Sentence rhythm** | a long, breathless sentence for excitement; short ones for shock |
| **Attitude** | humour, bitterness, wonder, worry |
| **Fit with the narrator** | calm, reflective sentences for an old narrator looking back; quick, lively ones for a child |

:::example Rhythm as voice
@step “And then the gates opened and the crowd roared and I ran and ran and I didn’t even feel my legs.”
@step The sentence piles up clauses with “and”, without stopping for breath.
@answer The rush of words shows the narrator’s excitement: we feel the race, not just hear about it.
:::

:::mistake Keep the voice steady
If a narrator who has been chatty and funny suddenly sounds like a textbook, readers may lose trust in the narrator and feel confused. Keep a voice consistent unless the change is deliberate and signalled.
:::

## Writing dialogue

Punctuation first:

- Speech goes inside **speech marks**, and the punctuation that ends the speech goes inside them too: “We’re late,” said Ada.
- Start a **new line** each time a different character speaks.
- A tag such as *said Ada* stays lower case after the comma.

**Tags** and **action beats** show who is speaking.

| Kind | Example | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| Plain tag | “I’m not going,” said Ada. | “said” is almost invisible, so the reader focuses on the words spoken |
| Fancy tag | “I’m not going,” Ada retorted angrily. | can distract if used too often |
| Action beat | Ada slammed the door. “I’m not going.” | shows who speaks and how she feels, with no tag at all |

:::definition Subtext
**Subtext** is the meaning beneath the words, which characters do not say directly. Real people rarely say exactly what they feel, so dialogue with subtext sounds more real.
:::

:::example Reading subtext
@step “Nice haircut.”
@step “Thanks. It’ll grow back.”
@answer The second speaker never says “I hate my haircut”, but “It’ll grow back” shows exactly that. Compare: “I am upset about my haircut.” “I can see that you are upset.” which says everything and feels flat.
:::

:::summary
- **Voice** comes from word choice, rhythm and attitude, and should fit the narrator.
- Keep a voice consistent unless a change is deliberate.
- Punctuation goes inside the speech marks; a new speaker starts a new line.
- Use “said” and **action beats** more than fancy tags, and let **subtext** carry feelings.
:::
