Not every source deserves to be believed, and not every argument that sounds convincing is sound. In this lesson you will learn to judge whether a source is **credible**, and to spot common **fallacies**: tricks of reasoning that look persuasive but fall apart when you look closely.

## Judging a source

:::definition Primary and secondary sources
A **primary source** is a first-hand account or original record from the time: a diary, a letter, a photograph, an interview or the data from an experiment. A **secondary source** describes or interprets primary sources later: a textbook, an encyclopedia article or a review.
:::

To judge whether a source is **credible** (trustworthy), ask five questions.

| Question | What to look for |
|---|---|
| **Who wrote it?** | an expert with relevant qualifications, or a named organisation with a good record |
| **Why was it written?** | to inform, or to sell something or win you over? |
| **When was it published?** | recent enough for the topic |
| **Does it show its evidence?** | sources, data and references you can check |
| **Do other good sources agree?** | the same findings from independent experts |

A **peer-reviewed** journal is especially credible, because other experts checked the research before it was published. An anonymous forum post or a company’s advert is much less so.

:::example Spotting a conflict of interest
@step A study finds that energy drinks improve concentration.
@step The study was paid for by an energy-drink company.
@step The company has something to gain if the results look good.
@answer The funding could create a **conflict of interest**. The study is not automatically wrong, but its results need checking against independent research.
:::

:::mistake Looks are not evidence
A website with a smart design and attractive colours can still be wrong, and a plain one can be excellent. Design says nothing about accuracy; expertise, date and evidence do.
:::

## Spotting faulty reasoning

:::definition Fallacy
A **fallacy** is a flaw in reasoning that makes an argument weak, even if it sounds convincing.
:::

| Fallacy | What it does | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Bandwagon** | says something is right because many people do it | “Everyone is buying this phone, so it must be the best.” |
| **False choice** | offers only two options when there are more | “Either we ban all cars, or the air will be poisoned forever.” |
| **Hasty generalization** | judges a whole group from too few examples | “My two cousins hated the film, so everyone will hate it.” |
| **Attack on the person** | attacks the speaker instead of their argument | “Why listen to her views on the park? She can’t even keep her own garden tidy.” |

:::example Naming the fallacy
@step “I met a rude tourist once, so tourists are all rude.”
@step The speaker has one example.
@step From it, they judge a whole group of millions of people.
@answer This is a **hasty generalization**: one case is far too few to support a claim about everyone.
:::

When you spot a fallacy, explain what is missing. A bandwagon argument gives popularity instead of evidence; a false choice hides the middle options; an attack on the person never answers what the person actually said.

:::summary
- **Primary sources** are first-hand; **secondary sources** interpret them later.
- Judge credibility by the author, purpose, date, evidence and agreement with other sources. Watch for conflicts of interest.
- Common **fallacies**: bandwagon, false choice, hasty generalization and attack on the person.
:::
