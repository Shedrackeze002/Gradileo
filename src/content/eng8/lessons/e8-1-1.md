Most novels tell more than one story at a time. Alongside the main story there are smaller ones, and writers arrange all of them with care: where to begin, what to hide and when to surprise us. In this lesson you will learn how a **main plot** and a **subplot** work together, and you will meet four **plot devices** that writers use to shape a story.

## Main plot and subplot

:::definition Main plot and subplot
The **main plot** is the central story, built around the main character and the main conflict. A **subplot** is a smaller, secondary story that runs alongside it, often involving other characters.
:::

Imagine a novel about a girl, Ada, training for a national swimming final. That is the main plot. Her brother is also trying to fix up an old car before his driving test, and her best friend is falling out with her parents. Those are subplots.

A good subplot is never just filler. It usually does one of three things:

| What it does | Example |
|---|---|
| **Echoes** the main plot | the brother’s car keeps breaking down, just as Ada keeps failing her time, so both stories are about refusing to give up |
| **Contrasts** with the main plot | the friend gives up on her dream, which makes Ada’s determination stand out |
| **Feeds into** the main plot | the brother’s car breaks down on the morning of the final, so Ada must find another way there |

Near the end, the strands often meet. Events in a subplot can change how the main conflict is resolved, which makes the ending feel earned.

## Plot devices

A **plot device** is a technique for arranging events. Here are four you will meet often.

:::definition In medias res
Latin for “in the middle of things”. The story opens in the middle of the action and explains what led up to it later. It grabs the reader at once.
:::

:::definition Twist ending
A sudden, surprising turn near the end that changes how we understand everything before it. A good twist is surprising but, looking back, fair: the clues were there.
:::

:::definition Frame story
A story told inside another story. An outer story (the frame) sets up a character who then tells the inner story, which is usually the main one.
:::

:::definition Red herring
A false clue that leads readers (and characters) to the wrong conclusion. Red herrings are common in mystery stories.
:::

:::example Spotting a frame story
@step In Samuel Taylor Coleridge’s poem “The Rime of the Ancient Mariner” (1798), a guest on his way to a wedding is stopped in the street by an old sailor.
@step The sailor then tells the long tale of his voyage, which takes up almost the whole poem.
@step The meeting in the street is the outer story; the voyage is the inner one.
@answer It is a frame story: the wedding guest’s meeting with the Mariner frames the Mariner’s tale.
:::

:::mistake A twist is not just any surprise
A twist changes the meaning of what came before. If a story simply ends with something unexpected that has no link to earlier events, it feels like a trick, not a twist. When you name a twist, say what it makes you see differently.
:::

:::summary
- The **main plot** is the central story; a **subplot** is a smaller story alongside it.
- Subplots echo, contrast with or feed into the main plot, and they often meet it near the end.
- **In medias res** begins in the middle of the action.
- A **twist ending** changes how we understand the story; a **frame story** is a story inside a story; a **red herring** is a false clue.
:::
