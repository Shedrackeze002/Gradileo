**Irony** is a gap between what is said or expected and what is really true. It is one of the most powerful tools a writer has, because it makes readers think twice. In this lesson you will learn to tell the three main kinds of irony apart and to explain what each one adds to a story.

## Three kinds of irony

| Kind | The gap | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Verbal irony** | between what a character says and what they mean | looking at pouring rain and saying, “Lovely weather for a picnic.” |
| **Situational irony** | between what we expect to happen and what does happen | a fire station burns down |
| **Dramatic irony** | between what the audience knows and what a character knows | we know the cup is poisoned, but the king drinks from it cheerfully |

:::definition Sarcasm
**Sarcasm** is verbal irony used to mock or hurt. “Oh, brilliant, you’ve broken it again” is sarcastic. Not all verbal irony is sarcasm: irony can also be gentle or funny.
:::

:::example Naming the irony
@step In Shakespeare’s *Romeo and Juliet*, Romeo finds Juliet lying still and believes she is dead.
@step The audience knows she has only taken a potion that makes her sleep.
@step The gap is between what the audience knows and what the character believes.
@answer This is dramatic irony.
:::

:::mistake Irony is not bad luck
Rain on your birthday is unlucky, but it is not ironic unless something made us expect the opposite. Irony needs a clear gap: a weather forecaster planning an outdoor wedding on the one day she forecast sunshine, only for it to pour, would be situational irony.
:::

## What irony does

Naming the kind of irony is only the first step. The real question is: **what does the gap make us feel or understand?**

- **Dramatic irony** builds tension. Because we know what a character does not, we watch every line with a double meaning only we can see, and we may want to shout a warning.
- **Situational irony** can make a point about life. When a greedy man finds treasure and is then locked inside the vault with it, the twist punishes his greed in a fitting way.
- **Verbal irony** reveals character. What a person says ironically, and why, shows their humour, bitterness or anger.

O. Henry’s story “The Gift of the Magi” (1905) is built on situational irony. A poor young wife, Della, sells her beautiful long hair to buy a chain for her husband’s watch. Her husband, Jim, sells his watch to buy her a set of combs for her hair. Each gift is now useless.

:::example Explaining the effect
@step Expectation: each gift will delight the other person.
@step Reality: each person has sold the very thing the gift was meant for.
@step The gifts fail, but the sacrifice behind them shows how much each loves the other.
@answer The irony shows that their love matters more than any object: the gifts are useless, but the love is not.
:::

:::summary
- **Verbal irony**: saying the opposite of what you mean. **Sarcasm** is verbal irony used to mock.
- **Situational irony**: the opposite of what we expect happens.
- **Dramatic irony**: the audience knows something a character does not.
- Always explain the effect: dramatic irony builds tension, situational irony makes a point, verbal irony reveals character.
:::
