A **theme** is a story’s message about life. In earlier grades you found themes and supported them with evidence. In Grade 8 you will make your theme statements more **precise**, and you will **compare** how two texts treat the same theme.

## Writing a precise theme statement

:::definition Theme statement
A **theme statement** is a full sentence that says what a text suggests about a big idea. “Courage” is a topic; “Real courage can mean admitting you are wrong” is a theme.
:::

A strong theme statement:

- is a **complete sentence**, not a single word;
- makes a **point about life** that goes beyond the plot, so it does not name the characters;
- is **specific** to this text, not a vague cliché.

:::mistake Avoid clichés
“Never give up” and “Family is important” could describe thousands of stories. Clichés are vague and miss what the story actually says. Ask what this particular story shows *about* the idea.
:::

:::example Sharpening a theme
@step A story shows two sisters who have not spoken for years. When their father falls ill, they meet at the hospital and slowly begin to talk again.
@step First try: “Family is important.” This is true but vague; it could fit any family story.
@step Ask what the story shows about family: the bond lasts even through silence and old arguments.
@answer A precise theme: “Family bonds can survive even long silences and old arguments.”
:::

## Comparing themes across texts

Some themes appear across many times and cultures: love and loss, the cost of ambition, growing up, the struggle between good and evil. These are **universal themes**. Readers compare them to see how different writers and cultures answer the same questions.

To compare two texts, follow three steps:

1. Name the **shared topic** (for example, ambition).
2. Say what **each text suggests** about it.
3. Explain **how the writers’ choices** (plot, character, imagery, ending) create the difference.

| | Story A | Story B |
|---|---|---|
| Topic | ambition | ambition |
| What it shows | ambition ruins a king who will do anything for power | ambition helps a poor girl build a business |
| Message | unchecked ambition destroys | ambition, joined with hard work, can lift people up |

:::example A comparison sentence
@step Both stories are about loneliness.
@step In the first, the main character ends the story still alone; in the second, she finds a friend.
@answer “Both stories explore loneliness, but only the second suggests that it can be overcome.”
:::

Notice that this sentence names the shared theme *and* the difference in one go. Weak comparisons only describe surface details (“The first story is longer”), or give a personal preference (“I liked the second one more”).
