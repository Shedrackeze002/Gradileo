Some data are not numbers but categories: yes or no, walk or ride, Grade 7 or Grade 8. When each person answers two such questions, a **two-way table** shows how the answers go together.

## Two-way tables

:::definition Two-way table
A **two-way table** counts data that are sorted by two categories at once: one category down the side (the rows) and one across the top (the columns). Each cell counts the people who fit both its row and its column. The totals are at the end of each row and the foot of each column, and the grand total is in the corner.
:::

:::figure

:::

In the table above, $24$ students have a pet and play a sport, $40$ students have a pet altogether, $45$ play a sport, and $100$ were asked.

:::example Completing a two-way table
In a survey of $80$ students, $50$ walk to school; $30$ of the walkers and $12$ of the others are in Grade 7. How many Grade 8 students ride?
@step The students who ride: $80 - 50 = 30$.
@step Of those, $12$ are in Grade 7, so $30 - 12 = 18$ are in Grade 8.
@answer $18$ Grade 8 students ride. (Check: Grade 8 walkers are $50 - 30 = 20$, and $30 + 12 + 20 + 18 = 80$.)
:::

## Relative frequencies

Counts are hard to compare when the groups have different sizes. Dividing by a total gives a **relative frequency**, usually written as a percent. Which total we divide by depends on the question.

:::definition Relative frequency
- A percent **of all** the people divides by the grand total.
- A **row percent** divides by the row's total: "What percent of the students with a pet play a sport?"
- A **column percent** divides by the column's total.
:::

:::example Row percents
Use the pet and sport table. What percent of the students with a pet play a sport? What percent of the students without a pet?
@step With a pet: $24 \div 40 = 0.6$, which is $60\%$.
@step Without a pet: $21 \div 60 = 0.35$, which is $35\%$.
@answer $60\%$ of pet owners play a sport, but only $35\%$ of the others.
:::

:::mistake Dividing by the wrong total
"What percent of the students with a pet play a sport?" is about the pet owners only, so divide by $40$ (their total), not by $100$ or by $45$.
:::

## Looking for an association

If the row percents are about the same, the row makes no difference to the column. If they are far apart, the two categories are **associated**: knowing one tells us something about the other. Above, $60\%$ against $35\%$ suggests that having a pet and playing a sport are associated in this group. An association does not show that one causes the other.

:::summary
- A two-way table counts data sorted by two categories; each row and column adds up to its total.
- A missing count is a total minus the other counts in its row or column.
- A relative frequency divides a count by a total: the grand total, a row total or a column total, as the question asks.
- Row percents that differ a lot suggest an association between the categories, which is not the same as one causing the other.
:::
