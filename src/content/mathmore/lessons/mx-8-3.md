A graph does more than show values. Its steepness tells us how fast one quantity changes compared with the other, and the area underneath it can be a quantity of its own. On a velocity–time graph, the gradient is the acceleration and the area is the distance travelled.

## Gradients as rates of change

:::definition Rate of change
The **gradient** of a graph is a **rate of change**: how much the quantity on the vertical axis changes for each unit on the horizontal axis.
$$\text{gradient} = \frac{\text{change in } y}{\text{change in } x}$$
On a distance–time graph the gradient is the **speed**. On a velocity–time graph it is the **acceleration**, in m/s² when velocity is in m/s and time in seconds.
:::

:::example Acceleration from a velocity–time graph
The graph shows a tram moving between two stops. Find its acceleration in the first $4$ seconds, and its deceleration at the end.
@step First $4$ s: the velocity rises from $0$ to $12$ m/s, so the gradient is $\dfrac{12}{4} = 3$.
@step From $10$ s to $14$ s: the velocity falls from $12$ to $0$, so the gradient is $\dfrac{0 - 12}{4} = -3$.
@answer The acceleration is $3$ m/s², and the deceleration at the end is $3$ m/s².

## Tangents to curves

On a curved graph the gradient keeps changing. To estimate it at one point, draw a **tangent**: a straight line that just touches the curve there, with the same steepness. Then find the gradient of the tangent.

:::example Speed from a tangent
A ball rolls $d = t^2$ metres in $t$ seconds. A tangent drawn at $t = 3$ passes through $(2,\ 3)$ and $(4,\ 15)$. Estimate the speed of the ball at $t = 3$.
@step Gradient of the tangent $= \dfrac{15 - 3}{4 - 2} = \dfrac{12}{2} = 6$.
@answer The ball’s speed at $t = 3$ s is about $6$ m/s.
:::

## Areas under graphs

:::definition Area under a graph
The area between a graph and the horizontal axis multiplies the two quantities. On a velocity–time graph it is velocity $\times$ time, which is the **distance travelled**.
:::

:::example Distance from a velocity–time graph
Find the total distance the tram travels in the graph above.
@step The area is a trapezium with parallel sides $14$ s (along the bottom) and $10 - 4 = 6$ s (along the top), and height $12$ m/s.
@step Area $= \frac{1}{2}(14 + 6) \times 12 = 120$
@answer The tram travels $120$ m.
:::

When the graph is curved, we split the area into strips of equal width and treat each strip as a trapezium.

:::example Estimating an area with trapezia
A cyclist’s velocity is $0,\ 7,\ 12,\ 15$ and $16$ m/s at $0,\ 2,\ 4,\ 6$ and $8$ seconds, on a smooth curve that bends downwards. Estimate the distance travelled in the $8$ seconds.
@step Strips: $\frac{1}{2}(0 + 7) \times 2 = 7$, $\ \frac{1}{2}(7 + 12) \times 2 = 19$, $\ \frac{1}{2}(12 + 15) \times 2 = 27$, $\ \frac{1}{2}(15 + 16) \times 2 = 31$.
@step Total: $7 + 19 + 27 + 31 = 84$.
@answer About $84$ m. The curve bends downwards, above the straight tops of the trapezia, so this is an underestimate.
:::

:::tip Over or under?
If the curve bends upwards (like $y = x^2$), the trapezia stick out above it, so the estimate is too high. If it bends downwards, the trapezia leave gaps under it, so the estimate is too low.
:::

:::

:::summary
- The gradient of a graph is a rate of change: speed on a distance–time graph, acceleration on a velocity–time graph.
- On a curve, estimate the gradient at a point with a tangent.
- The area under a velocity–time graph is the distance travelled.
- Estimate the area under a curve with trapezia; decide from the way the curve bends whether the estimate is too high or too low.
:::
