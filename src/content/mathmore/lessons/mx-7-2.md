When we calculate with rounded measurements, the answer is uncertain too. A rectangle measured as $8$ cm by $5$ cm, to the nearest centimetre, could have an area anywhere from $7.5 \times 4.5$ to $8.5 \times 5.5$. The bounds of a calculation tell us that range.

## Bounds of sums and products

:::definition Upper and lower bounds
The **upper bound** of a measurement is the largest value it could have before rounding, and the **lower bound** is the smallest. For a calculation, the upper bound is the largest possible answer and the lower bound is the smallest.
:::

To make a sum or a product as big as possible, use the biggest values of both numbers. To make it as small as possible, use the smallest values.

:::example The area of a rectangle
A rectangle is $8$ cm by $5$ cm, each measured to the nearest centimetre. Find the upper and lower bounds of its area.
@step Length: $7.5 \le l < 8.5$. Width: $4.5 \le w < 5.5$.
@step Upper bound $= 8.5 \times 5.5 = 46.75$ cm².
@step Lower bound $= 7.5 \times 4.5 = 33.75$ cm².
@answer The area is from $33.75$ cm² up to (but not including) $46.75$ cm².
:::

## Bounds of differences and quotients

Subtracting or dividing works the other way round for the second number: taking away a **smaller** number, or dividing by a **smaller** number, gives a **bigger** answer.

| To find the… | of $a - b$ | of $a \div b$ |
|---|---|---|
| upper bound | upper of $a$ $-$ lower of $b$ | upper of $a$ $\div$ lower of $b$ |
| lower bound | lower of $a$ $-$ upper of $b$ | lower of $a$ $\div$ upper of $b$ |

:::example A speed
A cyclist rides $200$ m, to the nearest $10$ m, in $25.4$ seconds, to $1$ decimal place. Find the upper bound of the cyclist’s average speed.
@step Distance: $195 \le d < 205$. Time: $25.35 \le t < 25.45$.
@step Speed $= d \div t$. The largest speed is the longest distance in the shortest time.
@step $205 \div 25.35 = 8.0867\ldots$
@answer The upper bound is about $8.09$ m/s.
:::

:::mistake Using the upper bound of both
For $205 \div 25.45$ the answer is smaller, not bigger, because dividing by a bigger number makes the answer smaller. For the upper bound of a quotient, divide the **upper** bound of the top by the **lower** bound of the bottom.
:::

:::tip A suitable degree of accuracy
If the upper and lower bounds of an answer round to the same value, that value is a fair answer. For example, if the bounds are $8.09$ and $7.66$, they agree to $1$ significant figure ($8$), so we can say the speed is about $8$ m/s.
:::

:::summary
- Write the bounds of each measurement first: half a unit either side.
- For $a + b$ and $a \times b$, the upper bound uses both upper bounds, and the lower bound both lower bounds.
- For $a - b$ and $a \div b$, the upper bound uses the upper bound of $a$ and the **lower** bound of $b$, and the other way round for the lower bound.
:::
