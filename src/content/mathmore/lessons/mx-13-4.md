A ball kicked into the air follows a curved path, yet we can describe it with nothing more than the suvat formulas. The secret is that its horizontal and vertical motions are completely separate: gravity only acts downwards, so it changes the vertical velocity and leaves the horizontal velocity alone.

## Two motions at once

:::definition Projectile
A **projectile** is an object thrown or launched into the air that then moves under gravity alone (we ignore air resistance and model it as a particle). If it is projected at speed $U$ at an angle $\theta$ above the horizontal, then:
- **horizontally** there is no force, so the velocity stays $U\cos\theta$ and the distance is $x = (U\cos\theta)t$;
- **vertically** the acceleration is $g$ downwards, starting from $U\sin\theta$, so the suvat formulas apply with $a = -9.8$ (upwards positive).
:::

The **time of flight** is how long it stays in the air, the **range** is the horizontal distance it travels, and its path, a parabola, is its **trajectory**.

:::example Thrown horizontally
A stone is thrown horizontally at $20$ m/s from a cliff $19.6$ m above the sea. Find how far from the foot of the cliff it lands.
@step Vertically (downwards positive): $u = 0$, $a = 9.8$, $s = 19.6$. So $19.6 = 4.9t^2$ and $t = 2$ s.
@step Horizontally: $x = 20 \times 2 = 40$ m.
@answer It lands $40$ m from the foot of the cliff.
:::

## Projected at an angle

:::example A kick from level ground
A ball is kicked at $25$ m/s at an angle $\theta$ above the horizontal, where $\tan\theta = \tfrac{3}{4}$. Find its time of flight, its range and its greatest height.
@step $\sin\theta = \tfrac{3}{5}$ and $\cos\theta = \tfrac{4}{5}$, so $u_x = 20$ m/s and $u_y = 15$ m/s.
@step It lands when the vertical displacement is $0$: $0 = 15t - 4.9t^2$, so $t = \dfrac{15}{4.9} \approx 3.06$ s.
@step Range $= 20 \times 3.06 \approx 61.2$ m.
@step At the top $v_y = 0$: $0 = 15^2 - 19.6h$, so $h \approx 11.5$ m.
@answer Time of flight $\approx 3.06$ s, range $\approx 61.2$ m and greatest height $\approx 11.5$ m.
:::

## Velocity at any time

The horizontal component of the velocity never changes; the vertical component falls by $9.8$ m/s every second. Put them together with Pythagoras’ theorem for the speed, and with $\tan^{-1}$ for the direction.

:::example Speed and direction
For the ball above, find its speed and direction $2$ s after the kick.
@step $v_x = 20$ and $v_y = 15 - 9.8 \times 2 = -4.6$ m/s.
@step Speed $= \sqrt{20^2 + 4.6^2} \approx 20.5$ m/s.
@answer It moves at about $20.5$ m/s at $\tan^{-1}\left(\tfrac{4.6}{20}\right) \approx 13.0^\circ$ below the horizontal (on its way down).
:::

:::mistake Adding the speeds
The speed is not $v_x + v_y$. The two components are at right angles, so the speed is $\sqrt{v_x^2 + v_y^2}$.
:::

:::summary
- Split the initial velocity: $U\cos\theta$ horizontally and $U\sin\theta$ vertically.
- Horizontally the velocity is constant: $x = (U\cos\theta)t$.
- Vertically use suvat with $a = -9.8$: it lands when $s$ reaches the landing height, and it is highest when $v_y = 0$.
- The speed at any time is $\sqrt{v_x^2 + v_y^2}$, and the sign of $v_y$ says whether it is rising or falling.
:::
