A $2 \times 2$ matrix can move every point of the plane at once. Reflections, rotations and enlargements about the origin each have their own matrix, and multiplying matrices combines the movements.

## A matrix as a transformation

:::definition Transformation matrix
A **transformation matrix** is a matrix $\mathbf{M}$ that sends each point $(x, y)$ to an **image** point. We write the point as a column vector and multiply:
$$\begin{pmatrix} a & b \\ c & d \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} x \\ y \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} ax + by \\ cx + dy \end{pmatrix}$$
:::

:::example The image of a point
A transformation has matrix $\begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 0 & 3 \end{pmatrix}$. Find the image of $(4, -1)$.
@step $\begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 0 & 3 \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} 4 \\ -1 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 2 \times 4 + 1 \times (-1) \\ 0 \times 4 + 3 \times (-1) \end{pmatrix}$
@answer $(7, -3)$
:::

## The standard matrices

The columns of a matrix tell us what it does. The first column is the image of $(1, 0)$ and the second column is the image of $(0, 1)$. So to find the matrix of a transformation, we ask where those two points go.

A rotation of $90°$ anticlockwise about the origin sends $(1, 0)$ to $(0, 1)$ and $(0, 1)$ to $(-1, 0)$, so its matrix is $\begin{pmatrix} 0 & -1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$.

| Transformation | Matrix |
| --- | --- |
| Reflection in the $x$-axis | $\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & -1 \end{pmatrix}$ |
| Reflection in the $y$-axis | $\begin{pmatrix} -1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$ |
| Reflection in the line $y = x$ | $\begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$ |
| Reflection in the line $y = -x$ | $\begin{pmatrix} 0 & -1 \\ -1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$ |
| Rotation of $90°$ anticlockwise about the origin | $\begin{pmatrix} 0 & -1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$ |
| Rotation of $90°$ clockwise about the origin | $\begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ -1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$ |
| Rotation of $180°$ about the origin | $\begin{pmatrix} -1 & 0 \\ 0 & -1 \end{pmatrix}$ |
| Enlargement with scale factor $k$ about the origin | $\begin{pmatrix} k & 0 \\ 0 & k \end{pmatrix}$ |

:::tip Work it out instead of memorizing
For any of these, picture where $(1, 0)$ and $(0, 1)$ land and write the two images as the columns. A reflection in the $x$-axis leaves $(1, 0)$ alone and sends $(0, 1)$ to $(0, -1)$, which gives the first matrix in the table.
:::

## The determinant and area

The determinant of $\begin{pmatrix} a & b \\ c & d \end{pmatrix}$ is $ad - bc$. Its size is the **area scale factor** of the transformation: the area of the image equals $|ad - bc|$ times the area of the object.

:::example Area of an image
A triangle of area $5$ square units is transformed by $\begin{pmatrix} 3 & 1 \\ 2 & 4 \end{pmatrix}$. Find the area of its image.
@step Determinant $= 3 \times 4 - 1 \times 2 = 10$
@step Area of the image $= 10 \times 5$
@answer $50$ square units
:::

Reflections and rotations have a determinant of $1$ or $-1$, so they keep areas the same. A negative determinant means that the shape has been flipped over.

## Combining transformations

When a shape is transformed by $\mathbf{P}$ and **then** by $\mathbf{Q}$, a point $\mathbf{x}$ goes first to $\mathbf{P}\mathbf{x}$ and then to $\mathbf{Q}(\mathbf{P}\mathbf{x})$. So the single matrix for the combined transformation is $\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{P}$, with the first transformation written on the right.

:::example Two transformations in a row
A shape is reflected in the $x$-axis, with matrix $\mathbf{P} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & -1 \end{pmatrix}$, and then rotated $90°$ anticlockwise about the origin, with matrix $\mathbf{Q} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & -1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$. Find the single matrix.
@step The combined matrix is $\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{P} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & -1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & -1 \end{pmatrix}$
@step $= \begin{pmatrix} 0 \times 1 + (-1) \times 0 & 0 \times 0 + (-1) \times (-1) \\ 1 \times 1 + 0 \times 0 & 1 \times 0 + 0 \times (-1) \end{pmatrix}$
@answer $\begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$, which is a reflection in the line $y = x$
:::

:::mistake Multiplying in the wrong order
"$\mathbf{P}$ then $\mathbf{Q}$" is $\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{P}$, and not $\mathbf{P}\mathbf{Q}$. Matrix multiplication depends on the order, so the two products are usually different.
:::

## Undoing a transformation

The inverse matrix $\mathbf{M}^{-1}$ undoes $\mathbf{M}$. If $\mathbf{M}$ sends $P$ to $P'$, then $\mathbf{M}^{-1}$ sends $P'$ back to $P$. For $\mathbf{M} = \begin{pmatrix} a & b \\ c & d \end{pmatrix}$,
$$\mathbf{M}^{-1} = \frac{1}{ad - bc}\begin{pmatrix} d & -b \\ -c & a \end{pmatrix}$$

:::example Finding the original point
$\mathbf{M} = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$ maps a point $P$ to $(7, 4)$. Find $P$.
@step Determinant $= 2 \times 1 - 1 \times 1 = 1$, so $\mathbf{M}^{-1} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & -1 \\ -1 & 2 \end{pmatrix}$.
@step $\begin{pmatrix} 1 & -1 \\ -1 & 2 \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} 7 \\ 4 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 3 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix}$
@answer $P = (3, 1)$
:::

:::check mx-transformation-combine 3
:::

:::summary
- A matrix sends $(x, y)$ to $(ax + by, cx + dy)$. Its columns are the images of $(1, 0)$ and $(0, 1)$.
- The size of the determinant $ad - bc$ is the area scale factor.
- "$\mathbf{P}$ then $\mathbf{Q}$" has the single matrix $\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{P}$.
- The inverse matrix undoes the transformation.
:::
