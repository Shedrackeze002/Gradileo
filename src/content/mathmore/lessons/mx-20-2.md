Squaring a number gives the area of a square. Cubing a number does the same job one dimension up: it gives the volume of a cube, and the cube root takes us back from the volume to the edge.

## Perfect cubes

:::definition Perfect cube
A **perfect cube** is the result of multiplying a whole number by itself three times. We write this with the exponent $3$ and say "cubed":
$$4^3 = 4 \times 4 \times 4 = 64$$
So $64$ is a perfect cube. The first few perfect cubes are $1, 8, 27, 64, 125, 216, 343, 512, 729$ and $1\,000$.
:::

:::example Cubing a number
Evaluate $6^3$.
@step $6 \times 6 = 36$
@step $36 \times 6 = 216$
@answer $6^3 = 216$
:::

:::mistake Multiplying by 3
$6^3$ does not mean $6 \times 3$. The exponent counts how many times $6$ is used as a factor: $6 \times 6 \times 6 = 216$, not $18$.
:::

## Cube roots

:::definition Cube root
The **cube root** of a number is the number that, when cubed, gives the original number. We write it with the symbol $\sqrt[3]{\phantom{8}}$:
$$\sqrt[3]{64} = 4 \quad \text{because} \quad 4^3 = 64$$
Cubing and taking the cube root are inverse operations: each one undoes the other.
:::

:::example Finding a cube root
Find $\sqrt[3]{343}$.
@step We need the number whose cube is $343$. Try $7$: $7 \times 7 = 49$ and $49 \times 7 = 343$.
@answer $\sqrt[3]{343} = 7$
:::

:::tip Using the last digit
The cubes of $1$ to $9$ all end in different digits, so the last digit of a perfect cube tells us the last digit of its cube root. A perfect cube that ends in $3$ has a cube root that ends in $7$, because $7^3 = 343$.
:::

:::check mx-perfect-cubes 2
:::

Powers and roots are worked out before multiplying, dividing, adding and subtracting, in the same place in the order of operations as squares and square roots.

:::example Cubes and cube roots in a calculation
Evaluate $2^3 + \sqrt[3]{125} \times 4$.
@step Powers and roots first: $2^3 = 8$ and $\sqrt[3]{125} = 5$
@step Multiply: $5 \times 4 = 20$
@step Add: $8 + 20 = 28$
@answer $28$
:::

:::check mx-perfect-cubes 3
:::

## Cubes and volume

A cube with edges $s$ units long is built from $s \times s \times s$ unit cubes, so its volume is $V = s^3$. This means that the edge of a cube is the cube root of its volume: $s = \sqrt[3]{V}$.

:::example From volume to edge
A cube-shaped box has a volume of $216$ cm³. How long is each edge?
@step $s = \sqrt[3]{216}$
@step $6 \times 6 \times 6 = 216$, so $\sqrt[3]{216} = 6$
@answer Each edge is $6$ cm long.
:::

Once we know the edge, we can find anything else about the cube. One face is a square with area $s^2$, and a cube has $12$ edges, so a wire frame along all of them has a length of $12s$.

:::summary
- $n^3 = n \times n \times n$. The results for whole numbers are the perfect cubes: $1, 8, 27, 64, 125, \ldots$
- $\sqrt[3]{a}$ is the number whose cube is $a$. Cubing and taking a cube root undo each other.
- Work out powers and roots before the other operations.
- A cube with edge $s$ has volume $s^3$, so the edge is the cube root of the volume.
:::
