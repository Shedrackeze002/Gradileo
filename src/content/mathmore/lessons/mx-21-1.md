Geometry has its own shorthand. A few symbols above two letters tell us whether we mean a line that never ends, a ray, or a segment with a length we can measure, and a few marks on a diagram tell us which lines are parallel or perpendicular.

## Points, lines, rays and segments

:::definition Point, line, ray and line segment
A **point** is an exact position, named with a capital letter. A **line** is a straight path that goes on forever in both directions. A **ray** is the part of a line that starts at one **endpoint** and goes on forever in one direction. A **line segment** is the part of a line between two endpoints, so it has a length.
:::

Each object is named with two of its points and a symbol drawn above them.

:::mistake The order of the letters in a ray
For a line or a segment the order does not matter: $\overline{AB}$ and $\overline{BA}$ are the same segment. For a ray it does. The first letter is always the endpoint, so $\overrightarrow{AB}$ starts at $A$ and $\overrightarrow{BA}$ starts at $B$. They are different rays.
:::

## Angles

:::definition Angle and vertex
An **angle** is formed by two rays, lines or segments that meet at a common point, called the **vertex**. We name an angle with three letters and the symbol $\angle$, with the vertex in the middle: $\angle ABC$ has its vertex at $B$. Reading the letters in the other order names the same angle, so $\angle ABC = \angle CBA$.
:::

## Parallel, perpendicular and equal

:::definition Parallel and perpendicular
**Parallel** lines are lines in the same plane that never meet. We write $\overleftrightarrow{AB} \parallel \overleftrightarrow{CD}$. **Perpendicular** lines meet at a right angle, $90^\circ$. We write $\overleftrightarrow{AB} \perp \overleftrightarrow{CD}$.
:::

Diagrams show these relationships with marks. Parallel lines carry the same number of arrowheads, a small square at a corner marks a right angle, segments of equal length carry the same number of hash marks, and equal angles carry the same number of arcs.

## Angles where lines intersect

When two lines intersect they form four angles. Angles that are across the intersection from each other are **opposite angles** (also called vertically opposite angles), and they are always equal. Angles that are next to each other along a straight line are **adjacent angles on a line**, and they are supplementary: they add to $180^\circ$.

:::example Angles at an intersection
Lines $AB$ and $CD$ intersect at $E$, and $\angle AEC = 65^\circ$. Find $\angle BED$ and $\angle AED$.
@step $\angle BED$ is opposite $\angle AEC$, so $\angle BED = 65^\circ$.
@step $\angle AED$ and $\angle AEC$ sit side by side on line $CD$, so they add to $180^\circ$: $\angle AED = 180^\circ - 65^\circ = 115^\circ$.
@answer $\angle BED = 65^\circ$ and $\angle AED = 115^\circ$
:::

A **transversal** is a line that crosses two other lines. It makes four angles at each crossing, and the angles in matching positions at the two crossings are called **corresponding angles**. When the two lines are parallel, corresponding angles are equal. This works in reverse as well: if a pair of corresponding angles are equal, the lines are parallel.

:::tip Testing for parallel lines
We cannot check that two lines never meet by following them forever. We can measure a pair of corresponding angles instead. Equal angles mean parallel lines, and unequal angles mean the lines will meet somewhere.
:::

:::summary
- $\overleftrightarrow{AB}$ is a line (no endpoints), $\overrightarrow{AB}$ is a ray (endpoint $A$) and $\overline{AB}$ is a line segment (two endpoints).
- $\angle ABC$ has its vertex at the middle letter, $B$.
- $\parallel$ means parallel and $\perp$ means perpendicular. Arrowheads, squares, hash marks and arcs show these on diagrams.
- Opposite angles at an intersection are equal, and adjacent angles on a line add to $180^\circ$.
- A transversal makes equal corresponding angles exactly when the lines it crosses are parallel.
:::
