A school wants to know what its 1200 students think of the new timetable, but asking everyone takes too long. Asking a sample is quicker, and the way the sample is chosen decides whether its answers can be trusted to speak for the whole school.

## Populations and samples

:::definition Sample
A **population** is the whole set of people or items we want to know about. A **sample** is the part of the population that we actually collect data from. A **sampling frame** is a list of the members of the population that a sample can be chosen from.
:::

The five methods below differ in two ways: whether the choice is **random**, and whether the population is first split into groups.

| Method | How it works |
| --- | --- |
| Simple random | Number every member, and choose numbers at random. |
| Systematic | Choose every $k$th member of an ordered list, from a random start. |
| Stratified | Split into groups (strata), and choose at random from each in proportion to its size. |
| Quota | An interviewer chooses people who fit each group until each group’s quota is filled. |
| Opportunity | Use whoever is available at the time, such as the first people to pass. |

Quota and opportunity sampling are **not random**, so they are quick and cheap but may be biased. The random methods need a sampling frame.

:::mistake Quota sampling is not stratified sampling
Both split the population into groups, but in stratified sampling the members of each group are chosen **at random** from a list. In quota sampling the interviewer chooses whoever fits, so there is no list and no randomness.
:::

## How many from each group?

In a stratified sample, each group gets the same fraction of its members.

:::example A stratified sample
A sixth form has $120$ students in Year 12 and $80$ in Year 13. A stratified sample of $25$ is taken. How many come from each year?
@step The population is $120 + 80 = 200$.
@step Year 12: $\dfrac{120}{200} \times 25 = 15$. Year 13: $\dfrac{80}{200} \times 25 = 10$.
@answer $15$ from Year 12 and $10$ from Year 13.
:::

For a systematic sample of $n$ from a list of $N$, the **interval** is $k = \dfrac{N}{n}$: choose a random start from the first $k$, then every $k$th member after it.

:::tip Check the total
The numbers from each group of a stratified or quota sample should add up to the sample size. If rounding makes them miss, adjust the group closest to rounding the other way.
:::

:::summary
- A sample is part of a population; a sampling frame is a list of the population.
- Simple random, systematic and stratified sampling are random and need a sampling frame.
- Quota and opportunity sampling are not random: quick and cheap, but they can be biased.
- In a stratified sample, each group’s share is $\dfrac{\text{group size}}{\text{population}} \times$ sample size.
- A systematic sample of $n$ from $N$ uses the interval $\dfrac{N}{n}$.
:::
