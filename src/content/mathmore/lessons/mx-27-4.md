Vectors can do geometry without any angles or lengths. With position vectors we can find the point that splits a line in a given ratio, and we can test whether three points lie in one straight line.

## Position vectors

:::definition Position vector
The **position vector** of a point $A$ is the vector $\overrightarrow{OA}$ from the origin $O$ to $A$. It is usually written as $\mathbf{a}$. The vector from $A$ to $B$ is then $\overrightarrow{AB} = \mathbf{b} - \mathbf{a}$.
:::

So the point $A(2, 5)$ has position vector $\mathbf{a} = \begin{pmatrix} 2 \\ 5 \end{pmatrix}$. To travel from $A$ to $B$ we can go back to the origin and out again, which is why $\overrightarrow{AB} = -\mathbf{a} + \mathbf{b}$.

## The midpoint

The midpoint $M$ of $AB$ is halfway along, so $\overrightarrow{OM} = \mathbf{a} + \dfrac{1}{2}(\mathbf{b} - \mathbf{a}) = \dfrac{1}{2}(\mathbf{a} + \mathbf{b})$. The position vector of the midpoint is the average of the two position vectors.

:::example Midpoint
$\mathbf{a} = \begin{pmatrix} 2 \\ 5 \end{pmatrix}$ and $\mathbf{b} = \begin{pmatrix} 8 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix}$. Find the position vector of the midpoint $M$ of $AB$.
@step $\overrightarrow{OM} = \dfrac{1}{2}(\mathbf{a} + \mathbf{b}) = \dfrac{1}{2}\begin{pmatrix} 10 \\ 6 \end{pmatrix}$
@answer $\begin{pmatrix} 5 \\ 3 \end{pmatrix}$
:::

## The ratio theorem

:::definition Ratio theorem
The **ratio theorem** says that if $P$ divides $AB$ in the ratio $AP : PB = m : n$, then
$$\overrightarrow{OP} = \frac{n\mathbf{a} + m\mathbf{b}}{m + n}$$
:::

The reason is that $P$ is $\dfrac{m}{m + n}$ of the way from $A$ to $B$, so $\overrightarrow{OP} = \mathbf{a} + \dfrac{m}{m + n}(\mathbf{b} - \mathbf{a})$, and collecting the terms gives the formula.

:::example Dividing in a ratio
$\mathbf{a} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \end{pmatrix}$ and $\mathbf{b} = \begin{pmatrix} 11 \\ 7 \end{pmatrix}$. $P$ divides $AB$ in the ratio $AP : PB = 2 : 3$. Find the position vector of $P$.
@step $\overrightarrow{OP} = \dfrac{3\mathbf{a} + 2\mathbf{b}}{2 + 3}$
@step $= \dfrac{1}{5}\left[\begin{pmatrix} 3 \\ 6 \end{pmatrix} + \begin{pmatrix} 22 \\ 14 \end{pmatrix}\right] = \dfrac{1}{5}\begin{pmatrix} 25 \\ 20 \end{pmatrix}$
@answer $\begin{pmatrix} 5 \\ 4 \end{pmatrix}$
:::

:::mistake The numbers cross over
In the formula, $\mathbf{a}$ is multiplied by $n$ and $\mathbf{b}$ by $m$. When $AP : PB = 2 : 3$, the point $P$ is nearer to $A$, so $\mathbf{a}$ must get the larger weight, which is $3$.
:::

:::check mx-ratio-theorem 2
:::

The same idea works backwards. If we know $A$ and $P$, we find $B$ by continuing in the same direction: $\overrightarrow{AB} = \dfrac{m + n}{m}\overrightarrow{AP}$.

:::example Finding the far end
$A$ has position vector $\begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 3 \end{pmatrix}$, and $P$, with position vector $\begin{pmatrix} 5 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix}$, divides $AB$ in the ratio $2 : 1$. Find the position vector of $B$.
@step $\overrightarrow{AP} = \begin{pmatrix} 4 \\ -2 \end{pmatrix}$
@step $\overrightarrow{AB} = \dfrac{3}{2}\overrightarrow{AP} = \begin{pmatrix} 6 \\ -3 \end{pmatrix}$
@step $\mathbf{b} = \mathbf{a} + \overrightarrow{AB} = \begin{pmatrix} 7 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix}$
@answer $\begin{pmatrix} 7 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix}$
:::

:::check mx-ratio-theorem 3
:::

## Collinear points

:::definition Collinear
Points are **collinear** when they all lie on one straight line. $A$, $B$ and $C$ are collinear when $\overrightarrow{AC}$ is a scalar multiple of $\overrightarrow{AB}$, that is $\overrightarrow{AC} = k\overrightarrow{AB}$ for some number $k$.
:::

A scalar multiple points in the same direction (or exactly the opposite one), so the two vectors are parallel. They also share the point $A$, and two parallel lines through one point must be the same line.

:::example Testing three points
Are $A(1, 2)$, $B(3, 6)$ and $C(6, 12)$ collinear?
@step $\overrightarrow{AB} = \begin{pmatrix} 2 \\ 4 \end{pmatrix}$ and $\overrightarrow{AC} = \begin{pmatrix} 5 \\ 10 \end{pmatrix}$
@step $\overrightarrow{AC} = \dfrac{5}{2}\overrightarrow{AB}$, since $\dfrac{5}{2} \times 2 = 5$ and $\dfrac{5}{2} \times 4 = 10$.
@answer Yes, they are collinear.
:::

:::check mx-collinear-points 1
:::

:::example Finding an unknown coordinate
$A(1, 3)$, $B(4, 9)$ and $C(7, k)$ are collinear. Find $k$.
@step $\overrightarrow{AB} = \begin{pmatrix} 3 \\ 6 \end{pmatrix}$ and $\overrightarrow{AC} = \begin{pmatrix} 6 \\ k - 3 \end{pmatrix}$
@step The top entries give the multiple: $6 = 2 \times 3$.
@step So $k - 3 = 2 \times 6 = 12$.
@answer $k = 15$
:::

:::check mx-collinear-points 2
:::

When no coordinates are given, the ratio theorem writes a point on $AB$ in terms of $\mathbf{a}$ and $\mathbf{b}$. For $AP : PB = 1 : 3$ we get $\overrightarrow{OP} = \dfrac{3\mathbf{a} + \mathbf{b}}{4} = \dfrac{3}{4}\mathbf{a} + \dfrac{1}{4}\mathbf{b}$. The two coefficients always add up to $1$, which is a quick check that $P$ really is on the line $AB$.

:::check mx-collinear-points 3
:::

:::summary
- $\overrightarrow{AB} = \mathbf{b} - \mathbf{a}$, and the midpoint of $AB$ has position vector $\dfrac{1}{2}(\mathbf{a} + \mathbf{b})$.
- Ratio theorem: if $AP : PB = m : n$, then $\overrightarrow{OP} = \dfrac{n\mathbf{a} + m\mathbf{b}}{m + n}$.
- $A$, $B$ and $C$ are collinear when $\overrightarrow{AC} = k\overrightarrow{AB}$.
:::
