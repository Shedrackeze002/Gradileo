A company that needs money to grow can sell small parts of itself to the public. People who buy those parts own a piece of the company and receive a piece of its profit.

## Shares and dividends

:::definition Share and dividend
A **share** is one of the equal parts into which the ownership of a company is divided. Each share has a **nominal value** (also called its par or face value), which is fixed when the share is first issued, and a **market price**, which is what it is bought and sold for today. A **dividend** is the part of the company's profit paid to shareholders, usually stated as a percent of the nominal value.
:::

The key point is that the dividend is worked out on the nominal value, even though the shares were bought at the market price.

:::example A dividend
A shareholder owns 800 shares with a nominal value of \$2 each. The company declares a dividend of $9\%$. Find the dividend.
@step Nominal value of the holding: $800 \times 2 = 1600$
@step Dividend: $9\% \times 1600 = 144$
@answer \$144
:::

## Buying at the market price

The number of shares that a sum of money buys depends on the market price.

:::example Investing a sum
An investor puts \$6,000 into shares with a nominal value of \$5 and a market price of \$7.50. The dividend is $12\%$. Find the number of shares and the dividend.
@step Number of shares: $\dfrac{6000}{7.5} = 800$
@step Nominal value: $800 \times 5 = 4000$
@step Dividend: $12\% \times 4000 = 480$
@answer 800 shares, and a dividend of \$480
:::

:::mistake The dividend is not a percent of the money invested
In the example, $12\%$ of the \$6,000 invested would be \$720, which is wrong. The $12\%$ applies to the nominal value, \$4,000.
:::

:::

## Profit on a sale

Shares bought at one price and sold at a higher price make a profit. A broker who arranges each deal charges a fee, called brokerage, as a percent of the value of the shares. The fee is added to the cost when buying and taken off the money received when selling.

:::example Buying and selling
500 shares are bought at \$4 each and sold at \$5 each. The broker charges $1\%$ on each deal. Find the profit.
@step Cost: $500 \times 4 \times 1.01 = 2020$
@step Money from the sale: $500 \times 5 \times 0.99 = 2475$
@step Profit: $2475 - 2020 = 455$
@answer \$455
:::

## Yield

:::definition Yield
The **yield** of a share is its yearly dividend as a percent of its market price. It shows the return an investor really gets on the money paid for the share.
:::

:::example The yield on a share
A share has a nominal value of \$5 and a market price of \$13.50, and pays a dividend of $12\%$. Find the yield.
@step Dividend on one share: $12\% \times 5 = 0.60$
@step Yield: $\dfrac{0.60}{13.50} \times 100\%$
@answer $4.44\%$
:::

The dividend looks like $12\%$, but an investor who pays \$13.50 for the share earns only $4.44\%$ on that money.

## Stocks

:::definition Stock
**Stock** is money lent to a government or a company in return for a fixed rate of interest. It is traded in units of \$100. A stock described as "$5\%$ at 125" pays \$5 of interest each year on every \$100 of stock, and \$100 of stock costs \$125 to buy.
:::

:::example Income from stock
An investor puts \$5,000 into a $5\%$ stock at 125. Find the yearly income.
@step Units of \$100 stock bought: $\dfrac{5000}{125} = 40$
@step Income: $40 \times 5 = 200$
@answer \$200 a year
:::

:::check mx-share-yield 2
:::

The yield of a stock is its interest on \$100 of stock as a percent of what that \$100 of stock costs. It lets us compare stocks with different rates and prices.

:::example Which stock is better
Compare a $5\%$ stock at 125 with a $4\%$ stock at 90.
@step First stock: $\dfrac{5}{125} \times 100\% = 4\%$
@step Second stock: $\dfrac{4}{90} \times 100\% \approx 4.44\%$
@answer The $4\%$ stock at 90 gives the better return.
:::

:::tip A low price can beat a high rate
A stock that costs less than \$100 gives a yield above its stated rate, and one that costs more than \$100 gives a yield below it.
:::

:::check mx-share-yield 3
:::

:::summary
- A dividend is a percent of the nominal value of the shares, not of the money invested.
- Number of shares $=$ money invested $\div$ market price.
- Brokerage is added to the cost of buying and taken off the money from selling.
- Yield $= \dfrac{\text{yearly income}}{\text{price paid}} \times 100\%$, for a share or for \$100 of stock.
:::
