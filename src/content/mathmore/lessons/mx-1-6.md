Does a new fertilizer make plants grow taller? Do students who play music do better at mathematics? How the data are collected decides what a study can tell us, and above all whether it can show that one thing causes another.

## Three kinds of study

:::definition Survey, observational study and experiment
- A **sample survey** asks a sample of people questions, to learn about a population.
- An **observational study** records what happens, without changing anything; the subjects are already in their groups.
- An **experiment** gives a **treatment** to some subjects and not to others (the **control group**), and compares the results.
:::

:::example Naming the study
(a) A researcher records whether people who own dogs walk more than people who do not. (b) A researcher gives half of a group of volunteers, chosen at random, a step counter with reminders and the other half a plain step counter, and compares how far they walk.
@step (a) People already own a dog or not; the researcher only records what happens. An observational study.
@step (b) The researcher decides, at random, who gets the reminders. An experiment.
@answer (a) Observational study. (b) Experiment.
:::

## Cause and effect

In an observational study, the groups can differ in other ways. Dog owners might walk more because they are dog owners, or because people who like walking are more likely to get dogs. A hidden difference like this is a **confounding variable**.

:::definition Randomized experiment
In a **randomized experiment**, chance decides which subjects get the treatment. Then the groups are alike on average in every other way, so a clear difference in the results can be put down to the treatment. Only a randomized experiment can show cause and effect.
:::

:::mistake Association is not causation
An observational study can show that two things go together (an association). It cannot show that one causes the other, because a confounding variable might explain both.
:::

## Simulation

Results vary by chance. To judge whether a result is surprising, we can **simulate**: use a computer, coins or random numbers to repeat the situation many times as if a claim were true, and see how often a result like ours turns up.

:::example Is the result unusual?
A coin is claimed to be fair. The dot plot shows $27$ simulated runs of $20$ fair tosses. In a real test, the coin gave $17$ heads in $20$ tosses. Is that unusual for a fair coin?
@step In the simulation, no run had $17$ or more heads: $0$ of $27$ runs, $0\%$.
@step A result the simulation almost never gives is unusual if the claim is true.
@answer Yes. $17$ heads would be very unusual for a fair coin, so the result is evidence that the coin is not fair.
:::

:::tip What counts as unusual?
A common rule is that a result is unusual when it happens in less than $5\%$ of the simulated runs. A result inside the bulk of the simulated runs fits the claim.

:::summary
- A survey asks questions, an observational study records what happens, and an experiment gives a treatment and compares it with a control group.
- Only a randomized experiment can show cause and effect; an observational study can show an association, which a confounding variable might explain.
- A simulation repeats a situation many times as if a claim were true; a result it rarely gives is evidence against the claim.
:::
