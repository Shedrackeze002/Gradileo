When a curve is described by a parameter, such as time $t$, both $x$ and $y$ change with $t$. The chain rule turns their rates of change into the gradient of the curve. The same lesson also shows where the derivatives of $\sin x$ and $\cos x$ come from.

## Parametric differentiation

:::definition Gradient of a parametric curve
If $x$ and $y$ are both functions of a parameter $t$, then
$$\frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{dy/dt}{dx/dt}, \qquad \text{provided } \frac{dx}{dt} \ne 0.$$
:::

:::example A tangent to a parametric curve
A curve has parametric equations $x = t^2 + 1$, $y = t^3 - 3t$. Find the equation of the tangent at the point where $t = 2$.
@step $\dfrac{dx}{dt} = 2t = 4$ and $\dfrac{dy}{dt} = 3t^2 - 3 = 9$ at $t = 2$, so $\dfrac{dy}{dx} = \dfrac{9}{4}$.
@step At $t = 2$, $x = 5$ and $y = 8 - 6 = 2$.
@answer $y - 2 = \dfrac{9}{4}(x - 5)$, that is $y = \dfrac{9}{4}x - \dfrac{37}{4}$.
:::

:::mistake Divide the right way round
$\dfrac{dy}{dx}$ is $\dfrac{dy}{dt}$ divided by $\dfrac{dx}{dt}$: the $y$ rate goes on top. Writing $\dfrac{dx/dt}{dy/dt}$ gives $\dfrac{dx}{dy}$, the reciprocal of the gradient.
:::

## Sin x and cos x from first principles

The derivative of $f(x)$ is the limit of the chord gradient $\dfrac{f(x + h) - f(x)}{h}$ as $h \to 0$. For $f(x) = \sin x$, expand with the compound angle formula:
$$\frac{\sin(x + h) - \sin x}{h} = \sin x\left(\frac{\cos h - 1}{h}\right) + \cos x\left(\frac{\sin h}{h}\right).$$
For $x$ in radians, $\dfrac{\sin h}{h} \to 1$ and $\dfrac{\cos h - 1}{h} \to 0$ as $h \to 0$ (the small-angle approximations show why), so $\dfrac{d}{dx}\sin x = \cos x$. In the same way, $\dfrac{d}{dx}\cos x = -\sin x$.

:::summary
- For a parametric curve, $\dfrac{dy}{dx} = \dfrac{dy/dt}{dx/dt}$.
- Find the point by putting the value of $t$ into both equations, then use $y - y_1 = m(x - x_1)$.
- From first principles, $\dfrac{d}{dx}\sin x = \cos x$ and $\dfrac{d}{dx}\cos x = -\sin x$, using $\dfrac{\sin h}{h} \to 1$ and $\dfrac{\cos h - 1}{h} \to 0$.
:::
