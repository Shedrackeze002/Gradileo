Will it snow on the long drive north? Is this box of spark plugs likely to have a dud? Is a raffle ticket worth buying? **Probability** puts a number on how likely something is, and **odds** say the same thing in a different way. Forecasters, quality inspectors, insurers and raffle organizers use both every day.

## Probability as a fraction, a decimal and a percent

:::definition Probability
When all the outcomes are equally likely, the **probability** of an event is
$$P(\text{event}) = \dfrac{\text{number of favourable outcomes}}{\text{total number of outcomes}}$$
A probability is between 0 (impossible) and 1 (certain). It can be written as a fraction, a decimal or a percent.
:::

:::example Spark plugs
A box of 40 spark plugs delivered to a repair shop contains 6 defective plugs. A mechanic takes one plug at random. What is the probability that it is defective?
@step Favourable outcomes: 6. Total outcomes: 40.
@step Fraction: $P = \dfrac{6}{40} = \dfrac{3}{20}$
@step Decimal: $6 \div 40 = 0.15$
@step Percent: $0.15 \times 100 = 15\%$
@answer The probability is $\dfrac{3}{20}$, or 0.15, or 15%.
:::

:::tip The complement
The probability that an event does **not** happen is $1 - P(\text{event})$. The probability that the plug is fine is $1 - 0.15 = 0.85$, or 85%.
:::

## Odds in favour and odds against

:::definition Odds
**Odds** compare outcomes with each other instead of with the total.

**Odds in favour** = favourable outcomes : unfavourable outcomes

**Odds against** = unfavourable outcomes : favourable outcomes

Odds are written as a ratio in lowest terms, such as $2 : 3$.
:::

:::example A raffle
The 4-H beef club in town sold 250 raffle tickets for a side of beef. Zainab bought 4 of them. What are the odds in favour of Zainab winning, and the odds against?
@step Favourable: 4. Unfavourable: $250 - 4 = 246$.
@step Odds in favour: $4 : 246$. Divide both by 2: $2 : 123$.
@step Odds against: $123 : 2$.
@answer The odds in favour are 2 : 123 and the odds against are 123 : 2. (The probability of winning is $\dfrac{4}{250} = \dfrac{2}{125}$, or 1.6%.)
:::

:::mistake Odds are not probability
$4 : 250$ compares the favourable outcomes with the **total**, so it is the probability written as a ratio, not the odds. Odds compare favourable with **unfavourable**: $4 : 246$.
:::

## Converting between odds and probability

The two numbers in the odds add up to the total number of outcomes.

- **Odds to probability:** odds in favour of $a : b$ give $P = \dfrac{a}{a + b}$.
- **Probability to odds:** a probability of $\dfrac{a}{n}$ gives odds in favour of $a : (n - a)$.

:::example Odds to probability
The odds against a curling team from a northern town winning a bonspiel are 7 : 3. What is the probability that the team wins?
@step Odds against 7 : 3 mean 7 unfavourable outcomes for every 3 favourable ones.
@step Total: $7 + 3 = 10$
@answer $P(\text{win}) = \dfrac{3}{10}$, or 30%.
:::

:::example Probability to odds
The forecast for Pinewood gives a 30% probability of precipitation tomorrow. What are the odds in favour of precipitation?
@step 30% means 30 out of 100, so $100 - 30 = 70$ are unfavourable.
@step Odds in favour: $30 : 70$. Divide both by 10: $3 : 7$.
@answer The odds in favour of precipitation are 3 : 7 (and the odds against are 7 : 3).
:::

## Expected outcomes and judging claims

A probability tells you what to expect over many trials:
$$\text{expected number} = \text{probability} \times \text{number of trials}$$

:::example Welds to redo
From past records, 3% of the welds made by apprentices at a fabrication shop need to be redone. How many of the next 800 welds would you expect to need redoing?
@step $3\% = 0.03$
@step $0.03 \times 800 = 24$
@answer You would expect about 24 welds to need redoing. The actual number will be close to 24, but probably not exactly 24.
:::

Probability describes the long run, not what happens next time. Use it to check claims:

- **“Tails is due.”** A coin that landed heads 6 times in a row still has a $\dfrac{1}{2}$ chance of tails. Each flip is independent.
- **“The forecast was wrong.”** A 70% chance of rain means rain on about 7 of every 10 days with that forecast. A dry day does not prove the forecast wrong.
- **“There are two outcomes, so it is 50–50.”** Only if the outcomes are equally likely. A midwinter high above 25°C in a cold northern town is not a 50% chance.
- **“Exactly 400 will fail.”** A probability from a small sample is only a rough estimate, and an expected number is never guaranteed.

:::summary
- $P(\text{event}) = \dfrac{\text{favourable}}{\text{total}}$, from 0 to 1, as a fraction, decimal or percent.
- Odds in favour = favourable : unfavourable; odds against = unfavourable : favourable. Write odds in lowest terms.
- Odds in favour $a : b$ give $P = \dfrac{a}{a + b}$.
- Expected number = probability × number of trials.
- Probability describes the long run: past results do not change independent events, and outcomes are not always equally likely.
:::
