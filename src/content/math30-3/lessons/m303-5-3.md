A graph or an equation lets you estimate values you never measured: the cost of a 3-hour rental when you only have bills for 2, 4 and 6 hours, or when a tank will run dry. Estimating inside your data is usually safe. Going far beyond it needs good judgment.

## Interpolation and extrapolation

:::definition Interpolation and extrapolation
**Interpolation** estimates a value **between** the data you have. **Extrapolation** estimates a value **beyond** the data, by assuming the trend keeps going.
:::

Emeka has bills for renting a skid steer for $2$, $4$ and $6$ hours. The solid part of the line covers his data; the dashed parts are extrapolation.

:::example Reading between the points
Use the graph to estimate the cost of a $3$-hour rental.
@step $3$ hours is between $2$ and $6$ hours, so this is interpolation.
@step Go up from $3$ on the time axis to the line, then across: \$200.
@step Check with the rate: the cost rises $\frac{240 - 160}{4 - 2} = 40$ dollars per hour, and $160 + 40 = 200$.
@answer A $3$-hour rental costs about \$200.
:::

## Extrapolating with the rate or the equation

To go beyond the data, keep adding the rate of change, or use the equation.

:::example A bigger concrete order
A contractor has these concrete prices. Predict the cost of $9\ \text{m}^3$.

| Concrete (m³) | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 | 6 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Cost (\$) | 470 | 645 | 820 | 995 | 1170 |
@step The cost goes up \$175 for each cubic metre.
@step From $6$ to $9$ is $3$ more cubic metres: $1170 + 3 \times 175 = 1695$.
@step Check with the equation $C = 175v + 120$: $175(9) + 120 = 1695$.
@answer If the pricing stays the same, $9\ \text{m}^3$ costs \$1,695. This is extrapolation.
:::

You can also extrapolate to find **when** something happens. A tractor starts with $400\ \text{L}$ and burns $18\ \text{L}$ an hour, so $V = 400 - 18h$. The tank is empty when $0 = 400 - 18h$, so $h = 400 \div 18 \approx 22.2$ hours.

:::tip Round to fit the situation
With a \$300 budget and a rental of $C = 45h + 60$, $h = 240 \div 45 \approx 5.3$, so you can afford only $5$ whole hours: round **down**. To earn at least \$250 at $P = 1.50n + 90$, $n \approx 106.7$, so you need $107$ packages: round **up**.
:::

:::check m303-interpolate 2
:::

## Lines of best fit

Real data rarely sit exactly on a line. A **line of best fit** passes through the middle of the points, and its equation is used to estimate.

:::figure

:::

:::example Tomato plants in a greenhouse
A greenhouse measured its tomato plants for $8$ weeks. The line of best fit is $h = 4.6w + 8.2$, where $h$ is the height in centimetres after $w$ weeks. Estimate the height at $5.5$ weeks.
@step $h = 4.6(5.5) + 8.2 = 25.3 + 8.2 = 33.5$
@step $5.5$ weeks is inside the data ($1$ to $8$ weeks), so this is interpolation.
@answer The plants are about $33.5\ \text{cm}$ tall at $5.5$ weeks.
:::

## When is extrapolation reasonable?

Extrapolation assumes nothing changes. Ask two questions:

- **Does the situation stay the same?** A rental shop's prices stay the same for a longer job. A calf's growth slows as it matures.
- **Is the answer even possible?** A tank cannot hold negative fuel, and nobody is $4\ \text{m}$ tall.

:::example A growing calf
A calf gained about $6\ \text{kg}$ a week from a birth mass of $40\ \text{kg}$ in its first $10$ weeks. Using $M = 6w + 40$ to predict its mass at $10$ years ($520$ weeks) gives $6(520) + 40 = 3160\ \text{kg}$. Is this reasonable?
@step $520$ weeks is far beyond the $10$ weeks of data.
@step Calves grow fast at first, then slower; a full-grown cow is a fraction of $3160\ \text{kg}$.
@answer No. The trend from the first $10$ weeks does not continue for years, so the prediction is not reasonable.
:::

:::mistake "Never extrapolate"
Extrapolation is not always wrong. Predicting a $5$-hour plumbing bill from bills of $1$ to $4$ hours is reasonable, because the pricing rule stays the same. The danger grows the further you go and the more the situation can change.
:::

:::check m303-interpolate 3
:::

:::summary
- Interpolation estimates **between** data values; extrapolation estimates **beyond** them.
- Use a graph, the rate of change, or the equation. For "when", set $y$ to the target and solve.
- Round to fit the situation: down for a budget, up to reach a goal.
- A line of best fit summarizes real data; use its equation to estimate.
- Extrapolation is reasonable only if the situation stays the same and the answer is possible.
:::
