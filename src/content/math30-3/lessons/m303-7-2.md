A quality inspector checks three parts, not one. A roofer needs two dry days in a row. A raffle draws two prizes from the same drum. Each of these asks about two or more events at once. The key questions are: does one event change the chances of the other, and can both happen together?

## Mutually exclusive events: “or”

:::definition Mutually exclusive events
Two events are **mutually exclusive** if they cannot happen at the same time. For mutually exclusive events,
$$P(A \text{ or } B) = P(A) + P(B)$$
:::

One washer cannot be both zinc and brass, and one die roll cannot be both a 2 and a 5. Those pairs are mutually exclusive.

:::example A parts bin
A parts bin in a repair shop holds 18 zinc washers, 12 stainless steel washers, 10 brass washers and 20 nylon washers. An apprentice takes one washer without looking. What is the probability that it is stainless steel or brass?
@step Total: $18 + 12 + 10 + 20 = 60$
@step $P(\text{stainless}) = \dfrac{12}{60}$ and $P(\text{brass}) = \dfrac{10}{60}$
@step The events are mutually exclusive, so add: $\dfrac{12}{60} + \dfrac{10}{60} = \dfrac{22}{60} = \dfrac{11}{30}$
@answer The probability is $\dfrac{11}{30}$.
:::

## Independent events: “and”

:::definition Independent events
Two events are **independent** if one happening does not change the probability of the other. For independent events,
$$P(A \text{ and } B) = P(A) \times P(B)$$
:::

Flipping a coin and spinning a spinner are independent. So are the weather on two days, if a question tells you to assume it.

:::example A fair game
At a summer fair, Wyatt spins the spinner above and flips a coin. He wins a prize for spinning a 4 and flipping heads. What is the probability that he wins?
@step $P(\text{spin a 4}) = \dfrac{1}{4}$ and $P(\text{heads}) = \dfrac{1}{2}$
@step The events are independent, so multiply: $\dfrac{1}{4} \times \dfrac{1}{2} = \dfrac{1}{8}$
@answer The probability that Wyatt wins is $\dfrac{1}{8}$.
:::

:::example Two days of rain
The forecast for Riverton gives a 40% probability of rain on Saturday and a 30% probability on Sunday. Assume the days are independent. What is the probability of rain on both days?
@step $0.40 \times 0.30 = 0.12$
@answer The probability of rain on both days is 12%.
:::

:::mistake Adding for “and”
For “and”, multiply. Adding gives $40\% + 30\% = 70\%$, which is far too high: rain on **both** days must be less likely than rain on either day alone.
:::

## Dependent events: without replacement

:::definition Dependent events
Two events are **dependent** if the first one changes the probability of the second. Picking items one after another **without replacement** (without putting the first one back) gives dependent events:
$$P(A \text{ and } B) = P(A) \times P(B \text{ after } A)$$
:::

:::example Blown fuses
A box of 20 fuses in an electrician’s van contains 3 blown fuses. The electrician takes two fuses, one after the other, without putting the first back. What is the probability that both are blown?
@step First fuse: $P = \dfrac{3}{20}$
@step After one blown fuse is taken, 2 of the 19 fuses left are blown: $P = \dfrac{2}{19}$
@step Multiply: $\dfrac{3}{20} \times \dfrac{2}{19} = \dfrac{6}{380} = \dfrac{3}{190}$
@answer The probability that both fuses are blown is $\dfrac{3}{190}$.
:::

If the electrician put the first fuse back, the picks would be independent and the probability would be $\dfrac{3}{20} \times \dfrac{3}{20} = \dfrac{9}{400}$.

:::mistake Not changing the second fraction
Without replacement, the second fraction has one fewer in the total **and** one fewer of the kind you want: $\dfrac{2}{19}$, not $\dfrac{3}{20}$ or $\dfrac{3}{19}$.
:::

## Putting it together: “at least one” and “exactly one”

Many real questions combine the rules.

- **At least one** is the opposite of **none**: $P(\text{at least one}) = 1 - P(\text{none})$.
- **Exactly one** can happen in more than one way. Find each way (multiply), then add the ways (they are mutually exclusive).

:::example Quality control
At a manufacturing plant, 5% of pipe fittings are defective. An inspector picks 3 fittings at random from a large batch. Assume they are independent. What is the probability that at least one is defective, to the nearest tenth of a percent?
@step One fitting is good: $1 - 0.05 = 0.95$
@step None defective: $0.95 \times 0.95 \times 0.95 = 0.857375$
@step At least one defective: $1 - 0.857375 = 0.142625$
@answer The probability is about 14.3%.
:::

:::example Rain on exactly one day
A roofing crew in Riverton is using the same forecast (40% Saturday, 30% Sunday). What is the probability of rain on exactly one of the two days?
@step Rain Saturday only: $0.40 \times 0.70 = 0.28$
@step Rain Sunday only: $0.60 \times 0.30 = 0.18$
@step These ways cannot both happen, so add: $0.28 + 0.18 = 0.46$
@answer The probability of rain on exactly one day is 46%.
:::

:::tip Matching pairs
With 6 black and 4 grey socks in a drawer, the chance of pulling a matching pair is $P(\text{both black}) + P(\text{both grey}) = \dfrac{6}{10} \times \dfrac{5}{9} + \dfrac{4}{10} \times \dfrac{3}{9} = \dfrac{30}{90} + \dfrac{12}{90} = \dfrac{7}{15}$.
:::

:::

:::summary
- Mutually exclusive (cannot both happen): $P(A \text{ or } B) = P(A) + P(B)$.
- Independent (one does not affect the other): $P(A \text{ and } B) = P(A) \times P(B)$.
- Dependent (without replacement): $P(A \text{ and } B) = P(A) \times P(B \text{ after } A)$; change both the top and the bottom of the second fraction.
- At least one = 1 − none. Exactly one: multiply for each way, then add the ways.
:::
