Not every value in a data set counts the same. A final exam is worth more than a quiz, and 60 studs bought at one price matter more than 4 bought at another. At a skills competition, one judge’s odd score should not decide the winner. **Weighted means** and **trimmed means** handle both situations.

## Weighted means: course marks

:::definition Weighted mean
In a **weighted mean**, each value is multiplied by its **weight** (how much it counts), and the total is divided by the sum of the weights:
$$\text{weighted mean} = \dfrac{\text{sum of (weight} \times \text{value)}}{\text{sum of the weights}}$$
When the weights are percents that add to 100%, write each weight as a decimal, multiply and add. There is no need to divide.
:::

Priya is in a construction technology course. The table shows how her final mark is calculated.

:::figure
{"type":"table","headers":["Part of the course","Weight","Mark"],"rows":[["Assignments","20%","85%"],["Unit tests","30%","72%"],["Shop projects","20%","90%"],["Final exam","30%","64%"]]}
:::

:::example Priya’s final mark
Find Priya’s final mark.
@step Write each weight as a decimal and multiply: $0.20(85) = 17$, $0.30(72) = 21.6$, $0.20(90) = 18$, $0.30(64) = 19.2$
@step Add: $17 + 21.6 + 18 + 19.2 = 75.8$
@answer Priya’s final mark is 75.8%.
:::

:::mistake Using the plain mean
The plain mean of the four marks is $(85 + 72 + 90 + 64) \div 4 = 77.75\%$. That treats the assignments and the final exam as equal, but the exam counts more. The weighted mean, 75.8%, is the correct final mark.
:::

## Weighted means: quantities and prices

When you buy the same item at different prices, the weights are the **quantities**. The mean price is the total cost divided by the total number of items.

:::example A framer’s studs
Mateo, a framer, bought 60 studs at \$4.29 each and 40 studs at \$4.89 each. What was the mean price per stud?
@step Total cost: $60(4.29) + 40(4.89) = 257.40 + 195.60 = 453.00$
@step Total studs: $60 + 40 = 100$
@step Mean price: $453.00 \div 100 = 4.53$
@answer The mean price was \$4.53 per stud. (The mean of the two prices, \$4.59, is too high because more studs were bought at the lower price.)
:::

The same idea works when one part “counts twice”. If a final exam counts twice as much as each of 4 unit tests, give each test a weight of 1 and the exam a weight of 2, then divide by $4 + 2 = 6$.

## Trimmed means

:::definition Trimmed mean
A **trimmed mean** is the mean of the data after removing the same number of values from each end of the ordered list. A **10% trimmed mean** removes the lowest 10% and the highest 10% of the values.
:::

Trimming protects the mean from outliers at either end. Many competitions use it so that one generous or harsh judge cannot decide the result.

:::example Judges’ scores
At a regional skills competition, 6 judges scored Kiona’s weld out of 10: 7, 9, 8, 6, 10, 8. The highest and the lowest scores are dropped. What is her trimmed mean score?
@step In order: 6, 7, 8, 8, 9, 10
@step Drop the lowest (6) and the highest (10): 7, 8, 8, 9
@step Mean of the rest: $(7 + 8 + 8 + 9) \div 4 = 32 \div 4 = 8$
@answer Kiona’s trimmed mean score is 8 out of 10.
:::

:::example A 10% trimmed mean
A courier company in a large city recorded the delivery times, in minutes, of 10 parcels: 34, 41, 38, 45, 39, 112, 36, 40, 43, 37. Find the mean and the 10% trimmed mean, to the nearest tenth.
@step Mean of all 10: $465 \div 10 = 46.5$ minutes.
@step $10\%$ of 10 values is $0.10 \times 10 = 1$, so remove 1 value from each end.
@step In order: 34, 36, 37, 38, 39, 40, 41, 43, 45, 112. Remove 34 and 112.
@step Trimmed mean: $(36 + 37 + 38 + 39 + 40 + 41 + 43 + 45) \div 8 = 319 \div 8 \approx 39.9$
@answer The mean is 46.5 min and the 10% trimmed mean is about 39.9 min. The trimmed mean better describes a typical delivery, because the 112-minute parcel pulls the mean up.
:::

:::mistake Trimming only one end
Always remove the same number of values from **both** ends. Removing only the outlier is not a trimmed mean.
:::

## The mark you need on a final exam

A weighted mean can also be worked backwards. Let $x$ be the unknown mark, write the weighted mean, and solve.

:::example What does Sofia need?
In Sofia’s welding program, assignments count 25%, unit tests 35% and the final exam 40%. Sofia has 78% on assignments and 70% on unit tests. She needs a final mark of 75% to get into the next course. What does she need on the final exam?
@step Weighted mean with exam mark $x$: $0.25(78) + 0.35(70) + 0.40x = 75$
@step The marks so far contribute $19.5 + 24.5 = 44$
@step So $44 + 0.40x = 75$, which gives $0.40x = 31$
@step $x = 31 \div 0.40 = 77.5$
@answer Sofia needs at least 77.5% on the final exam.
:::

:::check m303-weighted-mean 3
:::

:::summary
- Weighted mean = sum of (weight × value) ÷ sum of the weights. With percent weights that add to 100%, just multiply by the decimal weights and add.
- For a mean price, the weights are the quantities: total cost ÷ total number of items.
- A trimmed mean removes the same number of values from each end of the ordered data, then finds the mean of the rest. A 10% trimmed mean removes 10% from each end.
- To find a needed mark, write the weighted mean with $x$ for the unknown mark and solve.
:::
