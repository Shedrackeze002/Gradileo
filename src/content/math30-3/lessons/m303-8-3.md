Running a food truck downtown, a lawn care business or a candle stall at a weekend farmers’ market can be a great way to earn a living. Whether it works depends on simple numbers: what it costs to run, what it brings in, and how many sales it takes before the business starts making money.

## Fixed costs and variable costs

:::definition Fixed and variable costs
A **fixed cost** stays the same each month (or season) no matter how much you sell: a truck payment, insurance, rent, a licence. A **variable cost** goes up with each item you make or sell: ingredients, packaging, materials, fuel to drive to each job.
:::

Ask: *if I sold twice as many, would this cost go up?* If yes, it is variable.

| Business | Fixed costs | Variable costs |
|---|---|---|
| Food truck | Truck loan, insurance, kitchen rent, permits | Ingredients, containers |
| Lawn care | Mower loan, liability insurance, phone | Gas for the mowers, trimmer line |
| Market crafts | Stall fee, tent, tools | Wax, wood, labels |
| Mobile car detailing | Van payment, insurance, website | Soap, wax, fuel to each customer |

## Revenue, total costs and profit

:::definition Revenue and profit
**Revenue** (sales) is the money that comes in: $\text{price} \times \text{number sold}$.

$$\text{total costs} = \text{fixed costs} + \text{variable cost per item} \times \text{number sold}$$
$$\text{profit} = \text{revenue} - \text{total costs}$$

A negative result is a **loss**.
:::

:::example A month for the food truck
Nia runs a food truck downtown that sells loaded perogy bowls. Her fixed costs are \$2,450 a month (truck loan \$1,250, insurance \$250, kitchen rent \$850, permits \$100). Each bowl costs \$4.20 in ingredients and packaging and sells for \$13. In a slow month she sold 420 bowls. Did she make a profit?
@step Revenue: $13 \times 420 = 5{,}460.00$
@step Variable costs: $4.20 \times 420 = 1{,}764.00$
@step Total costs: $2{,}450 + 1{,}764.00 = 4{,}214.00$
@step Profit: $5{,}460.00 - 4{,}214.00 = 1{,}246.00$
@answer Nia made a profit of \$1,246.00 for the month.
:::

:::mistake Forgetting the fixed costs
The bowls alone made $(13 - 4.20) \times 420 = 3{,}696$, but the truck loan, insurance, rent and permits still have to be paid. Profit is what is left after **all** the costs.
:::

## The break-even point

:::definition Break-even point
The **break-even point** is the number of items you must sell so that revenue equals total costs: no profit and no loss. Each item sold brings in $\text{price} - \text{variable cost}$ toward the fixed costs (its **contribution**), so
$$\text{break-even quantity} = \dfrac{\text{fixed costs}}{\text{price} - \text{variable cost per item}}$$
Round **up** to a whole number of items.
:::

:::example Nia’s break-even point
How many bowls must Nia sell each month to break even?
@step Contribution per bowl: $13 - 4.20 = 8.80$
@step $2{,}450 \div 8.80 = 278.4\ldots$
@step 278 bowls would bring in $278 \times 8.80 = 2{,}446.40$, which is \$3.60 short, so round up.
@answer Nia must sell 279 bowls a month to break even.
:::

:::mistake Rounding down
Rounding 278.4 to 278 leaves a small loss. A break-even quantity is always rounded **up**.
:::

The same idea gives a **break-even price** when you know how many you expect to sell: each item must cover its variable cost plus its share of the fixed costs. If Nia expects to sell only 350 bowls, her break-even price is $4.20 + 2{,}450 \div 350 = 4.20 + 7.00 = 11.20$, or \$11.20 a bowl.

## Is the business viable?

A business is **viable** if it can make enough profit to be worth the owner’s time and money. An **income statement** lists the sales and expenses for a period.

:::example Mobile car detailing
Marcus runs a mobile car detailing business. He wants a profit of at least \$5,000 a month to pay himself. Use his income statement for July. Find his profit and profit margin (profit as a percent of sales). Is the business viable?
@step Total expenses: $1{,}421 + 780 + 210 + 260 + 115 + 180 = 2{,}966.00$
@step Profit: $9{,}570.00 - 2{,}966.00 = 6{,}604.00$
@step Profit margin: $6{,}604.00 \div 9{,}570.00 \times 100 \approx 69.0\%$
@answer Marcus made a profit of \$6,604.00 (a margin of about 69.0%), which is more than his \$5,000 goal, so the business is viable.
:::

**Changing the price.** A higher price raises the contribution of each sale, so the break-even point drops, but customers may buy less. If Nia raises her price to \$14, her break-even point drops to $2{,}450 \div 9.80 = 250$ bowls. If her sales fall to 380 bowls, her profit is $(14 - 4.20) \times 380 - 2{,}450 = 1{,}274.00$, only \$28 more than at \$13. Always compare the profits before deciding.

:::tip Profit for a goal
To reach a target profit, cover the fixed costs **and** the profit: number needed = (fixed costs + target profit) ÷ contribution. For a \$3,000 profit, Nia needs $(2{,}450 + 3{,}000) \div 8.80 = 619.3\ldots$, so 620 bowls.
:::

:::summary
- Fixed costs stay the same; variable costs grow with each item sold.
- Revenue = price × number sold. Total costs = fixed costs + variable cost × number sold. Profit = revenue − total costs (negative means a loss).
- Break-even quantity = fixed costs ÷ (price − variable cost), always rounded up.
- Break-even price = variable cost + fixed costs ÷ expected number sold.
- Judge viability by comparing the profit with the owner’s goal; check how a price change affects both sales and profit.
:::
