Picture a part-time job at a hardware store in your town. You price new stock, put up sale signs and ring through customers at the till. Every one of those jobs uses percents: markups, discounts and sales tax.

## Percent increase and decrease

Prices change. A percent change tells you how big the change is **compared with the original price**.

:::definition Percent increase and percent decrease
$$\text{percent change} = \frac{\text{new} - \text{original}}{\text{original}} \times 100\%$$
If the price went up, it is a **percent increase**. If it went down, it is a **percent decrease** (use the size of the change).
:::

:::example A percent increase
A bag of cattle feed went from \$32.00 to \$36.80. Find the percent increase.
@step Change: $36.80 - 32.00 = 4.80$ dollars.
@step Divide by the **original** price: $\dfrac{4.80}{32.00} \times 100\% = 15\%$
@answer The price increased by $15\%$.
:::

:::example A percent decrease
Work boots were \$189.99 and are now \$149.99. Find the percent decrease, to the nearest tenth of a percent.
@step Change: $189.99 - 149.99 = 40$ dollars.
@step $\dfrac{40}{189.99} \times 100\% \approx 21.05\%$
@answer The price decreased by about $21.1\%$.
:::

:::mistake Dividing by the new price
The percent is always compared with the **original** (old) price. Dividing by the new price gives a different, wrong answer.
:::

## Markups and discounts

Stores buy products at one price and sell them at a higher one. When things do not sell, they put them on sale.

:::definition Markup and discount
A **markup** is the amount a store adds to its cost to set the selling price. A **discount** is the amount taken off the regular price.
$$\text{selling price} = \text{cost} + \text{markup} \qquad \text{sale price} = \text{regular price} - \text{discount}$$
:::

:::example Marking up a hammer
A hardware store pays \$18.40 for a hammer and marks it up $45\%$. What is the selling price?
@step Markup: $0.45 \times 18.40 = 8.28$
@step Selling price: $18.40 + 8.28 = 26.68$
@answer The hammer sells for \$26.68.
:::

:::example A sale price
A winter jacket is regularly \$149.99. It is on sale for $30\%$ off. Find the sale price.
@step Discount: $0.30 \times 149.99 = 44.997$
@step Sale price: $149.99 - 44.997 = 104.993$
@answer The sale price is \$104.99.
:::

:::tip The multiplier shortcut
$30\%$ off means you pay $70\%$, so multiply by $0.70$: $149.99 \times 0.70 = 104.993$. A $45\%$ markup means the price becomes $145\%$ of the cost, so multiply by $1.45$: $18.40 \times 1.45 = 26.68$.
:::

## Two discounts in a row

Sometimes a store takes an extra discount off a price that is already on sale. The second discount is taken from the **sale price**, which is smaller than the regular price.

:::example 20% off, then another 10% off
A snowblower is regularly \$650. It is marked $20\%$ off, and at the till the store takes another $10\%$ off the sale price. What is the final price?
@step After $20\%$ off: $650 \times 0.80 = 520$
@step After another $10\%$ off: $520 \times 0.90 = 468$
@answer The final price is \$468. Taking $30\%$ off would give \$455, so the two discounts together save $28\%$, not $30\%$.
:::

:::mistake Adding the percents
$20\%$ off and then $10\%$ off is **not** $30\%$ off. Apply the discounts one at a time.
:::

## Sales tax

Many places add a tax to the price of most things you buy. Depending on the country, it may be called a **sales tax**, **GST** (goods and services tax) or **VAT** (value-added tax), and the rate is different from place to place. Some places combine two taxes into one, sometimes called an **HST** (harmonized sales tax). In this course the tax is added to the price at the till, and every question tells you the rate to use. The examples below use a rate of $5\%$.

:::definition Sales tax
A **sales tax** is a percent of the price that is added when you buy something. With a $5\%$ tax:
$$\text{total} = \text{price} + \text{tax} = \text{price} \times 1.05$$
:::

:::example Adding sales tax
A toolbox costs \$84.99. What is the total with a $5\%$ sales tax?
@step Tax: $0.05 \times 84.99 = 4.2495$, about \$4.25
@step Total: $84.99 + 4.25 = 89.24$
@answer The total is \$89.24. (Check: $84.99 \times 1.05 = 89.2395$.)
:::

:::example Discount first, then tax
A jacket regularly costs \$240 and is $25\%$ off. What is the total with a $5\%$ sales tax?
@step Sale price: $240 \times 0.75 = 180$
@step Add the tax: $180 \times 1.05 = 189$
@answer The total is \$189.00. Tax is charged on the price you actually pay.
:::

:::example Working backward from a receipt
Sometimes you know the total and need the price **before** tax. A receipt shows a total of \$131.25, including a $5\%$ sales tax. What was the price before tax?
@step $\text{total} = \text{price} \times 1.05$, so $\text{price} = \text{total} \div 1.05$
@step $131.25 \div 1.05 = 125$
@answer The price before tax was \$125.00.
:::

:::mistake Subtracting 5% of the total
$131.25 - 0.05 \times 131.25 = 124.69$ is wrong. The tax is $5\%$ of the **price**, not $5\%$ of the total, so divide by $1.05$ instead.
:::

:::summary
- Percent change $=$ change $\div$ original $\times 100\%$. Always divide by the original.
- Markup is added to the cost; a discount is taken off the regular price.
- Use multipliers: $30\%$ off means $\times 0.70$; a $45\%$ markup means $\times 1.45$.
- Apply two discounts one at a time. $20\%$ then $10\%$ is $28\%$ off, not $30\%$.
- Total with a $5\%$ sales tax $=$ price $\times 1.05$. To work backward, divide by $1.05$.
- The same idea finds an original price: if a price rose $15\%$ to \$73.60, it was $73.60 \div 1.15 = 64$ dollars before.
:::
