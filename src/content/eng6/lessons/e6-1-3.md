Every story is told by someone. The voice telling the story is the **narrator**, and the position the narrator tells it from is the **point of view**. Point of view decides what the reader is allowed to know, so changing it can change the whole feel of a story.

## First person and third person

:::definition Point of view
- In **first person**, the narrator is a character in the story and uses *I*, *me* and *we*.
- In **third person**, the narrator is outside the story and uses *he*, *she* and *they* for every character.
  - **Third person limited**: the narrator tells us the thoughts and feelings of only one character.
  - **Third person omniscient**: the narrator knows and tells the thoughts of many or all characters. *Omniscient* means all-knowing.
:::

| Point of view | Example |
|---|---|
| First person | *I grabbed my bag and ran for the bus. My heart was pounding.* |
| Third person limited | *Amara grabbed her bag and ran for the bus. Her heart was pounding.* |
| Third person omniscient | *Amara ran for the bus, heart pounding. Inside, the driver sighed, wondering why children were always late.* |

:::example Naming the point of view
> The wolf licked his lips as he watched the cottage. Inside, Grandmother dozed in her chair, dreaming of her garden, and down the path, Red Riding Hood hummed a song, thinking only of the flowers.

@step The narrator uses *he* and *she*, so it is third person.
@step We are told what three characters are thinking: the wolf, Grandmother and Red Riding Hood.
@answer Third person omniscient.
:::

## What a narrator can know

A first-person narrator can tell us only what they see, hear, think and feel. They can *guess* what other people think, but they cannot know for certain. This is why many mysteries are told in first person: the reader finds the clues at the same time as the narrator.

:::mistake Watch for slips in point of view
If a story is in first person and the narrator says “Jo was secretly jealous of me”, ask: how could the narrator know that? Unless Jo said so, it is only a guess. In third person limited, the narrator should not suddenly tell us what another character is thinking in a place the main character is not.
:::

Sometimes a narrator cannot be trusted. An **unreliable narrator** tells the story in a way that does not match the facts, perhaps because they are proud, confused or hiding something.

:::example Spotting an unreliable narrator
> I never get angry. I only threw the plate because it slipped.

@step The narrator claims never to get angry.
@step In the same breath, they describe throwing a plate, which suggests anger.
@answer The narrator is unreliable: their words and actions do not match.
:::

:::summary
- **First person** uses *I* and *we*; the narrator is a character.
- **Third person limited** follows one character’s thoughts; **third person omniscient** knows everyone’s.
- A first-person narrator knows only their own experience.
- An **unreliable narrator** gives an account the reader should question.
:::
