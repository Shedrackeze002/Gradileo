Poets often say things that are not literally true in order to say something truer. When Alfred Tennyson writes that the sea “crawls”, he does not mean it has legs. He wants you to see the sea far below, wrinkled and slow. These special uses of language are called **figures of speech**. In Grade 5 you met similes and metaphors; in this lesson you will add **personification** and **hyperbole**.

> He clasps the crag with crooked hands;\
> Close to the sun in lonely lands,\
> Ring’d with the azure world, he stands.
>
> The wrinkled sea beneath him crawls;\
> He watches from his mountain walls,\
> And like a thunderbolt he falls.
>
> — Alfred Tennyson, “The Eagle” (1851)

## Four figures of speech

| Figure of speech | What it does | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Simile** | compares two different things using *like* or *as* | *And like a thunderbolt he falls.* |
| **Metaphor** | says one thing *is* another | *My little brother is a whirlwind in the kitchen.* |
| **Personification** | gives human qualities or actions to something that is not human | *The wrinkled sea beneath him crawls.* |
| **Hyperbole** | exaggerates hugely for effect | *This backpack weighs a ton.* |

In “The Eagle”, Tennyson uses all of these ideas. The eagle has “hands” (personification), the sea “crawls” (personification), and the eagle falls “like a thunderbolt” (simile).

:::example Naming a figure of speech
> The old car coughed and groaned up the hill.

@step Look for *like* or *as*: there are none, so it is not a simile.
@step Cars cannot really cough or groan. Those are things people do.
@answer Personification.
:::

:::mistake Hyperbole is not lying
Hyperbole is an exaggeration so big that no one is meant to believe it. “I’ve told you a million times” is not a lie; everyone understands it means “many times”, said with feeling.
:::

## What figures of speech add

A figure of speech packs a lot of meaning into a few words. To explain one, ask what the two things have in common, and what feeling the comparison creates.

Carl Sandburg’s whole poem “Fog” compares the fog to a cat:

> The fog comes\
> on little cat feet.
>
> It sits looking\
> over harbor and city\
> on silent haunches\
> and then moves on.
>
> — Carl Sandburg, “Fog” (1916)

:::example Explaining the effect
What does the comparison of the fog to a cat suggest?
@step Think about how cats move: softly, quietly, on padded feet. They sit very still, then wander off.
@step The fog must arrive silently, settle over the city, and drift away.
@answer The fog comes quietly and gently, stays a while, and leaves without a sound.

:::summary
- A **simile** uses *like* or *as*; a **metaphor** says one thing *is* another.
- **Personification** gives human actions or qualities to non-human things.
- **Hyperbole** is a huge exaggeration for effect.
- To explain a figure of speech, ask what is shared and what feeling it creates.
:::
