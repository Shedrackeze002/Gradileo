When you finish a good story, it often leaves you thinking about something bigger than the plot: about friendship, honesty or growing up. That bigger idea is the story’s **theme**. In this lesson you will learn to state a theme clearly and to back it up with evidence from the story.

## Topic and theme

:::definition Theme
A **theme** is a message about life or people that a story shows. It is written as a **full sentence**.

A **topic** is just the subject, in a word or phrase: *friendship*, *courage*, *honesty*.
:::

| Topic | Theme |
|---|---|
| Friendship | True friends help each other in hard times. |
| Courage | Being brave means acting even when you are afraid. |
| Honesty | Telling the truth is worth the risk of trouble. |

:::example Finding the theme of a fable
> The Hare laughed at the slow Tortoise and agreed to race him. Far ahead, the Hare lay down for a nap. When he woke, the Tortoise, who had never stopped, was crossing the finish line.
>
> — Aesop, “The Tortoise and the Hare”, retold

@step What happens? The fast but careless Hare loses; the slow but steady Tortoise wins.
@step What does that show about life in general, not just about these animals?
@answer Slow and steady effort can beat speed.
:::

:::mistake A theme is not a summary
“A tortoise beats a hare in a race” retells the plot. A theme steps back from the characters and says something true about people in general.
:::

## Evidence for a theme

A theme must be supported by **evidence**: events, actions or words from the story that show the message in action. Good evidence answers the question *how does the story show this?*

To find evidence, look at:
- what the main character **learns** or how they **change**;
- the **climax** and the **ending**, where the message is often clearest;
- what characters **say** about the events.

:::example Choosing evidence
> Mia stared at the broken cookie jar. She could blame the cat. Instead, she went to find her mother. “I broke it,” she said. Her mother hugged her. “Thank you for telling me the truth.”

Theme: *It is brave to admit your mistakes.* Which part of the passage best supports it?
@step “Mia stared at the broken cookie jar” only sets up the problem.
@step Going to her mother and saying “I broke it” is the brave act the theme describes.
@answer “Instead, she went to find her mother. ‘I broke it,’ she said.”

:::summary
- A **topic** is a word or phrase; a **theme** is a full sentence about life.
- A theme is not a summary of the plot.
- Support a theme with **evidence**: what characters learn, the climax, the ending and what characters say.
:::
