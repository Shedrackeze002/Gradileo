Much of what we read is trying to convince us of something: an article arguing for a new park, a letter asking for a longer lunch, a website saying a product is the best. A careful reader finds the author’s **claim** and then asks whether the **evidence** really supports it.

## Claims and reasons

:::definition Claim, reason and evidence
- A **claim** is the main point an author argues for. It is something reasonable people could disagree with: *Our school should have a longer lunch break.*
- A **reason** explains why the claim is right: *Twenty minutes is not enough time to eat and exercise.*
- **Evidence** backs up a reason with facts, statistics, examples or the words of experts.
:::

| Statement | Claim or fact? |
|---|---|
| Our town should build a skate park. | Claim: people could disagree. |
| Our town has two parks. | Fact: it can be checked. |
| Every town should ban plastic bags. | Claim |
| Some towns have banned plastic bags. | Fact |

:::example Finding the claim
> Children should put screens away an hour before bed. Surveys show many children sleep less than nine hours, and the light from screens can make it harder to fall asleep. Sleep helps the brain store memories, so it matters for learning.

@step The survey, the light from screens and the brain are all facts that support something.
@step What are they supporting? That screens should be put away before bed.
@answer The claim is “Children should put screens away an hour before bed.”
:::

## Judging evidence

Strong evidence is **relevant** (it connects to the claim), **reliable** (it comes from a trustworthy source) and **sufficient** (there is enough of it).

| Kind of evidence | Example | How strong? |
|---|---|---|
| **Statistic** | *A survey of 500 pupils found 7 in 10 want a longer lunch.* | Strong if the survey was fair |
| **Expert** | *A children’s doctor says exercise helps concentration.* | Strong: the expert knows the subject |
| **Study or research** | *Scientists found plastic in the stomachs of many turtles.* | Strong |
| **Anecdote** | *My friend says she loves running at break.* | Weak: one person’s experience |

:::mistake "Everyone I know" is not everyone
An author who writes “Everyone I know loves skating, so the town needs a skate park” is using an anecdote from a small group. It cannot be checked and may not represent the whole town.
:::

:::summary
- A **claim** is an arguable position; **reasons** say why; **evidence** backs up the reasons.
- Strong evidence is relevant, reliable and sufficient: statistics, experts and studies.
- Anecdotes from a few people are weak evidence.
:::
