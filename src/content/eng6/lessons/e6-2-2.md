Poetry is meant to be heard. Poets choose words for their **sounds** as well as their meanings, so that a line can hiss, buzz, rush or slow down. In this lesson you will learn the main **sound devices** and the power of **repetition**.

## Sound devices

:::definition Sound device
A **sound device** is a way of choosing and arranging words for how they **sound** when read aloud, not only for what they mean. Sound devices make a line musical, help it stick in the memory and can even imitate the thing being described.
:::

| Device | What it is | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Alliteration** | the same beginning sound in nearby words | *He **c**lasps the **c**rag with **c**rooked hands* (Tennyson) |
| **Assonance** | the same vowel sound inside nearby words | *Each sw**ee**t gr**ee**n l**ea**f* |
| **Onomatopoeia** | a word that sounds like what it names | *buzz, crackle, hiss, splash* |
| **Rhyme** | words whose endings sound the same | *witches / ditches* |

Robert Louis Stevenson’s poem about a speeding train is full of sound:

> Faster than fairies, faster than witches,\
> Bridges and houses, hedges and ditches;\
> And charging along like troops in a battle,\
> All through the meadows the horses and cattle:
>
> — Robert Louis Stevenson, “From a Railway Carriage” (1885), first lines

:::example Hearing the devices
Which sound device is in “Faster than fairies, faster than witches”?
@step Say it aloud and listen to the starts of the words: **f**aster, **f**airies, **f**aster.
@step The same beginning sound repeats.
@answer Alliteration.
:::

:::mistake Sounds, not letters
Alliteration and assonance are about **sounds**. *Cat* and *city* both start with c, but they do not alliterate, because *city* begins with an s sound. *Kite* and *cat* do alliterate.
:::

## Repetition and refrain

When a poet repeats a word, phrase or line, the repetition makes it stand out and can create a rhythm or a feeling. A line or group of lines that repeats regularly, like the chorus of a song, is called a **refrain**.

:::example The effect of repetition
> We waited and we waited,\
> As the cold rain came down,\
> We waited for the last bus\
> To carry us to town.
>
> — written for Gradileo

What is the effect of repeating “waited”?
@step The word comes three times in three lines.
@step Saying it again and again makes the reader feel how long the wait is.
@answer It stresses how long and tiring the wait was.
:::

In Stevenson’s poem, the repeated pattern “*faster than … faster than*” and the pairs “*Bridges and houses, hedges and ditches*” give the lines a quick, steady beat, like a train’s wheels clattering on the rails.

:::summary
- **Alliteration** repeats beginning sounds; **assonance** repeats vowel sounds inside words.
- **Onomatopoeia** sounds like what it names.
- **Repetition** makes words stand out and builds rhythm or feeling; a **refrain** repeats like a chorus.
:::
